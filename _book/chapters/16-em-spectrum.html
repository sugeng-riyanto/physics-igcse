19.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content:

  • Know the main regions of the electromagnetic spectrum in order of frequency and in order of wavelength

  • Know that all electromagnetic waves travel at the same high speed in a vacuum

  • Describe typical uses of the different regions of the electromagnetic spectrum including:

    • Radio waves: radio and television transmissions, astronomy, radio frequency identification (RFID)

    • Microwaves: satellite television, mobile phones (cell phones), microwave ovens

    • Infrared: electric grills, short range communications such as remote controllers for televisions, intruder alarms, thermal imaging, optical fibres

    • Visible light: vision, photography, illumination

    • Ultraviolet: security marking, detecting fake bank notes, sterilising water

    • X-rays: medical scanning, security scanners

    • Gamma rays: sterilising food and medical equipment, detection of cancer and its treatment

  • Describe the harmful effects on people of excessive exposure to electromagnetic radiation, including:

    • Microwaves: internal heating of body cells

    • Infrared: skin burns

    • Ultraviolet: damage to surface cells and eyes, leading to skin cancer and eye conditions

    • X-rays and gamma rays: mutation or damage to cells in the body

  • Know that communication with artificial satellites is mainly by microwaves:

    • Some satellite phones use low orbit artificial satellites

    • Some satellite phones and direct broadcast satellite television use geostationary satellites

Supplement (Extended):

  • Know that the speed of electromagnetic waves in a vacuum is 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s and is approximately the same in air

  • Know that many important systems of communications rely on electromagnetic radiation including:

    • Mobile phones and wireless internet use microwaves because microwaves can penetrate some walls and only require a short aerial for transmission and reception

    • Bluetooth uses radio waves because radio waves pass through walls but the signal is weakened on doing so

    • Optical fibres (visible light or infrared) are used for cable television and high-speed broadband because glass is transparent to visible light and some infrared; visible light and short wavelength infrared can carry high rates of data

  • Know the difference between a digital and analogue signal

  • Know that a sound can be transmitted as a digital or analogue signal

  • Explain the benefits of digital signalling including increased rate of transmission of data and increased range due to accurate signal regeneration

19.2 16.1 What Is the Electromagnetic Spectrum?

19.2.1 Discovery of Invisible Light

In 1800, astronomer Sir Frederick William Herschel conducted an experiment using a prism to split sunlight into a spectrum. He placed thermometers in different parts of the visible spectrum and beyond.

Observation:

  • The thermometer placed just beyond the red light showed the highest temperature reading

  • This indicated the presence of invisible radiation beyond visible red light

Conclusion:

  • The electromagnetic spectrum contains more than just visible light

  • Invisible regions exist that can only be detected by instruments

19.2.2 The Electromagnetic Spectrum

The electromagnetic spectrum consists of all types of electromagnetic waves arranged in order of frequency or wavelength.

Key properties:

  • All electromagnetic waves are transverse waves

  • All electromagnetic waves can travel through a vacuum

  • All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum: \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\)

  • Frequency (\(f\)) and wavelength (\(\lambda\)) are related by: \(c = f\lambda\)

Note

Key Concept: Since \(c\) is constant, frequency and wavelength are inversely proportional: higher frequency means shorter wavelength, and vice versa.

19.2.3 Main Regions of the Electromagnetic Spectrum

Region Approximate Wavelength Range Approximate Frequency Range
Radio waves > 10⁻¹ m < 3 × 10⁹ Hz
Microwaves 10⁻³ m to 10⁻¹ m 3 × 10⁹ Hz to 3 × 10¹¹ Hz
Infrared 7 × 10⁻⁷ m to 10⁻³ m 3 × 10¹¹ Hz to 4 × 10¹⁴ Hz
Visible light 4 × 10⁻⁷ m to 7 × 10⁻⁷ m 4 × 10¹⁴ Hz to 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz
Ultraviolet 10⁻⁸ m to 4 × 10⁻⁷ m 7.5 × 10¹⁴ Hz to 3 × 10¹⁶ Hz
X-rays 10⁻¹¹ m to 10⁻⁸ m 3 × 10¹⁶ Hz to 3 × 10¹⁹ Hz
Gamma rays < 10⁻¹¹ m > 3 × 10¹⁹ Hz

Order of increasing frequency (decreasing wavelength):

  • Radio waves → Microwaves → Infrared → Visible light → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma rays

Memory aid: “Rugby Match Is Very Unusual, Xciting Games”

  • Radio → Microwave → Infrared → Visible → Ultraviolet → X-rays → Gamma

19.3 16.2 Uses of Electromagnetic Radiation

19.3.1 Radio Waves

Typical uses:

  • Radio and television transmissions: Tall broadcast towers send radio waves carrying sound and image data to receivers

  • Astronomy: Large radio telescopes detect radio waves from stars, galaxies, and other astronomical objects

  • Radio Frequency Identification (RFID): Radio waves transfer data for tracking items with RFID tags

Why radio waves are suitable:

  • Long wavelengths (up to 1 km) allow diffraction around obstacles like buildings and hills

  • Can pass through walls, enabling indoor reception

  • Low energy means they are generally safe for continuous exposure

19.3.2 Microwaves

Typical uses:

  • Satellite television: Microwaves carry image and sound data from satellites to ground-based dishes

  • Mobile phones (cell phones): Convert sound to microwave signals for wireless communication

  • Microwave ovens: Cause water molecules in food to vibrate, generating heat for cooking

  • Wireless internet (Wi-Fi): Transmit data between devices and routers

Why microwaves are suitable:

  • Can penetrate clouds and some building materials

  • Short wavelengths allow compact aerials for transmission and reception

  • High frequencies enable high data transmission rates

19.3.3 Infrared Radiation

Typical uses:

  • Electric grills and heaters: Convert electrical energy to infrared for heating and cooking

  • Remote controllers: Simple LEDs emit infrared pulses to control televisions and other devices

  • Intruder alarms: Infrared transmitters and detectors sense when a beam is blocked

  • Thermal imaging: Detectors convert infrared to electrical signals, producing thermal images showing temperature variations

  • Optical fibres: Some infrared wavelengths pass through glass with minimal signal loss, enabling long-distance data transmission

Why infrared is suitable:

  • Easily generated and detected using simple electronic components

  • Causes heating effects useful for cooking and warmth

  • Short-range communication minimises interference

19.3.4 Visible Light

Typical uses:

  • Vision: Human eyes detect visible light, enabling us to see our surroundings

  • Photography: Sensors sensitive to visible light capture images in cameras

  • Illumination: Light sources provide visibility for work, safety, and aesthetics

  • Optical fibres: Visible light undergoes total internal reflection for data transmission

Why visible light is suitable:

  • Detected by human eyes without special equipment

  • Can be focused and manipulated using lenses and mirrors

  • High frequencies enable high data rates in optical communication

19.3.5 Ultraviolet Radiation

Typical uses:

  • Security marking: Chemicals that glow under UV light create invisible markings for authentication

  • Detecting fake banknotes: UV lamps reveal security features invisible under normal light

  • Sterilising water: UV radiation damages microorganisms, making water safe to drink

Why ultraviolet is suitable:

  • High energy can break chemical bonds in microorganisms

  • Causes fluorescence in certain materials for detection purposes

  • Can be generated using specialised lamps

19.3.6 X-rays

Typical uses:

  • Medical scanning: X-rays penetrate soft tissues but are absorbed by bones, producing images for diagnosis

  • Security scanners: Airport baggage scanners use X-rays to detect hidden weapons or contraband

Why X-rays are suitable:

  • High penetrating power allows imaging of internal structures

  • Different materials absorb X-rays to different degrees, creating contrast in images

19.3.7 Gamma Rays

Typical uses:

  • Sterilising food and medical equipment: Gamma rays kill bacteria and other microorganisms without heating

  • Detection and treatment of cancer: Gamma rays can destroy cancer cells; radioactive tracers emitting gamma rays help locate tumours

Why gamma rays are suitable:

  • Extremely high penetrating power reaches deep into materials and tissues

  • High energy can kill cells and break chemical bonds

19.4 16.3 Harmful Effects of Electromagnetic Radiation

19.4.1 General Principle

The harmful effects of electromagnetic radiation depend on:

  • Frequency/energy: Higher frequency radiation carries more energy per photon

  • Intensity: Greater intensity means more radiation energy absorbed

  • Exposure time: Longer exposure increases total energy absorbed

  • Type of tissue: Different body tissues respond differently to radiation

19.4.2 Specific Harmful Effects

Radiation Type Harmful Effects of Excessive Exposure
Microwaves Internal heating of body cells; can cause tissue damage if intensity is very high
Infrared Skin burns; excessive exposure to intense infrared sources can damage skin
Ultraviolet Damage to surface cells and eyes; can cause skin cancer, premature skin ageing, cataracts, and macular degeneration
X-rays Mutation or damage to cells; excessive exposure increases cancer risk; can harm developing foetuses
Gamma rays Mutation or damage to cells; high doses can cause radiation sickness; increases long-term cancer risk
Warning

Important: Normal, controlled use of electromagnetic radiation (e.g., medical X-rays, UV sterilisation) is safe. Harm occurs only with excessive or uncontrolled exposure.

19.4.3 Safety Precautions

  • Microwaves: Maintain safe distance from operating microwave ovens; ensure door seals are intact

  • Infrared: Use protective eyewear when working with intense infrared sources; avoid prolonged exposure to heat lamps

  • Ultraviolet: Wear sunscreen and protective clothing outdoors; avoid staring at UV lamps; use UV-blocking sunglasses

  • X-rays and gamma rays: Use lead shielding; minimise exposure time; maintain distance from sources; only trained personnel should operate equipment

19.5 16.4 Electromagnetic Waves in Communication

19.5.1 Speed of Electromagnetic Waves

All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum:

\[c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\]

In air, the speed is approximately the same (slightly less, but the difference is negligible for most purposes).

Worked Example: Signal Travel Time

Question: How long does it take a microwave signal to travel from Earth to a geostationary satellite 36,000 km away and back?

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate total distance \[\text{Total distance} = 36,000 \text{ km} + 36,000 \text{ km} = 72,000 \text{ km} = 7.2 \times 10^7 \text{ m}\]

Step 2: Apply speed formula \[t = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{speed}} = \frac{7.2 \times 10^7}{3.0 \times 10^8} = \boxed{0.24 \text{ s}}\]

Interpretation: Communication via geostationary satellites has a noticeable delay of about 0.24 seconds for a round trip.

19.5.2 Satellite Communication

Types of artificial satellites:

Type Altitude Characteristics Uses
Geostationary satellites ~36,000 km above equator Orbit period = 24 hours; appear stationary relative to Earth Direct broadcast TV, some satellite phones, weather monitoring
Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellites ~500–2,000 km above Earth Orbit period ~90 minutes; move rapidly across sky Some satellite phones, Earth observation, internet constellations

Why microwaves for satellite communication?

  • Can penetrate clouds and atmosphere with minimal attenuation

  • Short wavelengths allow compact, directional aerials on satellites and ground stations

  • High frequencies support high data transmission rates for TV and internet

19.5.3 Wireless Communication Technologies

19.5.3.1 Mobile Phones and Wireless Internet

  • Use microwaves (typically 800 MHz to 2.6 GHz)

  • Microwaves can penetrate some walls, enabling indoor use

  • Short wavelengths allow compact aerials in mobile devices

  • High frequencies support voice, text, and data transmission

19.5.3.2 Bluetooth

  • Uses radio waves (2.4 GHz band)

  • Radio waves pass through walls but are weakened, limiting range to ~10 m

  • Suitable for short-range device-to-device communication (e.g., phone to speaker)

19.5.3.3 Optical Fibres

  • Use visible light or short-wavelength infrared (650–1600 nm)

  • Glass is transparent to these wavelengths; light undergoes total internal reflection

  • Very high frequencies enable extremely high data rates (terabits per second)

  • Minimal signal loss over long distances; ideal for cable TV and broadband internet

Note

Key Insight: The choice of electromagnetic region for a communication technology depends on wavelength, penetration ability, data capacity, and practical considerations like aerial size.

19.6 16.5 Analogue and Digital Signals

19.6.1 What Is a Signal?

A signal is a varying quantity that carries information. In communication systems, signals represent sound, images, or data.

19.6.2 Analogue Signals

Characteristics:

  • Have continuous values that vary smoothly over time

  • Information is encoded in variations of amplitude, frequency, or phase

  • Examples: traditional radio broadcasts, vinyl records, analogue telephone lines

Graphical representation:

Value
  ^
  |    /\/\    /\
  |   /    \  /  \
  |  /      \/    \
  +------------------> Time

19.6.3 Digital Signals

Characteristics:

  • Have discrete values, typically two states: ‘on’ (1) and ‘off’ (0)

  • Information is encoded as sequences of binary digits (bits)

  • Examples: digital TV, mobile phone calls, internet data, CDs

Graphical representation:

Value
  ^
1 |  ┌─┐  ┌───┐  ┌─
  |  │ │  │   │  │
0 |──┘ └──┘   └──┘──> Time

19.6.4 Transmitting Sound as Signals

Sound can be transmitted using either analogue or digital signals:

Analogue transmission:

  1. Microphone converts sound waves to continuous electrical signals

  2. Signals modulate a carrier wave (e.g., radio wave)

  3. Receiver demodulates and converts back to sound

Digital transmission:

  1. Microphone converts sound to analogue electrical signals

  2. Analogue-to-digital converter (ADC) samples and encodes signals as binary data

  3. Binary data modulates a carrier wave for transmission

  4. Receiver demodulates and digital-to-analogue converter (DAC) reconstructs sound

19.7 16.6 Benefits of Digital Signalling

19.7.1 Increased Data Transmission Rate

  • Digital signals can be compressed using mathematical algorithms

  • Compression reduces the number of bits needed to represent the same information

  • More data can be transmitted in the same time → higher data rates

  • Enables high-quality video streaming, fast internet downloads

19.7.2 Accurate Signal Regeneration

Problem with analogue signals:

  • Noise (unwanted variations) accumulates during transmission

  • Amplifiers boost both signal and noise → degraded quality over long distances

Advantage of digital signals:

  • Only two values (1 and 0) are expected

  • If noise is not too large, the original signal can be regenerated accurately

  • Repeaters along the transmission path recover and retransmit clean digital signals

  • Enables high-quality communication over very long distances

19.7.3 Other Benefits

  • Error detection and correction: Digital systems can detect and correct transmission errors

  • Encryption: Digital data can be encrypted for secure communication

  • Multiplexing: Multiple digital signals can be combined and transmitted simultaneously

  • Storage and processing: Digital signals are easily stored, copied, and processed by computers

19.8 Key Concepts Summary

19.8.1 Core Concepts

  1. Electromagnetic spectrum regions in order of increasing frequency: radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma

  2. All electromagnetic waves travel at \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\) in vacuum; speed approximately same in air

  3. Uses of each region: Radio (broadcasting, RFID), microwave (satellites, phones, ovens), infrared (heating, remote controls, thermal imaging), visible (vision, photography), UV (security, sterilisation), X-ray (medical imaging, security), gamma (sterilisation, cancer treatment)

  4. Harmful effects: Microwaves (internal heating), infrared (skin burns), UV (skin/eye damage, cancer), X-rays/gamma (cell damage, mutation, cancer)

  5. Satellite communication: Mainly uses microwaves; geostationary (~36,000 km) and LEO (~500–2,000 km) satellites serve different purposes

19.8.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Communication technologies: Mobile phones/Wi-Fi use microwaves (penetrate walls, short aerials); Bluetooth uses radio waves (pass through walls but weaken); optical fibres use visible/short-wavelength infrared (high data rates, low loss)

  2. Analogue vs digital signals: Analogue = continuous values; digital = discrete values (1/0); sound can be transmitted either way

  3. Benefits of digital signalling: Higher data rates via compression; accurate regeneration over long distances; error correction; encryption; easy storage/processing

19.9 Common Misconceptions

19.9.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Different electromagnetic waves travel at different speeds”

Incorrect: “Radio waves travel slower than light”

Correct:

  • All electromagnetic waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum: \(3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\)

  • Differences in wavelength and frequency do not affect speed in vacuum

  • In materials (e.g., glass), different frequencies may travel at slightly different speeds (dispersion), but this is not the case in vacuum/air

19.9.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Higher frequency means more dangerous radiation”

Incorrect: “Gamma rays are always dangerous; radio waves are always safe”

Correct:

  • Danger depends on intensity, exposure time, and frequency, not frequency alone

  • Low-intensity gamma rays (e.g., medical tracers) can be safe; high-intensity radio waves can cause heating

  • Normal, controlled use of any electromagnetic radiation is generally safe

19.9.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Microwaves cook food by making it radioactive”

Incorrect: “Microwave ovens make food radioactive”

Correct:

  • Microwaves cause water molecules to vibrate, generating heat through friction

  • This is a non-ionising process; it does not change the chemical structure of food or make it radioactive

  • Food heated in a microwave is no different from food heated by other methods

19.9.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Digital signals are always better than analogue”

Incorrect: “Analogue signals are obsolete; digital is always superior”

Correct:

  • Digital signals have advantages for long-distance transmission, storage, and processing

  • Analogue signals can be simpler and adequate for short-range, low-complexity applications

  • Choice depends on application requirements, cost, and available technology

19.9.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Optical fibres use any type of light”

Incorrect: “Any light can travel through optical fibres”

Correct:

  • Optical fibres are transparent only to visible light and short-wavelength infrared

  • Other wavelengths (e.g., long-wavelength infrared, ultraviolet) are absorbed or scattered

  • The specific wavelengths used (650–1600 nm) are chosen for minimal loss in glass

19.9.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “All ultraviolet radiation comes from the Sun”

Incorrect: “UV radiation only comes from sunlight”

Correct:

  • The Sun is a major natural source of UV radiation

  • Artificial sources include UV lamps for sterilisation, tanning beds, and certain types of lighting

  • Some electrical equipment (e.g., welding arcs) also emits UV radiation

19.9.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “X-rays and gamma rays are the same thing”

Incorrect: “X-rays and gamma rays are identical; the names are interchangeable”

Correct:

  • Both are high-frequency, high-energy electromagnetic radiation

  • Origin differs: X-rays are produced by electron transitions; gamma rays are produced by nuclear processes

  • Energy ranges overlap, but gamma rays typically have higher frequencies

  • Applications and safety precautions may differ based on source and energy

19.10 Worked Examples

19.10.1 Example 1: Ordering Electromagnetic Waves

Question: Arrange the following electromagnetic waves in order of increasing wavelength: visible light, microwaves, gamma rays, infrared, radio waves.

Solution:

Step 1: Recall order of increasing frequency: gamma → X-ray → UV → visible → infrared → microwave → radio

Step 2: Since wavelength is inversely proportional to frequency, reverse the order for increasing wavelength:

\[\text{gamma rays} < \text{visible light} < \text{infrared} < \text{microwaves} < \text{radio waves}\]

Answer: Gamma rays, visible light, infrared, microwaves, radio waves

19.10.2 Example 2: Calculating Wavelength

Question: A mobile phone uses microwaves with a frequency of 1.8 GHz. Calculate the wavelength of these microwaves. (Take \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\))

Solution:

Step 1: Convert frequency to Hz \[f = 1.8 \text{ GHz} = 1.8 \times 10^9 \text{ Hz}\]

Step 2: Apply wave equation \[c = f\lambda \quad \Rightarrow \quad \lambda = \frac{c}{f} = \frac{3.0 \times 10^8}{1.8 \times 10^9} = \boxed{0.17 \text{ m} = 17 \text{ cm}}\]

Interpretation: The wavelength is about 17 cm, which is why mobile phone aerials can be compact.

19.10.3 Example 3: Signal Travel Time to Satellite

Question: A geostationary satellite is 36,000 km above the Earth. Calculate the minimum time delay for a signal to travel from Earth to the satellite and back.

Solution:

Step 1: Total distance for round trip \[d = 2 \times 36,000 \text{ km} = 72,000 \text{ km} = 7.2 \times 10^7 \text{ m}\]

Step 2: Time calculation \[t = \frac{d}{c} = \frac{7.2 \times 10^7}{3.0 \times 10^8} = \boxed{0.24 \text{ s}}\]

Interpretation: This 0.24 s delay is noticeable in satellite phone conversations and live satellite TV broadcasts.

19.10.4 Example 4: Digital vs Analogue Signal Quality

Question: Explain why a digital TV signal received over a long distance via satellite maintains better quality than an analogue TV signal transmitted over the same distance.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify key difference: signal regeneration

Step 2: Analogue signal behaviour: - Noise accumulates during transmission - Amplifiers boost both signal and noise - Quality degrades progressively with distance

Step 3: Digital signal behaviour: - Only two values (1/0) are expected - Repeaters detect and regenerate clean digital signals - Noise is eliminated at each regeneration point

Conclusion: Digital signals can be accurately regenerated at repeaters, maintaining quality over long distances; analogue signals accumulate noise that cannot be removed.

19.12 Laboratory Investigation

19.12.1 Experiment 16.1: Investigating Infrared Radiation

Objective: To demonstrate the detection of infrared radiation and its heating effect.

Apparatus:

  • Infrared lamp or heat source

  • Thermometer or temperature sensor with data logger

  • Black and white/silver surfaces (e.g., painted metal plates)

  • Retort stand and clamp

  • Stopwatch

Variables:

  • Independent: Surface colour/texture (black vs. white/silver)

  • Dependent: Temperature change over time

  • Controlled: Distance from heat source, initial temperature, exposure time

Procedure:

  1. Set up the infrared lamp at a fixed distance from the test surfaces.

  2. Place the black surface and the white/silver surface at equal distances from the lamp.

  3. Attach a thermometer or temperature sensor to each surface.

  4. Record the initial temperature of each surface.

  5. Switch on the infrared lamp and start the stopwatch.

  6. Record the temperature of each surface every 30 seconds for 5 minutes.

  7. Switch off the lamp and allow surfaces to cool.

  8. Repeat the experiment with the surfaces swapped to check for consistency.

Data Table:

Time (s) Temperature of black surface (°C) Temperature of white/silver surface (°C)

Analysis:

  1. Plot a graph of temperature (y-axis) against time (x-axis) for both surfaces on the same axes.

  2. Compare the rates of temperature increase for the two surfaces.

  3. Explain the difference in terms of absorption of infrared radiation.

Precautions:

  • ✓ Keep the distance from the lamp to each surface constant

  • ✓ Avoid touching the hot surfaces; use tongs if necessary

  • ✓ Ensure the thermometer bulbs are in good thermal contact with the surfaces

  • ✓ Allow surfaces to return to room temperature between trials

Expected Results:

  • The black surface will heat up faster than the white/silver surface

  • This demonstrates that dark, dull surfaces absorb infrared radiation more effectively than light, shiny surfaces

Conclusion Template:

“The experiment demonstrated that black, dull surfaces absorb infrared radiation more effectively than white, shiny surfaces, as shown by the faster temperature rise of the black surface. This supports the principle that surface colour and texture affect the absorption of infrared radiation.”

Extension (Supplement):

  • Investigate the emission of infrared radiation by comparing cooling rates of hot black and white surfaces

  • Research applications: solar water heaters (blackened surfaces), space blankets (shiny surfaces)

19.14 Chapter Summary

19.14.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Electromagnetic spectrum order (increasing frequency): radio → microwave → infrared → visible → ultraviolet → X-ray → gamma
✓ All EM waves travel at \(c = 3.0 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s}\) in vacuum; approximately same in air
✓ Uses: Radio (broadcasting, RFID), microwave (satellites, phones, ovens), infrared (heating, remote controls, thermal imaging), visible (vision, photography), UV (security, sterilisation), X-ray (medical imaging, security), gamma (sterilisation, cancer treatment)
✓ Harmful effects: Microwaves (internal heating), infrared (skin burns), UV (skin/eye damage, cancer), X-rays/gamma (cell damage, mutation, cancer)
✓ Satellite communication: Uses microwaves; geostationary (~36,000 km) for TV/phones; LEO (~500–2,000 km) for phones/observation
✓ Wireless tech: Mobile/Wi-Fi use microwaves (penetrate walls, short aerials); Bluetooth uses radio waves (pass through walls but weaken); optical fibres use visible/short IR (high data rates, low loss)
✓ Analogue vs digital: Analogue = continuous values; digital = discrete (1/0); digital enables compression, regeneration, error correction
✓ Digital benefits: Higher data rates, accurate regeneration over distance, encryption, easy storage/processing

19.16 Glossary

Term Definition
Analogue signal A signal with continuous values that vary smoothly over time; information encoded in amplitude, frequency, or phase variations
Digital signal A signal with discrete values, typically two states (‘on’/1 and ‘off’/0); information encoded as binary data
Electromagnetic spectrum The complete range of electromagnetic waves arranged in order of frequency or wavelength
Electromagnetic wave A transverse wave consisting of oscillating electric and magnetic fields; can travel through vacuum at speed \(c\)
Frequency (\(f\)) Number of complete waves passing a point per second; unit: hertz (Hz)
Geostationary satellite A satellite orbiting ~36,000 km above the equator with an orbital period of 24 hours, appearing stationary relative to Earth
Infrared radiation Electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths longer than visible red light; associated with heating effects
Low Earth Orbit (LEO) satellite A satellite orbiting ~500–2,000 km above Earth with an orbital period of ~90 minutes
Microwave Electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths between radio waves and infrared; used in communication and cooking
Optical fibre A thin glass or plastic fibre that transmits light by total internal reflection; used for high-speed data transmission
Total internal reflection The complete reflection of light within a denser medium when the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle; principle behind optical fibres
Ultraviolet radiation Electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths shorter than visible violet light; can cause fluorescence and cell damage
Wavelength (\(\lambda\)) Shortest distance between two consecutive points in phase on a wave; unit: metre (m)
