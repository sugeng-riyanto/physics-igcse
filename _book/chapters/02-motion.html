5.1 Learning Objectives

5.1.1 Core Content

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Define speed as distance travelled per unit time; recall and use the equation \(v = \frac{s}{t}\)

  • Define velocity as speed in a given direction

  • Recall and use the equation average speed = \(\frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time taken}}\)

  • Sketch, plot and interpret distance–time and speed–time graphs

  • Determine, qualitatively, from given data or the shape of a distance–time graph or speed–time graph when an object is: (a) at rest, (b) moving with constant speed, (c) accelerating, (d) decelerating

  • Calculate speed from the gradient of a straight-line section of a distance–time graph

  • Calculate the area under a speed–time graph to determine the distance travelled for motion with constant speed or constant acceleration

  • State that the acceleration of free fall \(g\) for an object near to the surface of the Earth is approximately constant and is approximately \(9.8\ \text{m/s}^{2}\)

5.1.2 Supplement (Extended)

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

  • Define acceleration as change in velocity per unit time; recall and use the equation \(a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\)

  • Determine from given data or the shape of a speed–time graph when an object is moving with: (a) constant acceleration, (b) changing acceleration

  • Calculate acceleration from the gradient of a speed–time graph

  • Know that a deceleration is a negative acceleration and use this in calculations

  • Describe the motion of objects falling in a uniform gravitational field with and without air/liquid resistance, including reference to terminal velocity

5.2 Key Concepts

5.2.1 Speed and Velocity

  • Speed is a scalar quantity that describes how fast an object moves, regardless of direction.

    • Definition: Speed = \(\frac{\text{distance travelled}}{\text{time taken}}\)

    • Equation: \(v = \frac{s}{t}\)

    • SI unit: metre per second (m/s)

    • Example: A car travelling 100 m in 10 s has a speed of \(v = \frac{100\ \text{m}}{10\ \text{s}} = 10\ \text{m/s}\)

  • Average speed is used when an object’s speed varies during its journey.

    • Equation: \(\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance travelled}}{\text{total time taken}}\)

  • Velocity is a vector quantity that describes speed in a specified direction.

    • Definition: Velocity = \(\frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time taken}}\)

    • SI unit: metre per second (m/s), with direction stated

  • Key distinction: Speed is scalar (magnitude only); velocity is vector (magnitude + direction).

5.2.2 Distance and Displacement

  • Distance is a scalar quantity representing the total path length travelled by an object.

    • Always positive or zero

  • Displacement is a vector quantity representing the shortest straight-line distance from start to finish, with direction.

    • Can be positive, negative, or zero depending on reference direction

  • Relationship: Displacement magnitude \(\leq\) Distance travelled

5.2.3 Acceleration

  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity with time; it is a vector quantity.

    • Definition: Acceleration = \(\frac{\text{change in velocity}}{\text{time taken}}\)

    • Equation: \(a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v - u}{t}\)

    • SI unit: metre per second squared (m/s\(^{2}\))

  • Deceleration (negative acceleration): Velocity decreases with time.

    • Represented by a negative value of acceleration when direction of motion is taken as positive

5.2.4 Distance–Time Graphs

  • Gradient of distance–time graph = speed of the object.

  • Interpreting sections of a distance–time graph:

Graph Shape Gradient Motion Description
Horizontal line Zero Object at rest
Straight line, constant positive gradient Constant Moving with constant speed away from start
Curve, gradient increasing Increasing Accelerating (speeding up)
Curve, gradient decreasing Decreasing Decelerating (slowing down)

5.2.5 Speed–Time Graphs

  • Gradient of speed–time graph = acceleration of the object.

  • Area under speed–time graph = distance travelled.

  • Interpreting sections of a speed–time graph:

Graph Shape Gradient Motion Description
Horizontal line at \(v = 0\) Zero Object at rest
Horizontal line at \(v > 0\) Zero Moving with constant speed
Straight line, positive gradient Constant positive Constant acceleration
Straight line, negative gradient Constant negative Constant deceleration
Curve, gradient changing Changing Changing acceleration

5.2.6 Acceleration of Free Fall

  • Free fall: Motion of an object under the influence of gravity alone, with no other forces acting.

  • Acceleration due to gravity (\(g\)):

    • Near Earth’s surface, \(g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^{2}\) (often approximated to \(10\ \text{m/s}^{2}\) for calculations)

    • Direction: Vertically downwards towards the centre of the Earth

    • Independent of mass: All objects fall with the same acceleration in vacuum

  • Effect of air resistance:

    • Air resistance opposes motion and increases with speed and surface area

    • Initially: Object accelerates at \(g\) (air resistance negligible at low speed)

    • As speed increases: Air resistance increases, reducing resultant force and acceleration

    • Terminal velocity: When air resistance equals weight, resultant force = 0, acceleration = 0, object falls at constant maximum speed

5.2.7 Vector Addition for Perpendicular Quantities

  • Perpendicular vectors – Calculation method:

    • Magnitude: \(R = \sqrt{F_{1}^{2} + F_{2}^{2}}\)

    • Direction: \(\tan \theta = \frac{F_{2}}{F_{1}}\)

5.3 Common Misconceptions

Warning⚠️ Misconception 1: “Speed and velocity are the same thing”

Incorrect belief: Speed and velocity can be used interchangeably; both just tell how fast something moves.

Correct understanding:

  • Speed is scalar: magnitude only (e.g., 20 m/s)

  • Velocity is vector: magnitude + direction (e.g., 20 m/s due north)

  • An object moving in a circle at constant speed has changing velocity because direction changes

Warning⚠️ Misconception 2: “If speed is constant, acceleration must be zero”

Incorrect belief: Constant speed implies no acceleration under any circumstances.

Correct understanding:

  • Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, not speed

  • If direction changes while speed remains constant, velocity changes → acceleration exists

  • Example: Uniform circular motion has constant speed but continuous acceleration towards the centre

Warning⚠️ Misconception 3: “Heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones”

Incorrect belief: A heavier object will reach the ground before a lighter one when dropped from the same height.

Correct understanding:

  • In vacuum (no air resistance): All objects fall with identical acceleration \(g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^{2}\), regardless of mass

  • In air: Differences arise due to air resistance, not mass; a feather falls slower due to large surface area relative to mass

Warning⚠️ Misconception 4: “The gradient of a distance–time graph gives acceleration”

Incorrect belief: The steepness of a distance–time graph directly indicates acceleration.

Correct understanding:

  • Gradient of distance–time graph = speed

  • Gradient of speed–time graph = acceleration

  • Confusing these relationships leads to incorrect analysis of motion

Warning⚠️ Misconception 5: “Area under a distance–time graph has physical meaning”

Incorrect belief: The area beneath a distance–time graph represents a useful physical quantity.

Correct understanding:

  • Area under speed–time graph = distance travelled (physically meaningful)

  • Area under distance–time graph has no standard physical interpretation in kinematics

Warning⚠️ Misconception 6: “Deceleration is a different quantity from negative acceleration”

Incorrect belief: Deceleration is a separate concept with its own formula, distinct from acceleration.

Correct understanding:

  • Deceleration is simply negative acceleration when the direction of motion is defined as positive

  • Same equation applies: \(a = \frac{v - u}{t}\); if \(v < u\), then \(a\) is negative

Warning⚠️ Misconception 7: “Terminal velocity means the object stops falling”

Incorrect belief: When an object reaches terminal velocity, it ceases to move downwards.

Correct understanding:

  • Terminal velocity is the constant maximum speed reached when air resistance equals weight

  • At terminal velocity: resultant force = 0, acceleration = 0, but velocity is constant and non-zero

  • Object continues to fall at steady speed, not at rest

5.4 Essential Skills

5.4.1 Graphical Analysis of Motion

  • Sketching distance–time graphs:

    • Label axes with quantity and unit: distance/m, time/s

    • Use appropriate scale to utilise most of graph paper

    • Plot points accurately with small crosses (×)

    • Draw best-fit line/curve (do not join points dot-to-dot)

  • Sketching speed–time graphs:

    • Horizontal line at \(v = 0\): object at rest

    • Horizontal line at \(v > 0\): constant speed

    • Straight line with positive gradient: constant acceleration

    • Curve approaching horizontal: approaching terminal velocity

  • Extracting information from graphs:

    • Speed from distance–time graph: \(v = \text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta s}{\Delta t}\)

    • Acceleration from speed–time graph: \(a = \text{gradient} = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t}\)

    • Distance from speed–time graph: distance = area under graph

5.4.2 Calculation Techniques

  • Using kinematic equations (constant acceleration):

    • \(v = u + at\) (final velocity)

    • \(s = ut + \frac{1}{2}at^{2}\) (displacement)

    • \(v^{2} = u^{2} + 2as\) (velocity–displacement, time-independent)

    • \(s = \frac{(u + v)}{2} \times t\) (average velocity method)

  • Unit conversion and consistency:

    • Convert all quantities to SI units before calculation: km → m, h → s, km/h → m/s

    • Conversion: \(1\ \text{km/h} = \frac{1000\ \text{m}}{3600\ \text{s}} = \frac{1}{3.6}\ \text{m/s}\)

    • Example: \(72\ \text{km/h} = 72 \div 3.6 = 20\ \text{m/s}\)

  • Sign convention for vector quantities:

    • Choose a positive direction at start of problem (e.g., upwards = +ve)

    • Apply signs consistently to displacement, velocity, acceleration

    • Negative result indicates direction opposite to chosen positive direction

5.4.3 Experimental Skills for Motion Investigations

  • Measuring speed and acceleration:

    • Use light gates and data logger for precise timing of moving objects

    • Measure distance between gates accurately with metre rule

    • Calculate speed: \(v = \frac{\text{distance between gates}}{\text{time interval}}\)

  • Investigating free fall:

    • Use electromagnet to release object from rest (eliminates manual release error)

    • Measure fall time with electronic timer or video analysis

    • Plot \(s\) against \(t^{2}\); gradient = \(\frac{1}{2}g\) → determine \(g\)

  • Minimising errors in motion experiments:

    • Parallax error: view scales perpendicularly when measuring distance

    • Reaction time: use electronic timing or measure multiple oscillations/distances

    • Friction/air resistance: use smooth surfaces, streamlined objects, or vacuum where possible

5.5 Worked Calculations

5.5.1 Example 1: Average Speed Calculation

Question:
A cyclist travels 3.6 km in 12 minutes. Calculate her average speed in m/s.

Solution:

  • Step 1: Convert quantities to SI units

    • Distance: \(3.6\ \text{km} = 3.6 \times 1000 = 3600\ \text{m}\)

    • Time: \(12\ \text{min} = 12 \times 60 = 720\ \text{s}\)

  • Step 2: Apply average speed equation

    \[\text{Average speed} = \frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}} = \frac{3600\ \text{m}}{720\ \text{s}}\]

  • Step 3: Calculate

    \[\text{Average speed} = 5.0\ \text{m/s}\]

Answer: The cyclist’s average speed is \(5.0\ \text{m/s}\).

5.5.2 Example 2: Acceleration from Velocity Change

Question:
A car accelerates uniformly from \(8.0\ \text{m/s}\) to \(26.0\ \text{m/s}\) in \(6.0\ \text{s}\). Calculate its acceleration.

Solution:

  • Step 1: Identify known values and sign convention

    • Initial velocity, \(u = +8.0\ \text{m/s}\) (direction of motion = positive)

    • Final velocity, \(v = +26.0\ \text{m/s}\)

    • Time, \(t = 6.0\ \text{s}\)

  • Step 2: Apply acceleration equation

    \[a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{26.0 - 8.0}{6.0}\]

  • Step 3: Calculate

    \[a = \frac{18.0}{6.0} = 3.0\ \text{m/s}^{2}\]

Answer: The acceleration is \(3.0\ \text{m/s}^{2}\) in the direction of motion.

5.5.3 Example 3: Distance from Speed–Time Graph Area

Question:
The speed–time graph for a train journey shows: accelerate from 0 to 36 m/s in 6 s, constant speed for 9 s, then decelerate to 0 in 9 s. Calculate the total distance travelled.

Solution:

  • Step 1: Divide area under graph into simple shapes

    • Triangle A (0–6 s): base = 6 s, height = 36 m/s

    • Rectangle B (6–15 s): width = 9 s, height = 36 m/s

    • Triangle C (15–24 s): base = 9 s, height = 36 m/s

  • Step 2: Calculate area of each section

    • Area A = \(\frac{1}{2} \times 6 \times 36 = 108\ \text{m}\)

    • Area B = \(9 \times 36 = 324\ \text{m}\)

    • Area C = \(\frac{1}{2} \times 9 \times 36 = 162\ \text{m}\)

  • Step 3: Sum areas for total distance

    \[\text{Total distance} = 108 + 324 + 162 = 594\ \text{m}\]

Answer: The train travels a total distance of \(594\ \text{m}\).

5.5.4 Example 4: Free Fall with Air Resistance (Terminal Velocity)

Question:
A skydiver of mass 70 kg falls from rest. Air resistance \(R\) (in newtons) is given by \(R = 0.25v^{2}\), where \(v\) is speed in m/s. Take \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^{2}\).

  1. Calculate the skydiver’s initial acceleration.
  2. Determine the terminal velocity.

Solution:

  • Part (a): Initial acceleration (at \(v = 0\))

    • At release: \(v = 0\) → \(R = 0.25(0)^{2} = 0\ \text{N}\)

    • Resultant force: \(F_{\text{res}} = W - R = mg - 0 = 70 \times 10 = 700\ \text{N}\) downwards

    • Apply Newton’s second law: \(F_{\text{res}} = ma\)

      \[700 = 70a \quad \Rightarrow \quad a = 10\ \text{m/s}^{2}\]

  • Part (b): Terminal velocity (when \(a = 0\))

    • At terminal velocity: resultant force = 0 → \(W = R\)

      \[mg = 0.25v_{t}^{2}\]

      \[70 \times 10 = 0.25v_{t}^{2}\]

      \[v_{t}^{2} = \frac{700}{0.25} = 2800\]

      \[v_{t} = \sqrt{2800} = 52.9\ \text{m/s} \approx 53\ \text{m/s}\]

Answer:

  1. Initial acceleration = \(10\ \text{m/s}^{2}\) downwards

  2. Terminal velocity = \(53\ \text{m/s}\) downwards

5.5.5 Example 5: Using Gradient to Find Acceleration

Question:
The speed–time graph below shows a toy car accelerating uniformly. Speed increases from 2.4 m/s at \(t = 2.0\ \text{s}\) to 6.0 m/s at \(t = 5.0\ \text{s}\). Determine its acceleration.

Solution:

  • Step 1: Read speeds at specified times from graph

    • At \(t_{1} = 2.0\ \text{s}\), \(v_{1} = 2.4\ \text{m/s}\)

    • At \(t_{2} = 5.0\ \text{s}\), \(v_{2} = 6.0\ \text{m/s}\)

  • Step 2: Calculate change in velocity and time interval

    • \(\Delta v = v_{2} - v_{1} = 6.0 - 2.4 = 3.6\ \text{m/s}\)

    • \(\Delta t = t_{2} - t_{1} = 5.0 - 2.0 = 3.0\ \text{s}\)

  • Step 3: Apply acceleration equation (gradient)

    \[a = \frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{3.6}{3.0} = 1.2\ \text{m/s}^{2}\]

Answer: The acceleration is \(1.2\ \text{m/s}^{2}\).

5.6 Practice Questions

5.6.1 Core Level

  1. A runner completes a 200 m race in 25 s. Calculate her average speed.

  2. Define the term velocity and state how it differs from speed.

  3. The distance–time graph for a car journey is shown. Describe the motion in each section:

      1. Horizontal line
      1. Straight line with constant positive gradient
      1. Curve with increasing gradient

  4. A ball is dropped from rest. State the value of its acceleration due to gravity near Earth’s surface, including unit.

  5. The speed–time graph for a bicycle is a horizontal line at \(4.0\ \text{m/s}\) for 10 s. Calculate the distance travelled.

5.6.2 Extended Level

  1. A car accelerates uniformly from \(12\ \text{m/s}\) to \(30\ \text{m/s}\) in \(6.0\ \text{s}\). Calculate:

      1. Its acceleration
      1. The distance travelled during this time

  2. Explain, with reference to forces, why a falling object reaches terminal velocity in air but not in vacuum.

  3. The speed–time graph for a train shows:

    • 0–20 s: speed increases uniformly from 0 to \(20\ \text{m/s}\)

    • 20–50 s: constant speed \(20\ \text{m/s}\)

    • 50–65 s: speed decreases uniformly to 0

    Calculate:

      1. Acceleration during first 20 s
      1. Total distance travelled
      1. Average speed for whole journey

  4. A stone is thrown vertically upwards with initial velocity \(15\ \text{m/s}\). Take \(g = 10\ \text{m/s}^{2}\) downwards.

      1. Calculate time to reach maximum height
      1. Calculate maximum height reached
      1. State velocity when it returns to starting point (ignore air resistance)

  5. Distinguish between:

      1. Constant speed and constant velocity (provide example)
      1. Acceleration and deceleration (provide example with calculation)

5.7 Laboratory Investigation

5.7.1 Experiment 2.1: Investigating Acceleration Using a Trolley and Inclined Plane

Objective
To determine the acceleration of a trolley rolling down an inclined plane and to verify that acceleration is constant for a fixed slope angle.

Apparatus

  • Dynamics trolley

  • Inclined plane (ramp) with adjustable angle

  • Two light gates connected to data logger

  • Metre rule

  • Protractor

  • Clamp and stand

Variables

  • Independent: Angle of inclination, \(\theta\) (°)

  • Dependent: Acceleration, \(a\) (m/s\(^{2}\))

  • Controlled: Mass of trolley, distance between light gates, surface friction

Procedure

  1. Set up ramp at small angle (e.g., \(\theta = 5^{\circ}\)); measure angle with protractor

  2. Position light gates 0.50 m apart along ramp; measure distance accurately with metre rule

  3. Release trolley from rest at top of ramp (use electromagnet for consistent release)

  4. Data logger records time taken for trolley to pass each gate; calculate speeds \(u\) and \(v\)

  5. Calculate acceleration using \(a = \frac{v^{2} - u^{2}}{2s}\) (time-independent equation)

  6. Repeat steps 3–5 three times; calculate average acceleration

  7. Increase ramp angle to \(10^{\circ}\), \(15^{\circ}\), \(20^{\circ}\); repeat measurements

Data Table

Angle \(\theta\) (°) \(u\) (m/s) \(v\) (m/s) \(s\) (m) \(a\) (m/s\(^{2}\)) Average \(a\) (m/s\(^{2}\))
5 0.50
10 0.50
15 0.50
20 0.50

Analysis

  1. Plot graph of average acceleration \(a\) (y-axis) against \(\sin \theta\) (x-axis)

  2. Draw best-fit straight line through origin (theoretical relationship: \(a = g \sin \theta\))

  3. Determine gradient of graph; compare with accepted \(g = 9.8\ \text{m/s}^{2}\)

  4. Calculate percentage difference: \(\% \text{diff} = \frac{|g_{\text{exp}} - 9.8|}{9.8} \times 100\%\)

Expected Conclusion
Acceleration increases with \(\sin \theta\); gradient of \(a\) vs. \(\sin \theta\) graph approximates \(g\), confirming \(a = g \sin \theta\) for frictionless motion.

Safety Considerations

  • Ensure ramp is securely clamped to prevent slipping

  • Place soft landing material (e.g., foam) at bottom of ramp to cushion trolley

  • Keep fingers clear of moving trolley and light gate beams

  • Use low ramp angles initially to control trolley speed

5.9 Chapter Summary

5.9.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Speed = \(\frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}}\) (scalar); Velocity = \(\frac{\text{displacement}}{\text{time}}\) (vector, includes direction)

✓ Average speed = \(\frac{\text{total distance}}{\text{total time}}\); use when speed varies during journey

✓ Acceleration = \(\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = \frac{v - u}{t}\) (vector); SI unit: m/s\(^{2}\)

✓ Distance–time graph:

  • Gradient = speed

  • Horizontal line = rest; straight line = constant speed; curve = changing speed

✓ Speed–time graph:

  • Gradient = acceleration

  • Area under graph = distance travelled

  • Horizontal line = constant speed; straight line = constant acceleration

✓ Free fall:

  • Acceleration \(g \approx 9.8\ \text{m/s}^{2}\) downwards (near Earth’s surface)

  • Independent of mass in vacuum

  • With air resistance: object reaches terminal velocity when air resistance = weight

✓ Terminal velocity: Constant maximum speed when resultant force = 0 (acceleration = 0, velocity constant)

✓ Sign convention: Define positive direction at start; apply consistently to vector quantities

✓ Unit conversion: \(1\ \text{km/h} = \frac{1}{3.6}\ \text{m/s}\); always convert to SI units before calculation

5.10 Glossary

Term Definition
Acceleration Rate of change of velocity with time; vector quantity; SI unit: m/s\(^{2}\)
Average speed Total distance travelled divided by total time taken; scalar quantity
Deceleration Negative acceleration; decrease in speed with time when direction of motion is positive
Displacement Shortest straight-line distance from start to finish with direction; vector quantity
Distance Total path length travelled; scalar quantity; always positive or zero
Free fall Motion under gravity alone, with no other forces (especially air resistance) acting
Gradient Slope of a graph; calculated as \(\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}\); represents rate of change
Terminal velocity Constant maximum speed reached by a falling object when air resistance equals weight
Uniform acceleration Acceleration that remains constant in magnitude and direction
Velocity Speed in a specified direction; vector quantity; SI unit: m/s
