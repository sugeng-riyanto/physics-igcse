9  Momentum

Author

Sugeng Riyanto, M.Sc.

9.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content:

  • Define momentum as mass × velocity; recall and use the equation \(p = mv\)

  • Define impulse as force × time for which force acts; recall and use the equation \(\text{impulse} = F\Delta t = \Delta(mv)\)

  • Apply the principle of the conservation of momentum to solve simple problems in one dimension

  • Define resultant force as the change in momentum per unit time; recall and use the equation \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\)

9.2 What Is Momentum?

9.2.1 Definition and Formula

Momentum is a measure of the quantity of motion possessed by a moving object. It is defined as the product of mass and velocity:

\[p = mv\]

Where: - \(p\) = momentum (kg m/s) - \(m\) = mass (kg) - \(v\) = velocity (m/s)

Note

Key Concept: Momentum is a vector quantity—it has both magnitude and direction. The direction of momentum is the same as the direction of velocity.

9.2.2 Understanding Momentum Through Examples

Situation Explanation
Fast-moving arrow vs. slow-moving arrow (same mass) The faster arrow has greater momentum and causes more impact on the target
Elephant vs. dog (same velocity) The elephant has greater momentum due to its larger mass
Two trains moving in opposite directions They have different momenta because momentum includes direction (one positive, one negative)

9.2.3 Calculating Momentum: Worked Examples

9.2.3.1 Example 1: Basic Momentum Calculation

Question: Calculate the momentum of: (a) a runner of mass 50 kg running at 4 m/s (b) a soccer ball of mass 400 g moving at 25 m/s (c) a car of total mass 1000 kg travelling at 18 km/h

Solution:

(a) Runner: \[p = mv = 50 \times 4 = \boxed{200 \text{ kg m/s}}\]

(b) Soccer ball: First convert mass: \(400 \text{ g} = 0.4 \text{ kg}\) \[p = mv = 0.4 \times 25 = \boxed{10 \text{ kg m/s}}\]

(c) Car: First convert speed: \(18 \text{ km/h} = \frac{18 \times 1000}{3600} = 5 \text{ m/s}\) \[p = mv = 1000 \times 5 = \boxed{5000 \text{ kg m/s}}\]

9.2.3.2 Example 2: Finding Mass or Velocity from Momentum

Question: (a) What is the speed of a bus with mass 8000 kg and momentum of 88 000 kg m/s? (b) A car travelling at 12 m/s has a momentum of 14 400 kg m/s. Calculate its mass.

Solution:

(a) Speed of bus: \[p = mv \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \frac{p}{m} = \frac{88000}{8000} = \boxed{11 \text{ m/s}}\]

(b) Mass of car: \[p = mv \quad \Rightarrow \quad m = \frac{p}{v} = \frac{14400}{12} = \boxed{1200 \text{ kg}}\]

Warning

Important: To calculate momentum in SI units (kg m/s), mass must be in kilograms and velocity in metres per second. Always check and convert units before calculating.

9.3 Impulse and Change in Momentum

9.3.1 What Is Impulse?

When a force acts on an object for a period of time, it causes a change in the object’s momentum. This product of force and time is called impulse.

Definition: \[\text{Impulse} = F \times \Delta t\]

Where: - \(F\) = average resultant force (N) - \(\Delta t\) = time interval for which the force acts (s) - Unit: newton-second (N s)

9.3.2 The Impulse–Momentum Theorem

The impulse of a resultant force equals the change in momentum of the object:

\[F\Delta t = \Delta(mv) = \Delta p\]

Where: - \(\Delta p\) = change in momentum = \(p_{\text{final}} - p_{\text{initial}}\) (kg m/s or N s)

Note

Key Insight: The SI unit for impulse (N s) is equivalent to the SI unit for momentum (kg m/s). This confirms that impulse and change in momentum represent the same physical quantity.

9.3.3 Calculating Impulse: Worked Examples

9.3.3.1 Example 3: Impulse on a Kicked Ball

Question: A boy kicks a stationary ball of mass 0.4 kg with an average force of 100 N. The ball moves at 5 m/s immediately afterwards. Calculate: (a) the impulse of the force exerted on the ball (b) the time of contact between his boot and the ball

Solution:

(a) Impulse = change in momentum: \[\Delta p = mv_{\text{final}} - mv_{\text{initial}} = (0.4 \times 5) - (0.4 \times 0) = 2 \text{ kg m/s} = \boxed{2 \text{ N s}}\]

(b) Time of contact: \[F\Delta t = \Delta p \quad \Rightarrow \quad \Delta t = \frac{\Delta p}{F} = \frac{2}{100} = \boxed{0.02 \text{ s}}\]

9.3.3.2 Example 4: Impulse with Direction Change (Extended)

Question: A ball of mass 0.625 kg hits the ground at 4 m/s. It bounces back from the ground at 3.8 m/s. Calculate the impulse on the ball.

Solution:

Step 1: Assign directions (upwards = positive) - Initial velocity (downwards): \(u = -4.0 \text{ m/s}\) - Final velocity (upwards): \(v = +3.8 \text{ m/s}\)

Step 2: Calculate change in momentum: \[\Delta p = mv - mu = m(v - u)\] \[\Delta p = 0.625 \times [3.8 - (-4.0)] = 0.625 \times 7.8 = \boxed{4.88 \text{ N s (upwards)}}\]

Warning

Common Error: Forgetting that velocity is a vector. When an object reverses direction, the change in velocity is \(v - (-u) = v + u\), not \(v - u\).

9.4 Resultant Force and Rate of Change of Momentum

9.4.1 Newton’s Second Law in Momentum Form

From the impulse–momentum theorem: \[F\Delta t = \Delta p\]

Rearranging gives the definition of resultant force:

\[F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\]

Resultant force on an object is the change in momentum per unit time.

9.4.2 Connection to \(F = ma\)

For an object of constant mass: \[F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{\Delta(mv)}{\Delta t} = m\frac{\Delta v}{\Delta t} = ma\]

Thus, \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\) is the more general form of Newton’s Second Law, applicable even when mass changes (e.g., rockets expelling fuel).

9.4.3 Application: Crumple Zones and Safety

Worked Example 5: Force in a Car Crash

Question: A car of mass 1250 kg crashes into a concrete wall. The speed of the car is 7.2 m/s just before impact. Calculate the average force on the car if it takes: (a) 0.1 s to come to a complete stop (b) 0.4 s to come to a complete stop

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate initial momentum (forward = positive): \[p_{\text{initial}} = mv = 1250 \times 7.2 = 9000 \text{ kg m/s}\] \[p_{\text{final}} = 0 \text{ kg m/s (car stops)}\] \[\Delta p = 0 - 9000 = -9000 \text{ N s}\]

(a) Force for \(\Delta t = 0.1 \text{ s}\): \[F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t} = \frac{-9000}{0.1} = \boxed{-90000 \text{ N}}\]

(b) Force for \(\Delta t = 0.4 \text{ s}\): \[F = \frac{-9000}{0.4} = \boxed{-22500 \text{ N}}\]

Interpretation: The negative sign indicates the force acts backwards (opposing motion). Increasing the stopping time by a factor of 4 reduces the force by a factor of 4.

Note

Safety Application: Crumple zones, seat belts, and airbags increase the time over which momentum changes during a collision, thereby reducing the force on passengers and minimising injury.

9.5 The Principle of Conservation of Momentum

9.5.1 Statement of the Principle

When two or more objects interact in the absence of external forces, the total momentum of the system before the interaction equals the total momentum after the interaction.

\[\sum p_{\text{before}} = \sum p_{\text{after}}\]

9.5.2 Conditions for Conservation

Condition Explanation
Closed system No external forces act on the system (or external forces are negligible during the interaction)
One-dimensional problems For IGCSE, we consider collisions and explosions along a straight line
Vector addition Assign one direction as positive; opposite direction is negative

9.5.3 Types of Interactions

Type Description Example
Collision (objects stick together) Objects move together after impact Two trolleys coupling; car crash
Collision (objects separate) Objects rebound after impact Billiard balls; Newton’s cradle
Explosion (objects separate from rest) Stationary system breaks apart Firework; recoil of a gun

9.5.4 Applying Conservation of Momentum: Worked Examples

9.5.4.1 Example 6: Collision with Objects Sticking Together

Question: Trolley A of mass 2 kg travelling at 2 m/s collides with a stationary trolley B of mass 3 kg. The two trolleys stick together after the collision. Calculate: (a) the velocity of the combined trolleys immediately after collision (b) the impulse experienced by trolley B

Solution:

(a) Velocity after collision:

Step 1: Total momentum before collision: \[p_{\text{before}} = m_A v_A + m_B v_B = (2 \times 2) + (3 \times 0) = 4 \text{ kg m/s}\]

Step 2: Total momentum after collision (combined mass = 5 kg): \[p_{\text{after}} = (m_A + m_B)v = 5v\]

Step 3: Apply conservation: \[5v = 4 \quad \Rightarrow \quad v = \frac{4}{5} = \boxed{0.8 \text{ m/s (forward)}}\]

(b) Impulse on trolley B: \[\text{Impulse} = \Delta p_B = m_B v_{\text{final}} - m_B v_{\text{initial}} = (3 \times 0.8) - (3 \times 0) = \boxed{2.4 \text{ N s (forward)}}\]

9.5.4.2 Example 7: Collision with Objects Separating

Question: Ball A of mass 0.12 kg is moving forward at 0.40 m/s. It collides with a stationary ball B of mass 0.09 kg. After collision, ball B moves forward at 0.40 m/s. What is the velocity of ball A after the collision?

Solution:

Step 1: Total momentum before collision: \[p_{\text{before}} = (0.12 \times 0.40) + (0.09 \times 0) = 0.048 \text{ kg m/s}\]

Step 2: Total momentum after collision (let \(v\) = velocity of A after): \[p_{\text{after}} = (0.12 \times v) + (0.09 \times 0.40) = 0.12v + 0.036\]

Step 3: Apply conservation: \[0.12v + 0.036 = 0.048\] \[0.12v = 0.012\] \[v = \frac{0.012}{0.12} = \boxed{0.10 \text{ m/s (forward)}}\]

9.5.4.3 Example 8: Explosion from Rest (Extended)

Question: Two ice skaters, A (mass 80 kg) and B (mass 50 kg), are initially stationary on a frictionless rink. They push each other apart. Skater A moves off with velocity 0.5 m/s. Calculate: (a) the momentum of skater A (b) the momentum of skater B (c) the velocity of skater B

Solution:

(a) Momentum of A: \[p_A = m_A v_A = 80 \times 0.5 = \boxed{40 \text{ kg m/s}}\]

(b) Momentum of B: Total momentum before = 0 (both stationary) By conservation: \(p_A + p_B = 0\) \[p_B = -p_A = \boxed{-40 \text{ kg m/s}}\]

(c) Velocity of B: \[v_B = \frac{p_B}{m_B} = \frac{-40}{50} = \boxed{-0.8 \text{ m/s}}\]

Interpretation: Skater B moves at 0.8 m/s in the opposite direction to skater A.

Warning

Important: In explosion problems, the total initial momentum is zero. Therefore, the momenta of the separating objects must be equal in magnitude and opposite in direction.

9.6 Key Concepts Summary

9.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Momentum: \(p = mv\); vector quantity (kg m/s); direction same as velocity

  2. Impulse: \(F\Delta t = \Delta p\); equals change in momentum; unit N s ≡ kg m/s

  3. Resultant force: \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\); rate of change of momentum (general form of \(F = ma\))

  4. Conservation of momentum: \(\sum p_{\text{before}} = \sum p_{\text{after}}\) for closed systems with no external forces

  5. Vector handling: Assign one direction as positive; opposite direction is negative; add algebraically

9.6.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Direction changes: When an object reverses direction, \(\Delta v = v - (-u) = v + u\)

  2. Safety applications: Increasing collision time reduces force (\(F = \Delta p / \Delta t\))

  3. Explosions: Initial momentum = 0 → final momenta are equal and opposite

9.7 Common Misconceptions

9.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Momentum is the same as force”

Incorrect: “A fast-moving object has a large force”

Correct: - Momentum (\(p = mv\)) is a property of a moving object - Force (\(F = \Delta p / \Delta t\)) is what changes momentum - An object can have large momentum with zero resultant force (moving at constant velocity)

9.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Momentum is conserved in all collisions”

Incorrect: “Total momentum is always conserved, even with friction”

Correct: - Momentum is conserved only in closed systems with no external forces - In real collisions, friction or air resistance may act as external forces - For IGCSE problems, we assume external forces are negligible during the brief collision

9.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Heavier objects always have more momentum”

Incorrect: “A truck always has more momentum than a car”

Correct: - Momentum depends on both mass and velocity: \(p = mv\) - A light object moving very fast can have more momentum than a heavy slow object - Example: A 0.01 kg bullet at 500 m/s (\(p = 5\) kg m/s) vs. a 1000 kg car at 0.001 m/s (\(p = 1\) kg m/s)

9.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Impulse and momentum are different physical quantities”

Incorrect: “Impulse (N s) and momentum (kg m/s) cannot be compared”

Correct: - The units are equivalent: \(1 \text{ N s} = 1 \text{ kg m/s}\) - Impulse equals change in momentum: \(F\Delta t = \Delta p\) - They represent the same physical dimension, just different contexts

9.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “In a collision, both objects experience the same change in velocity”

Incorrect: “When two objects collide, they change speed by the same amount”

Correct: - Both objects experience equal and opposite impulses (Newton’s Third Law) - But \(\Delta v = \frac{\text{impulse}}{m}\), so the lighter object experiences a larger change in velocity - Example: A tennis ball hitting a wall rebounds with large \(\Delta v\); the wall’s \(\Delta v\) is negligible

9.7.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “If momentum is conserved, kinetic energy is also conserved”

Incorrect: “Conservation of momentum means no energy is lost”

Correct: - Momentum is always conserved in closed systems - Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions - Most real collisions are inelastic: momentum conserved, but kinetic energy is converted to heat, sound, or deformation

9.8 Worked Examples

9.8.1 Example 9: Multi-Step Conservation Problem

Question: A car of mass 1200 kg travelling at 8.0 m/s collides with a lorry of mass 2800 kg travelling at 2.0 m/s in the same direction. After the collision, the two vehicles stick together. Calculate their speed immediately after the collision.

Solution:

Step 1: Assign direction (forward = positive)

Step 2: Total momentum before collision: \[p_{\text{before}} = (1200 \times 8.0) + (2800 \times 2.0) = 9600 + 5600 = 15200 \text{ kg m/s}\]

Step 3: Total momentum after collision (combined mass = 4000 kg): \[p_{\text{after}} = 4000v\]

Step 4: Apply conservation: \[4000v = 15200\] \[v = \frac{15200}{4000} = \boxed{3.8 \text{ m/s (forward)}}\]

9.8.2 Example 10: Force from Momentum Change (Extended)

Question: A basketball player bounces a ball on the ground. The ball hits the ground 10 times in 20 seconds. The average change in momentum for each collision is 15 kg m/s. What is the average force that the ground exerts on the ball?

Solution:

Step 1: Total change in momentum over 20 s: \[\Delta p_{\text{total}} = 10 \times 15 = 150 \text{ kg m/s}\]

Step 2: Average force: \[F_{\text{avg}} = \frac{\Delta p_{\text{total}}}{\Delta t} = \frac{150}{20} = \boxed{7.5 \text{ N}}\]

Note

Interpretation: This is the average force over the 20-second interval. The actual force during each brief impact is much larger, but acts for a very short time.

    1. the average force

    1. the change in momentum of the ball
    1. the impulse exerted by the wall on the ball

    1. their common velocity after collision
    1. the change in momentum of the car

9.10 Laboratory Investigation

9.10.1 Experiment 6.1: Verifying Conservation of Momentum

Objective: To investigate the conservation of momentum in a one-dimensional collision using trolleys on a friction-compensated runway.

Apparatus: - Two dynamics trolleys with Velcro attachments (for sticking collisions)

  • Friction-compensated runway

  • Light gates or ticker-timer with tape

  • Electronic balance

  • Metre rule

  • Data logger (optional)

Variables: - Independent: Masses of trolleys; initial velocity of moving trolley

  • Dependent: Final velocity of combined trolleys (or both trolleys if separating)

  • Controlled: Runway angle (friction compensation); same collision mechanism

Procedure (Collision with Sticking):

  1. Measure and record masses \(m_A\) and \(m_B\) of the two trolleys.

  2. Set up the runway with a slight incline to compensate for friction (trolleys move at constant speed when given a gentle push).

  3. Place trolley B stationary at the centre of the runway.

  4. Give trolley A a gentle push so it collides with B; the Velcro causes them to stick together.

  5. Use light gates or ticker-tape to measure:

    • Velocity of A before collision (\(v_A\))
    • Velocity of combined trolleys after collision (\(v_{\text{combined}}\))

  6. Repeat three times for reliability.

  7. Calculate:

    • Total momentum before: \(p_{\text{before}} = m_A v_A\)
    • Total momentum after: \(p_{\text{after}} = (m_A + m_B) v_{\text{combined}}\)

  8. Compare \(p_{\text{before}}\) and \(p_{\text{after}}\); calculate percentage difference.

Data Table:

Trial \(m_A\) (kg) \(m_B\) (kg) \(v_A\) (m/s) \(v_{\text{combined}}\) (m/s) \(p_{\text{before}}\) (kg m/s) \(p_{\text{after}}\) (kg m/s) % Difference
1
2
3

Analysis:

  1. For each trial, calculate percentage difference: \[\% \text{ diff} = \frac{|p_{\text{before}} - p_{\text{after}}|}{(p_{\text{before}} + p_{\text{after}})/2} \times 100\%\]

  2. If all differences are within ±10% (typical experimental accuracy), conservation of momentum is verified.

  3. Identify sources of error: residual friction, air resistance, timing inaccuracies.

Precautions: - ✓ Ensure runway is properly friction-compensated before starting

  • ✓ Use light gates or ticker-timer for accurate velocity measurement

  • ✓ Ensure trolleys collide centrally to avoid rotational motion

  • ✓ Repeat measurements to obtain reliable averages

Conclusion Template: “The experiment verified the principle of conservation of momentum: within experimental error (±___%), the total momentum before collision equalled the total momentum after collision. This confirms that in the absence of significant external forces, momentum is conserved in one-dimensional collisions.”

Extension (Supplement): - Investigate elastic collisions (using magnetic repulsion instead of Velcro)

  • Measure kinetic energy before and after to distinguish elastic vs. inelastic collisions

9.12 Chapter Summary

9.12.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Momentum: \(p = mv\); vector (kg m/s); direction = velocity direction
✓ Impulse: \(F\Delta t = \Delta p\); equals change in momentum; unit N s ≡ kg m/s
✓ Resultant force: \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\); general form of Newton’s Second Law
✓ Conservation of momentum: \(\sum p_{\text{before}} = \sum p_{\text{after}}\) for closed systems
✓ Vector handling: Assign + direction; opposite = −; add algebraically
✓ Safety principle: Increasing \(\Delta t\) reduces \(F\) for same \(\Delta p\) (\(F = \Delta p / \Delta t\))
✓ Explosions: Initial \(p = 0\) → final momenta equal and opposite

9.14 Glossary

Term Definition
Closed system A system on which no external forces act; required for momentum conservation
Conservation of momentum Principle that total momentum of a closed system remains constant during interactions
Impulse Product of force and time for which it acts; equals change in momentum (\(F\Delta t = \Delta p\))
Inelastic collision Collision in which kinetic energy is not conserved (converted to heat, sound, deformation); momentum is conserved
Momentum (\(p\)) Product of mass and velocity (\(p = mv\)); vector quantity; SI unit: kg m/s
Resultant force Single force equivalent to all forces acting on an object; \(F = \frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}\)
Vector quantity Physical quantity with both magnitude and direction (e.g., momentum, velocity, force)
