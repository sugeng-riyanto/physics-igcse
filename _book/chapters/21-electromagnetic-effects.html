24.2 Electromagnetic Induction

24.2.1 What Is Electromagnetic Induction?

In 1831, Michael Faraday discovered that a changing magnetic field can produce an electric current. This phenomenon is known as electromagnetic induction.

Electromagnetic induction is the process through which an induced e.m.f. is produced in a conductor due to a changing magnetic field.

If the conductor is part of a complete circuit, the induced e.m.f. will drive an induced current.

24.2.2 Demonstrating Electromagnetic Induction

Apparatus:

  • Solenoid (coil of insulated wire)

  • Strong bar magnet

  • Centre-zero galvanometer (sensitive ammeter)

  • Connecting wires

Procedure and Observations:

  1. Move the N pole of the magnet into the solenoid.

    • Observation: Galvanometer needle deflects momentarily to one side.

    • Conclusion: An e.m.f. is induced, causing a current to flow.

  2. Hold the magnet stationary inside the solenoid.

    • Observation: Galvanometer needle returns to zero and stays there.

    • Conclusion: No e.m.f. is induced when there is no relative motion or change in magnetic flux.

  3. Withdraw the magnet out of the solenoid.

    • Observation: Galvanometer needle deflects momentarily to the opposite side.

    • Conclusion: The direction of the induced e.m.f. reverses when the direction of motion reverses.

  4. Repeat with the S pole, or reverse the magnet’s poles.

    • Observation: Deflection directions reverse accordingly.

24.2.3 Factors Affecting the Magnitude of Induced E.m.f.

The size of the induced e.m.f. depends on the rate of change of magnetic flux through the coil. It can be increased by:

  1. Increasing the speed of relative motion between the magnet and the coil.

  2. Increasing the strength of the magnetic field (using a stronger magnet).

  3. Increasing the number of turns on the solenoid.

  4. Increasing the cross-sectional area of the coil.

24.2.4 Lenz’s Law and Fleming’s Right-Hand Rule (Extended)

Lenz’s Law:

The direction of the induced e.m.f. (and hence induced current) is always such that it opposes the change causing it.

  • When a N pole moves into a coil, the induced current creates a N pole at that end to repel the incoming magnet.

  • When a N pole moves out of a coil, the induced current creates a S pole at that end to attract the departing magnet.

  • This ensures conservation of energy: mechanical work must be done to overcome the magnetic opposition, which is converted into electrical energy.

Fleming’s Right-Hand Rule (for generators):

Used to determine the direction of induced current when a conductor moves in a magnetic field.

  • Thumb: Direction of motion (force applied)

  • Forefinger: Direction of magnetic field (N to S)

  • Second finger: Direction of induced current

24.3 The A.C. Generator

24.3.1 How Does an A.C. Generator Work?

An a.c. generator converts mechanical energy into electrical energy using electromagnetic induction.

Key Components:

  1. Armature coil: Rectangular coil of wire mounted on an axle.

  2. Magnets: Provide a uniform magnetic field.

  3. Slip rings: Two continuous metal rings attached to the coil ends, rotating with it.

  4. Carbon brushes: Stationary contacts pressing against the slip rings to transfer current to the external circuit without twisting wires.

Operation:

  • As the coil rotates, it cuts through magnetic field lines.

  • This changes the magnetic flux linking the coil, inducing an alternating e.m.f.

  • The direction of induced current reverses every half-turn, producing alternating current (a.c.).

24.3.2 E.m.F. Against Time Graph

The induced e.m.f. varies sinusoidally with time:

  • Maximum e.m.f. (peaks/troughs): Occur when the plane of the coil is parallel to the magnetic field. The sides of the coil cut field lines at the maximum rate.

  • Zero e.m.f.: Occurs when the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the magnetic field. The sides move parallel to field lines and cut none.

Factors increasing maximum induced e.m.f.:

  1. Increasing the number of turns in the coil.

  2. Using stronger magnets.

  3. Increasing the frequency of rotation.

  4. Winding the coil around a soft iron core to concentrate flux.

24.4 Magnetic Effect of a Current

24.4.1 Magnetic Field Patterns

When an electric current flows through a conductor, it produces a magnetic field around it.

1. Straight Current-Carrying Wire:

  • Pattern: Concentric circles centred on the wire.

  • Direction: Determined by the right-hand grip rule (thumb = current direction, curled fingers = field direction).

  • Strength: Strongest near the wire; decreases with distance. Increases with current magnitude.

2. Solenoid (Coil of Wire):

  • Pattern: Resembles a bar magnet; field lines emerge from one end (N) and enter the other (S).

  • Inside: Field lines are straight, parallel, and equally spaced → uniform magnetic field.

  • Direction: Right-hand grip rule (fingers curl in current direction, thumb points to N pole).

  • Strength increased by: Increasing current, increasing turns per unit length, inserting a soft iron core.

24.4.2 Applications

Relays:

  • An electromagnetic switch that uses a small current in one circuit to control a large current in another.

  • When the control circuit is closed, the electromagnet attracts an iron armature, closing the high-power circuit contacts.

  • Provides safe isolation between low-voltage control circuits and high-voltage power circuits.

Loudspeakers:

  • An alternating current passes through a coil attached to a paper cone, placed in a radial magnetic field.

  • The motor effect produces an alternating force on the coil, causing the cone to vibrate.

  • Cone vibrations produce sound waves matching the electrical signal frequency.

24.5 Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor

24.5.1 The Motor Effect

When a current-carrying conductor is placed in an external magnetic field, it experiences a force. This is the motor effect.

Conditions:

  • The conductor must not be parallel to the magnetic field.

  • Maximum force occurs when the conductor is perpendicular to the field.

Reversing the Force:

  • Reversing the current direction reverses the force.

  • Reversing the magnetic field direction reverses the force.

24.5.2 Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule (Extended)

Used to determine the direction of force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field:

  • Thumb: Direction of Force (Motion)

  • Forefinger: Direction of Magnetic Field (N to S)

  • Second finger: Direction of Current (conventional, + to –)

Force on Charged Particle Beams:

  • A beam of moving charges is equivalent to a current.

  • Positive particles: Deflect according to conventional current direction.

  • Negative particles (electrons): Deflect in the opposite direction to the second finger, as electron flow is opposite to conventional current.

  • Particles move in circular paths while inside a uniform magnetic field.

24.6 The D.C. Motor

24.6.1 How a D.C. Motor Works

A d.c. motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy.

Structure:

  • Rectangular coil mounted on an axle between magnetic poles.

  • Connected to a split-ring commutator and carbon brushes.

Operation Cycle:

  1. Current flows through the coil.

  2. Forces on opposite sides of the coil are in opposite directions (Fleming’s left-hand rule), creating a turning effect (torque).

  3. The coil rotates.

  4. When the coil reaches the vertical position, the split-ring commutator gaps align with the brushes, cutting off current momentarily.

  5. Momentum carries the coil past the vertical.

  6. The commutator reverses contact, reversing current direction in the coil.

  7. Forces reverse, ensuring continuous rotation in the same direction.

24.6.2 Increasing the Turning Effect

  1. Increasing the current through the coil.

  2. Increasing the number of turns on the coil.

  3. Using stronger permanent magnets.

  4. Winding the coil on a soft iron core to concentrate the magnetic field.

24.7 The Transformer

24.7.1 Construction and Principle

A transformer changes the voltage of an alternating current supply.

Construction:

  • Primary coil: Connected to a.c. supply.

  • Secondary coil: Connected to the load.

  • Laminated soft iron core: Links the two coils magnetically. Lamination reduces eddy current heating.

Principle of Operation:

  1. Alternating current in the primary coil produces a changing magnetic field.

  2. The soft iron core channels this changing flux to the secondary coil.

  3. The changing flux induces an alternating e.m.f. in the secondary coil (mutual induction).

  4. Does not work with d.c. because d.c. produces a constant magnetic field → no change in flux → no induced e.m.f.

24.7.2 Transformer Equations

Voltage and Turns Ratio:

\[\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}\]

  • Step-up transformer: \(N_s > N_p\) → \(V_s > V_p\) (used at power stations).

  • Step-down transformer: \(N_s < N_p\) → \(V_s < V_p\) (used in homes, chargers).

Power and Efficiency (Ideal Transformer):

Assuming 100% efficiency (power in = power out):

\[I_p V_p = I_s V_s\]

Where:

  • \(V_p, V_s\) = primary and secondary voltages

  • \(I_p, I_s\) = primary and secondary currents

  • \(N_p, N_s\) = number of turns in primary and secondary coils

24.7.3 High-Voltage Power Transmission

Problem: Power cables have resistance \(R\). Power loss due to heating is given by:

\[P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R\]

Solution:

  • Use step-up transformers to increase voltage at the power station.

  • Since \(P = VI\), for constant power \(P\), increasing \(V\) drastically decreases current \(I\).

  • Lower current means \(I^2R\) losses are minimised.

  • Step-down transformers reduce voltage to safe levels (e.g., 230 V or 240 V) for domestic use.

24.8 Key Concepts Summary

24.8.1 Core Concepts

  1. Electromagnetic induction: Changing magnetic flux induces e.m.f.; requires relative motion or changing current.

  2. Factors affecting induction: Speed, magnetic field strength, number of turns, coil area.

  3. A.C. generator: Rotating coil in magnetic field produces a.c.; slip rings and brushes transfer current.

  4. Magnetic effect of current: Straight wires produce circular fields; solenoids produce bar-magnet-like fields.

  5. Applications: Relays (electromagnetic switches), loudspeakers (motor effect converts electricity to sound).

  6. Motor effect: Force on current-carrying conductor in magnetic field; maximum when perpendicular.

  7. D.C. motor: Split-ring commutator reverses current every half-turn for continuous rotation.

  8. Transformer: Changes a.c. voltage via mutual induction; \(V_p/V_s = N_p/N_s\).

  9. Power transmission: High voltage reduces current, minimising \(I^2R\) heating losses in cables.

24.8.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Lenz’s Law: Induced e.m.f. direction opposes the change causing it (conservation of energy).

  2. Fleming’s rules: Left-hand rule for motors/force; Right-hand rule for generators/induction.

  3. Charged particle deflection: Direction depends on charge sign; negative charges deflect opposite to conventional current.

  4. Ideal transformer equation: \(I_pV_p = I_sV_s\); step-up increases voltage but decreases current proportionally.

  5. Power loss explanation: \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R\); doubling voltage quarters the current, reducing loss by factor of 16.

24.9 Common Misconceptions

24.9.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “A transformer works with both a.c. and d.c.”

Incorrect: “Transformers can step up or step down direct current.”

Correct:

  • Transformers rely on a changing magnetic flux to induce e.m.f.

  • D.c. produces a constant magnetic field → no change in flux → zero induced e.m.f. in secondary.

  • Transformers only work with alternating current.

24.9.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Induced current flows in the same direction as the motion causing it”

Incorrect: “Moving a N pole into a coil makes that end a S pole to help it enter.”

Correct:

  • Lenz’s Law states the induced current opposes the change.

  • Moving N pole in → induced N pole to repel it.

  • Mechanical work is required to overcome this repulsion, conserving energy.

24.9.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Slip rings and split-ring commutators are the same”

Incorrect: “Both are used interchangeably in generators and motors.”

Correct:

  • Slip rings: Continuous rings; maintain same electrical contact; used in a.c. generators to output alternating current.

  • Split-ring commutator: Split into two halves; reverses contact every half-turn; used in d.c. motors to maintain unidirectional rotation.

24.9.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Fleming’s left-hand and right-hand rules can be used interchangeably”

Incorrect: “Either hand rule works for both motors and generators.”

Correct:

  • Left-hand rule: For motors (current + magnetic field → force/motion).

  • Right-hand rule: For generators/induction (motion + magnetic field → induced current).

  • Mixing them gives incorrect directions.

24.9.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “High voltage transmission causes high power loss”

Incorrect: “Stepping up voltage increases energy waste in cables.”

Correct:

  • Power loss depends on current, not voltage: \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R\).

  • High voltage transmission reduces current for the same power (\(P = VI\)).

  • Lower current drastically reduces \(I^2R\) heating losses.

24.9.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “The magnetic field inside a solenoid is zero”

Incorrect: “Coils only produce magnetic fields outside themselves.”

Correct:

  • Inside a solenoid, field lines are straight, parallel, and closely spaced.

  • This indicates a strong, uniform magnetic field inside the coil.

  • Outside, the field resembles a bar magnet and is weaker.

24.9.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “Electron beams deflect in the same direction as conventional current”

Incorrect: “A beam of electrons deflects according to Fleming’s left-hand rule second finger.”

Correct:

  • Electrons are negatively charged.

  • Their motion is opposite to conventional current direction.

  • When applying Fleming’s left-hand rule, the second finger must point opposite to electron motion to find the correct force direction.

24.10 Worked Examples

24.10.1 Example 1: Transformer Calculations

Question:

A step-down transformer has 2400 turns on the primary coil and 120 turns on the secondary coil. The primary voltage is 240 V.

  1. Calculate the secondary voltage.

  2. If the secondary current is 4.0 A, calculate the primary current (assume 100% efficiency).

  3. State one advantage of using this transformer in a phone charger.

Solution:

(a) Secondary voltage:

\[\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s} \Rightarrow V_s = V_p \times \frac{N_s}{N_p} = 240 \times \frac{120}{2400} = \boxed{12 \text{ V}}\]

(b) Primary current:

\[I_p V_p = I_s V_s \Rightarrow I_p = \frac{I_s V_s}{V_p} = \frac{4.0 \times 12}{240} = \boxed{0.20 \text{ A}}\]

(c) Advantage:

  • Steps down high mains voltage to a safe, low voltage suitable for charging delicate electronic devices without damage.

24.10.2 Example 2: Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule Application

Question:

A straight wire carries a current flowing vertically upwards. It is placed in a uniform magnetic field directed horizontally from left to right. Determine the direction of the force on the wire.

Solution:

Step 1: Align left hand.

Step 2: Forefinger points right (magnetic field direction).

Step 3: Second finger points up (current direction).

Step 4: Thumb points into the page (away from observer).

Answer: The force acts perpendicularly into the plane of the page.

24.10.3 Example 3: A.C. Generator Graph Interpretation

Question:

The graph shows the e.m.f. output of an a.c. generator against time. At \(t = 0\), the coil is perpendicular to the magnetic field.

  1. At what times is the e.m.f. zero? Explain why.

  2. At what times is the e.m.f. maximum? Explain why.

Solution:

(a) Zero e.m.f. times:

  • Occurs at \(t = 0\), \(T/2\), \(T\), etc.

  • Explanation: Coil plane is perpendicular to magnetic field. Sides of coil move parallel to field lines and cut no flux. Rate of change of flux is zero.

(b) Maximum e.m.f. times:

  • Occurs at \(t = T/4\), \(3T/4\), etc.

  • Explanation: Coil plane is parallel to magnetic field. Sides cut field lines at maximum rate. Rate of change of flux is maximum.

24.10.4 Example 4: Power Transmission Loss Calculation

Question:

A power station transmits 500 kW of power to a town.

  1. Calculate the current in the transmission cables if the voltage is 10 kV.

  2. Calculate the power loss if the total cable resistance is 5.0 Ω.

  3. If the voltage is stepped up to 100 kV, calculate the new power loss and explain the benefit.

Solution:

(a) Current at 10 kV:

\[I = \frac{P}{V} = \frac{500\,000}{10\,000} = 50 \text{ A}\]

(b) Power loss at 10 kV:

\[P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R = 50^2 \times 5.0 = \boxed{12\,500 \text{ W} = 12.5 \text{ kW}}\]

(c) Current at 100 kV:

\[I = \frac{500\,000}{100\,000} = 5.0 \text{ A}\]

\[P_{\text{loss}} = 5.0^2 \times 5.0 = \boxed{125 \text{ W} = 0.125 \text{ kW}}\]

Benefit: Increasing voltage by 10× reduces current by 10×, reducing power loss by a factor of 100 (\(I^2R\) dependence). This greatly improves transmission efficiency.

24.10.5 Example 5: Electromagnetic Induction Factors

Question:

A student moves a bar magnet into a solenoid connected to a galvanometer. State what happens to the galvanometer deflection if:

  1. The magnet is moved faster.

  2. The magnet is held stationary inside the coil.

  3. The magnet is withdrawn with the same speed as insertion.

Solution:

(a) Moved faster:

  • Deflection angle increases.

  • Reason: Higher rate of change of magnetic flux → larger induced e.m.f. → larger current.

(b) Held stationary:

  • Deflection returns to zero.

  • Reason: No change in magnetic flux → no induced e.m.f.

(c) Withdrawn:

  • Deflection is in the opposite direction.

  • Reason: Change in flux is reversed → induced e.m.f. polarity reverses (Lenz’s Law).

24.12 Laboratory Investigation

24.12.1 Experiment 21.1: Investigating Transformer Turns Ratio

Objective:

To investigate the relationship between the number of turns on the primary and secondary coils of a transformer and the output voltage.

Apparatus:

  • Laminated soft iron core (C-cores or E-cores)

  • Primary coil (known turns, e.g., 100 turns)

  • Secondary coils (varying turns, e.g., 50, 150, 200, 300 turns)

  • Low-voltage a.c. power supply (e.g., 2 V or 6 V a.c.)

  • A.C. voltmeter (dual range or two meters)

  • Connecting wires

Variables:

  • Independent: Number of turns on secondary coil (\(N_s\))

  • Dependent: Secondary voltage (\(V_s\))

  • Controlled: Primary voltage (\(V_p\)), primary turns (\(N_p\)), core material, coil tightness

Procedure:

  1. Assemble the transformer by clamping the C-cores together with the primary and secondary coils.

  2. Connect the primary coil to the a.c. power supply set to a fixed voltage (e.g., 6.0 V).

  3. Measure and record the actual primary voltage \(V_p\) using an a.c. voltmeter.

  4. Connect the secondary coil with \(N_s = 50\) turns to a second a.c. voltmeter.

  5. Measure and record the secondary voltage \(V_s\).

  6. Repeat steps 4–5 for secondary coils with 100, 150, 200, and 300 turns.

  7. Record all results in a table.

Data Table:

\(N_p\) (turns) \(V_p\) (V) \(N_s\) (turns) \(V_s\) (V) Calculated ratio \(V_s/V_p\) Expected ratio \(N_s/N_p\)
100 50
100 100
100 150
100 200
100 300

Analysis:

  1. Calculate the ratio \(V_s/V_p\) and \(N_s/N_p\) for each trial.

  2. Plot a graph of \(V_s\) (y-axis) against \(N_s\) (x-axis).

  3. Determine the gradient of the graph. The gradient should equal \(V_p/N_p\).

  4. Comment on whether the results support the equation \(V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p\).

Precautions:

  • ✓ Ensure the iron cores are tightly clamped to minimise magnetic flux leakage.

  • ✓ Use a.c. supply only; d.c. will not induce voltage and may overheat the coil.

  • ✓ Allow the power supply and coils to cool between readings if they become warm.

  • ✓ Check voltmeter zero error and select appropriate ranges.

Expected Results:

  • The graph of \(V_s\) against \(N_s\) will be a straight line through the origin.

  • The ratio \(V_s/V_p\) will approximately equal \(N_s/N_p\).

  • Slight deviations may occur due to energy losses (eddy currents, hysteresis, resistance).

Conclusion Template:

“The experiment verified the transformer equation \(V_s/V_p = N_s/N_p\). The secondary voltage was directly proportional to the number of turns on the secondary coil. The gradient of the \(V_s\) vs \(N_s\) graph agreed with \(V_p/N_p\) within experimental limits, confirming ideal transformer behaviour.”

Extension (Supplement):

  • Investigate efficiency by measuring primary and secondary currents simultaneously.

  • Compare results with and without the soft iron core to demonstrate its role in flux linkage.

  • Research sources of energy loss in real transformers and suggest engineering solutions.

24.13 Exam-Style Questions

24.13.1 Paper 1 Style (Multiple Choice)

  1. Which change will not increase the magnitude of the induced e.m.f. in a coil?

A. Increasing the speed of the magnet

B. Increasing the number of turns on the coil

C. Using a stronger magnet

D. Using a coil with higher electrical resistance

  1. What is the function of the split-ring commutator in a d.c. motor?

A. To increase the magnetic field strength

24.14 Chapter Summary

24.14.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Electromagnetic induction: Changing magnetic flux induces e.m.f.; requires relative motion or changing current

✓ Induction factors: Speed, magnetic field strength, number of turns, coil area

✓ A.C. generator: Rotating coil produces a.c.; slip rings maintain continuous contact; output is sinusoidal

✓ Magnetic effect of current: Straight wire → concentric circles; solenoid → uniform inside, bar-magnet-like outside

✓ Applications: Relays (electromagnetic switches), loudspeakers (motor effect on coil)

✓ Motor effect: Force on current-carrying conductor in magnetic field; max when perpendicular

✓ D.C. motor: Split-ring commutator reverses current every half-turn for continuous rotation

✓ Transformer: Changes a.c. voltage via mutual induction; \(V_p/V_s = N_p/N_s\)

✓ High-voltage transmission: Reduces current, minimising \(I^2R\) heating losses in cables

✓ Lenz’s Law (Extended): Induced e.m.f. opposes the change causing it (conservation of energy)

✓ Fleming’s rules (Extended): Left-hand = motor/force; Right-hand = generator/induction

✓ Ideal transformer (Extended): \(I_pV_p = I_sV_s\); power loss \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2R\)

24.14.2 Formulae Sheet

Quantity Formula Variables
Transformer voltage ratio \(\frac{V_p}{V_s} = \frac{N_p}{N_s}\) \(V\)=voltage, \(N\)=number of turns
Ideal transformer power \(I_p V_p = I_s V_s\) \(I\)=current
Power loss in cables \(P_{\text{loss}} = I^2 R\) \(R\)=cable resistance
Electrical power \(P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}\) Standard power equations

24.16 Glossary

Term Definition
A.C. generator Device that converts mechanical energy to alternating electrical energy using electromagnetic induction
D.C. motor Device that converts electrical energy to mechanical rotation using the motor effect and split-ring commutator
Electromagnetic induction Production of an e.m.f. in a conductor due to a changing magnetic field
Fleming’s left-hand rule Rule to determine direction of force on a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field
Fleming’s right-hand rule Rule to determine direction of induced current when a conductor moves in a magnetic field
Lenz’s Law The direction of induced e.m.f. opposes the change in magnetic flux that produced it
Motor effect The force experienced by a current-carrying conductor placed in an external magnetic field
Relay An electromagnetic switch used to control a high-current circuit with a low-current signal
Slip rings Continuous metal rings in an a.c. generator that transfer alternating current to the external circuit
Split-ring commutator A split metal ring in a d.c. motor that reverses current direction in the coil every half-turn
Step-down transformer Transformer where secondary voltage is lower than primary voltage (\(N_s < N_p\))
Step-up transformer Transformer where secondary voltage is higher than primary voltage (\(N_s > N_p\))
Transformer Device that changes the voltage of an alternating current supply using mutual induction
Mutual induction Induction of an e.m.f. in one coil due to a changing current in a nearby coil
