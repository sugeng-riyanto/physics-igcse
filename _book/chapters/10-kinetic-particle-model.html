13.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (2.1.1 States of Matter):

  • Know the distinguishing properties of solids, liquids and gases

  • Know the terms for the changes in state between solids, liquids and gases (gas to solid and solid to gas transfers are not required)

Core Content (2.1.2 Particle Model):

  • Describe the particle structure of solids, liquids and gases in terms of the arrangement, separation and motion of the particles and represent these states using simple particle diagrams

  • Describe the relationship between the motion of particles and temperature, including the idea that there is a lowest possible temperature (−273 °C), known as absolute zero, where the particles have least kinetic energy

  • Describe the pressure and the changes in pressure of a gas in terms of the motion of its particles and their collisions with a surface

  • Know that the random motion of microscopic particles in a suspension is evidence for the kinetic particle model of matter

  • Describe and explain this motion (sometimes known as Brownian motion) in terms of random collisions between the microscopic particles in a suspension and the particles of the gas or liquid

Core Content (2.1.3 Gases and the Absolute Scale of Temperature):

  • Describe qualitatively, in terms of particles, the effect on the pressure of a fixed mass of gas of: (a) a change of temperature at constant volume (b) a change of volume at constant temperature

  • Convert temperatures between kelvin and degrees Celsius; recall and use the equation T(in K) = θ(in °C) + 273

Supplement (Extended):

  • Know that the forces and distances between particles (atoms, molecules, ions and electrons) and the motion of the particles affects the properties of solids, liquids and gases

  • Describe the pressure and the changes in pressure of a gas in terms of the forces exerted by particles colliding with surfaces, creating a force per unit area

  • Know that microscopic particles may be moved by collisions with light fast-moving molecules and correctly use the terms atoms or molecules as distinct from microscopic particles

  • Recall and use the equation pV = constant for a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature, including a graphical representation of this relationship

13.2 The Three States of Matter

13.2.1 Distinguishing Properties

All matter exists in three states: solid, liquid, and gas. Each state has distinct properties that arise from the arrangement and motion of its particles.

Property Solid Liquid Gas
Shape Fixed shape No fixed shape; takes shape of container No fixed shape; fills entire container
Volume Fixed volume Fixed volume No fixed volume; expands to fill container
Density High density High density (slightly less than solid) Low density
Compressibility Incompressible Incompressible Compressible
Flow Cannot flow Can flow Can flow
Note

Key Concept: The properties of each state depend on the arrangement, separation, and motion of particles, not on the type of substance itself.

13.2.2 Changes of State

Matter can change from one state to another when energy is added or removed. The main changes of state are:

  • Melting: Solid → Liquid (at melting point)

  • Freezing/Solidifying: Liquid → Solid (at melting point)

  • Boiling: Liquid → Gas (at boiling point)

  • Condensing: Gas → Liquid (at boiling point)

Warning

Important: During a change of state, the temperature remains constant even though energy is being supplied or removed. This energy is used to overcome or form intermolecular forces, not to increase particle kinetic energy.

13.3 The Kinetic Particle Model

13.3.1 What Is the Kinetic Particle Model?

The kinetic particle model of matter states that:

  • All matter is made up of tiny particles (atoms, molecules, or ions)

  • These particles are in continuous random motion

  • The motion and arrangement of particles determine the properties of each state

13.3.2 Particle Arrangement and Motion

State Particle Arrangement Particle Separation Particle Motion Interparticle Forces
Solid Regular, ordered pattern (usually) Very close together Vibrate about fixed positions Very strong
Liquid Random, disordered Slightly further apart than solids Move freely within the liquid, sliding past each other Moderately strong
Gas Random, widely spaced Far apart Move rapidly in all directions, colliding with each other and container walls Negligible
Note

Visual Aid: Simple particle diagrams help represent these states: - Solid: Particles in a grid, closely packed

  • Liquid: Particles close but randomly arranged

  • Gas: Particles far apart, scattered randomly

13.3.3 How Particle Motion Relates to Temperature

Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance.

  • When temperature increases, particles move or vibrate faster (higher average kinetic energy)

  • When temperature decreases, particles move or vibrate slower (lower average kinetic energy)

Absolute Zero:

  • The lowest possible temperature is −273 °C, known as absolute zero

  • At absolute zero, particles have the least possible kinetic energy (they do not stop completely due to quantum effects, but this is beyond IGCSE scope)

  • Absolute zero is the starting point of the Kelvin temperature scale: 0 K = −273 °C

Temperature Conversion:

\[T(\text{in K}) = \theta(\text{in °C}) + 273\]

Examples:

  • 0 °C = 0 + 273 = 273 K

  • 25 °C = 25 + 273 = 298 K

  • 100 °C = 100 + 273 = 373 K

Warning

Important: A change of 1 °C is equal to a change of 1 K. Only the starting points differ.

13.4 Gas Pressure and the Particle Model

13.4.1 How Do Gases Exert Pressure?

Gas pressure is caused by the constant collisions of gas particles with the walls of their container.

Explanation using the particle model:

  1. Gas particles move rapidly in random directions

  2. They collide with the container walls

  3. Each collision exerts a tiny force on the wall

  4. The sum of all these forces per unit area is the gas pressure

\[\text{Pressure} = \frac{\text{Total force from collisions}}{\text{Area of container wall}}\]

13.4.2 Factors Affecting Gas Pressure

For a fixed mass of gas:

Change Effect on Pressure Particle-Level Explanation
Temperature increases (constant volume) Pressure increases Particles move faster → more frequent and more forceful collisions with walls
Volume decreases (constant temperature) Pressure increases Particles have less space → more frequent collisions with walls
Volume increases (constant temperature) Pressure decreases Particles have more space → less frequent collisions with walls
Temperature decreases (constant volume) Pressure decreases Particles move slower → less frequent and less forceful collisions
Note

Key Insight: Pressure depends on the rate and force of particle collisions with the container walls.

13.4.3 Boyle’s Law (Supplement)

For a fixed mass of gas at constant temperature:

\[pV = \text{constant} \quad \text{or} \quad p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2\]

Where: - \(p\) = pressure (Pa) - \(V\) = volume (m³)

Graphical Representation:

  • A graph of \(p\) against \(V\) is a curve (hyperbola)

  • A graph of \(p\) against \(\frac{1}{V}\) is a straight line through the origin, confirming inverse proportionality

13.5 10.4 Brownian Motion: Evidence for the Particle Model

13.5.1 What Is Brownian Motion?

Brownian motion is the random, jerky movement of microscopic particles (e.g., smoke particles, pollen grains) suspended in a fluid (liquid or gas).

Discovery: First observed by botanist Robert Brown in 1827 when studying pollen grains in water.

13.5.2 Explanation Using the Particle Model

Brownian motion occurs because:

  1. The suspended microscopic particles are much larger than the fluid molecules

  2. Fluid molecules (too small to see) move rapidly and randomly

  3. These molecules collide randomly with the suspended particles from all directions

  4. At any instant, the collisions are unbalanced, causing the suspended particle to jerk in a random direction

  5. The continuous, random collisions produce the observed zigzag motion

Note

Key Evidence: Brownian motion provides direct observational evidence that: - Fluids are made of tiny, moving particles

  • These particles are in continuous random motion

  • The motion is random, not directed

13.5.3 Observing Brownian Motion

Experiment: Smoke Cell

Apparatus: - Glass cell containing smoke

  • Microscope

  • Light source

Method: 1. Seal a small amount of smoke in a glass cell

  1. Illuminate the cell with a bright light

  2. Observe through a microscope

Observation: - Smoke particles appear as bright, dancing specks

  • They move in a random, jerky path

  • Larger particles move less vigorously than smaller ones

Conclusion: The random motion of smoke particles is caused by invisible air molecules colliding with them, supporting the kinetic particle model.

13.6 Key Concepts Summary

13.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Three states of matter have distinct properties due to particle arrangement and motion

  2. Changes of state occur at fixed temperatures (melting/boiling points) with energy transfer but no temperature change

  3. Kinetic particle model: Matter is made of tiny particles in continuous random motion

  4. Temperature measures average particle kinetic energy; absolute zero (−273 °C = 0 K) is the theoretical minimum

  5. Gas pressure results from particle collisions with container walls; increases with temperature (constant volume) or decreases with volume (constant temperature)

  6. Brownian motion provides evidence for the particle model: random motion of suspended particles due to collisions with invisible fluid molecules

13.6.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Interparticle forces and distances determine state properties: strong forces and close spacing in solids; weak forces and large spacing in gases

  2. Pressure as force per unit area: Gas pressure arises from the force exerted by many particle collisions per unit area of container wall

  3. Boyle’s Law: For fixed mass at constant temperature, \(pV = \text{constant}\); pressure and volume are inversely proportional

  4. Terminology precision: Distinguish between atoms, molecules, ions (constituents of matter) and microscopic particles (visible under microscope, e.g., smoke particles)

13.7 Common Misconceptions

13.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Particles in a solid do not move”

Incorrect: “Particles in ice are completely still”

Correct:

  • Particles in a solid vibrate about fixed positions

  • They do not move from place to place, but they are not stationary

  • Higher temperature → more vigorous vibration

13.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “During boiling, temperature increases because energy is added”

Incorrect: “When water boils, its temperature keeps rising”

Correct:

  • During a change of state (melting, boiling), temperature remains constant

  • Added energy is used to overcome intermolecular forces, not to increase particle kinetic energy

  • This is why a boiling kettle stays at 100 °C (at standard pressure) until all water has vaporised

13.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Gas particles are stationary until they hit the wall”

Incorrect: “Gas particles only move when they collide”

Correct:

  • Gas particles are in continuous, rapid, random motion at all times

  • Collisions change their direction and speed, but motion never stops (except theoretically at absolute zero)

13.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Brownian motion is the motion of the fluid molecules themselves”

Incorrect: “We see air molecules moving in Brownian motion”

Correct:

  • Brownian motion is the motion of microscopic suspended particles (e.g., smoke), not the fluid molecules

  • Fluid molecules are too small to see; their random collisions cause the visible jerky motion of larger suspended particles

13.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Pressure in a gas is caused by particles pushing on each other”

Incorrect: “Gas pressure comes from particles pushing against each other”

Correct:

  • Gas pressure is caused by particles colliding with the container walls, not with each other

  • Particle-particle collisions redistribute energy but do not directly create pressure on the container

13.7.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Kelvin and Celsius scales have different sized degrees”

Incorrect: “1 K is a bigger change than 1 °C”

Correct:

  • A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C

  • Only the zero points differ: 0 K = −273 °C

  • Conversion: \(T(\text{K}) = \theta(°C) + 273\)

13.7.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “When volume decreases, gas particles get smaller”

Incorrect: “Compressing a gas makes the particles shrink”

Correct:

  • Gas particles themselves do not change size when compressed

  • Compression reduces the space between particles, increasing collision frequency with walls → higher pressure

13.8 Worked Examples

13.8.1 Example 1: Temperature Conversion

Question: Convert the following temperatures: (a) 37 °C to kelvin (b) 300 K to degrees Celsius (c) A gas is heated from 20 °C to 80 °C. What is the change in temperature in kelvin?

Solution:

(a) 37 °C to K: \[T = \theta + 273 = 37 + 273 = \boxed{310 \text{ K}}\]

(b) 300 K to °C: \[\theta = T - 273 = 300 - 273 = \boxed{27 \text{ °C}}\]

(c) Change in temperature: Initial: \(20 \text{ °C} = 293 \text{ K}\) Final: \(80 \text{ °C} = 353 \text{ K}\) Change: \(353 - 293 = \boxed{60 \text{ K}}\)

Note: The change is also \(80 - 20 = 60 \text{ °C}\), confirming that 1 °C change = 1 K change.

13.8.2 Example 2: Gas Pressure and Particle Motion

Question: A fixed mass of gas is sealed in a rigid container. Explain, in terms of particle motion, why the pressure increases when the gas is heated.

Solution:

Step 1: Heating increases the average kinetic energy of gas particles.

Step 2: Particles move faster on average.

Step 3: Faster particles collide with the container walls: - More frequently (higher collision rate)

  • With greater force per collision (higher momentum change)

Step 4: Pressure = total force per unit area from collisions.

Conclusion: Both increased collision frequency and increased force per collision cause the pressure to rise.

13.8.3 Example 3: Boyle’s Law Calculation (Supplement)

Question: A gas occupies 200 cm³ at a pressure of 150 kPa. If the volume is reduced to 120 cm³ at constant temperature, calculate the new pressure.

Solution:

Given: - \(p_1 = 150 \text{ kPa}\) - \(V_1 = 200 \text{ cm}^3\) - \(V_2 = 120 \text{ cm}^3\) - Temperature constant → use \(p_1 V_1 = p_2 V_2\)

Calculation: \[p_2 = \frac{p_1 V_1}{V_2} = \frac{150 \times 200}{120} = \frac{30\,000}{120} = \boxed{250 \text{ kPa}}\]

Check: Volume decreased by factor \(200/120 = 1.67\), so pressure should increase by same factor: \(150 \times 1.67 = 250\) kPa ✓

13.8.4 Example 4: Brownian Motion Explanation

Question: Describe and explain what is observed when smoke particles in air are viewed under a microscope.

Solution:

Observation: - Smoke particles appear as bright specks of light

  • They move in a random, jerky, zigzag path

  • Smaller particles move more vigorously than larger ones

Explanation: 1. Smoke particles are microscopic (visible under microscope) but much larger than air molecules

  1. Air molecules (N₂, O₂) are too small to see but move rapidly and randomly

  2. Air molecules constantly collide with smoke particles from all directions

  3. At any instant, collisions are unbalanced, giving a net force in a random direction

  4. Continuous random collisions produce the observed jerky motion

Conclusion: This Brownian motion provides evidence that air is made of tiny, rapidly moving particles.

13.10 Laboratory Investigation

13.10.1 Experiment 10.1: Observing Brownian Motion

Objective: To observe and explain the random motion of smoke particles as evidence for the kinetic particle model.

Apparatus: - Glass smoke cell with cover slip

  • Microscope (×100 or higher magnification)

  • Bright light source (LED torch or microscope lamp)

  • Dropper with dilute smoke suspension (or incense stick for generating smoke)

Variables: - Independent: None (observational experiment)

  • Dependent: Motion of smoke particles (qualitative description)

  • Controlled: Light intensity, magnification, smoke concentration, temperature

Procedure: 1. Prepare the smoke cell: - If using a commercial cell, ensure it is sealed and contains a small amount of smoke

  • If making your own, gently introduce a tiny amount of smoke into a clean glass cell and seal immediately

  1. Place the smoke cell on the microscope stage

  2. Illuminate the cell from the side or below to make smoke particles visible as bright specks

  3. Focus the microscope to obtain a clear view of individual smoke particles

  4. Observe the motion of several particles for 2–3 minutes

  5. Sketch the path of one particle over a short time interval

  6. Compare the motion of small particles with larger ones

Observations to Record: - Describe the path of a typical smoke particle (e.g., straight, curved, zigzag)

  • Note whether motion is continuous or jerky

  • Compare vigour of motion for particles of different sizes

  • Note any changes when the cell is gently warmed (e.g., by holding in hands)

Analysis: 1. Explain the observed motion using the kinetic particle model: - What causes the jerky motion?

  • Why is the path random?

  • Why do smaller particles move more vigorously?

  1. Relate observations to the existence and motion of invisible air molecules

Precautions: - ✓ Use minimal smoke to avoid obscuring the view

  • ✓ Avoid heating the cell excessively, which could cause convection currents

  • ✓ Handle glass cells carefully to avoid breakage

  • ✓ Do not look directly at bright light sources

Conclusion Template: “The observed random, jerky motion of smoke particles (Brownian motion) is caused by continuous, random collisions with invisible, rapidly moving air molecules. This provides direct evidence that gases are composed of tiny particles in continuous random motion, supporting the kinetic particle model of matter.”

Extension (Supplement): - Investigate the effect of temperature: gently warm the cell and observe changes in particle motion

  • Compare Brownian motion in different gases (if equipment allows)

  • Research historical significance: How did Einstein’s explanation of Brownian motion help confirm the existence of atoms?

13.12 Chapter Summary

13.12.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Three states of matter: Solids (fixed shape/volume), liquids (fixed volume, variable shape), gases (variable shape/volume)

✓ Changes of state: Melting, freezing, boiling, condensing occur at fixed temperatures with energy transfer but no temperature change

✓ Kinetic particle model: Matter is made of tiny particles in continuous random motion; arrangement and motion determine state properties

✓ Temperature and particle motion: Temperature measures average kinetic energy; absolute zero (−273 °C = 0 K) is theoretical minimum

✓ Gas pressure: Caused by particle collisions with container walls; increases with temperature (constant volume) or decreases with volume (constant temperature)

✓ Brownian motion: Random jerky motion of suspended microscopic particles provides evidence for invisible, moving fluid molecules

✓ Boyle’s Law (Extended): For fixed mass at constant temperature, \(pV = \text{constant}\); pressure and volume are inversely proportional

✓ Terminology: Distinguish atoms/molecules (constituents) from microscopic particles (visible suspended particles)
