12.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content:

  • Define pressure as force per unit area; recall and use the equation \(p = \frac{F}{A}\)

  • Describe how pressure varies with force and area in the context of everyday examples

  • Describe, qualitatively, how the pressure beneath the surface of a liquid changes with depth and density of the liquid

Supplement (Extended):

  • Recall and use the equation for the change in pressure beneath the surface of a liquid: \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\)

12.2 What Is Pressure?

12.2.1 Definition of Pressure

In physics:

Pressure is defined as force per unit area.

\[p = \frac{F}{A}\]

Where:

  • \(p\) = pressure (Pa or N/m²)

  • \(F\) = force acting perpendicular to the surface (N)

  • \(A\) = area of contact (m²)

Note

Key Concept: The SI unit of pressure is the pascal (Pa), where \(1 \text{ Pa} = 1 \text{ N/m}^2\). For larger pressures, kilopascals (kPa) or megapascals (MPa) are used.

12.2.2 Understanding Pressure Through Everyday Examples

Situation Explanation
Walking on eggs with sports shoes Wide soles distribute weight over large area → low pressure → eggs do not break
Walking on eggs with high heels Small heel area concentrates force → high pressure → eggs break easily
Tractor with wide tyres Large contact area reduces pressure on soft ground → prevents sinking
Sharp knife cutting Small edge area creates high pressure for same force → cuts easily
Nail with pointed tip Small tip area produces high pressure → penetrates wood with less force
Warning

Important: Pressure depends on both force and area. Increasing force increases pressure; increasing area decreases pressure (for constant force).

12.3 Calculating Pressure: Worked Examples

12.3.1 Example 1: Basic Pressure Calculation

Question:

A woman weighing 600 N stands on the floor. The total area of her shoes in contact with the floor is 0.03 m².

  1. Calculate the pressure she exerts on the floor.

  2. If she balances on one heel with area 0.0003 m², calculate the new pressure.

Solution:

(a) Pressure with both feet:

\[p = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{600}{0.03} = \boxed{20\,000 \text{ Pa} = 2.0 \times 10^4 \text{ Pa}}\]

(b) Pressure on one heel:

\[p = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{600}{0.0003} = \boxed{2\,000\,000 \text{ Pa} = 2.0 \times 10^6 \text{ Pa}}\]

Interpretation: The pressure on one heel is 100 times greater than with both feet, explaining why high heels can damage floors.

12.3.2 Example 2: Finding Force or Area from Pressure

Question:

  1. A polystyrene cube exerts a pressure of 89 N/m² on a horizontal surface. If the cube has mass 5.0 kg, calculate the area of contact. (Take \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\))

  2. A force of 1200 N produces a pressure of 50 000 Pa. Calculate the area over which the force acts.

Solution:

(a) Area of contact:

Weight \(F = mg = 5.0 \times 10 = 50 \text{ N}\)

\[A = \frac{F}{p} = \frac{50}{89} = \boxed{0.56 \text{ m}^2}\]

(b) Area from pressure:

\[A = \frac{F}{p} = \frac{1200}{50\,000} = \boxed{0.024 \text{ m}^2}\]

12.3.3 Example 3: Pressure from a Rectangular Block

Question:

A block of dimensions 20 cm × 10 cm × 5 cm has density 2.0 g/cm³.

  1. Calculate the weight of the block. (Take \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\))

  2. On which face must the block rest to exert the greatest pressure? Calculate this pressure.

  3. On which face must the block rest to exert the least pressure? Calculate this pressure.

Solution:

(a) Weight of block:

Volume \(V = 20 \times 10 \times 5 = 1000 \text{ cm}^3\)

Mass \(m = \rho V = 2.0 \times 1000 = 2000 \text{ g} = 2.0 \text{ kg}\)

Weight \(F = mg = 2.0 \times 10 = \boxed{20 \text{ N}}\)

(b) Greatest pressure (smallest area):

Smallest face area: \(5 \text{ cm} \times 10 \text{ cm} = 50 \text{ cm}^2 = 0.005 \text{ m}^2\)

\[p_{\text{max}} = \frac{F}{A} = \frac{20}{0.005} = \boxed{4000 \text{ Pa}}\]

(c) Least pressure (largest area):

Largest face area: \(20 \text{ cm} \times 10 \text{ cm} = 200 \text{ cm}^2 = 0.02 \text{ m}^2\)

\[p_{\text{min}} = \frac{20}{0.02} = \boxed{1000 \text{ Pa}}\]

12.4 Pressure in Liquids

12.4.1 How Depth Affects Liquid Pressure

The pressure beneath the surface of a liquid increases with depth because:

  • There is more liquid above pushing down

  • The weight of the liquid column increases with depth

Demonstration: A container with holes at different depths shows water spurting furthest from the bottom hole, indicating greatest pressure at greatest depth.

12.4.2 How Density Affects Liquid Pressure

For the same depth:

  • A denser liquid exerts greater pressure because it has more mass per unit volume

  • Example: Seawater (\(\rho \approx 1025 \text{ kg/m}^3\)) exerts more pressure than freshwater (\(\rho \approx 1000 \text{ kg/m}^3\)) at the same depth

12.4.3 Calculating Change in Liquid Pressure (Extended)

The change in pressure beneath the surface of a liquid is given by:

\[\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\]

Where:

  • \(\Delta p\) = change in pressure (Pa)

  • \(\rho\) = density of liquid (kg/m³)

  • \(g\) = gravitational field strength (N/kg); on Earth, \(g \approx 10 \text{ N/kg}\)

  • \(\Delta h\) = change in depth (m)

Note

Key Insight: This equation shows that liquid pressure depends only on density and depth, not on the shape or total volume of the container.

12.5 9.4 Worked Examples: Liquid Pressure

12.5.1 Example 4: Pressure Change with Depth

Question:

A submarine descends from the surface of the sea to a depth of 3000 m. The density of seawater is 1030 kg/m³ and \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\).

  1. Calculate the change in pressure experienced by the submarine.

  2. If the pressure on the submarine changes by 0.10 MPa, calculate the change in depth.

Solution:

(a) Change in pressure:

\[\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h = 1030 \times 10 \times 3000 = \boxed{3.09 \times 10^7 \text{ Pa} = 30.9 \text{ MPa}}\]

(b) Change in depth:

\[\Delta h = \frac{\Delta p}{\rho g} = \frac{0.10 \times 10^6}{1030 \times 10} = \boxed{9.7 \text{ m}}\]

Warning

Note: The actual pressure on the submarine is the sum of liquid pressure plus atmospheric pressure at the surface (~101 kPa).

12.5.2 Example 5: Comparing Pressures in Different Liquids

Question:

Two identical containers are filled with liquids X and Y to the same height. The pressure at the bottom of container Y is greater than at the bottom of container X. What can you conclude about the densities of the liquids?

Solution:

Since \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\) and \(g\) and \(\Delta h\) are the same for both containers:

  • Greater pressure at bottom of Y → greater density of Y

\[\therefore \boxed{\rho_Y > \rho_X}\]

12.5.3 Example 6: Pressure at Different Points in Same Liquid

Question:

Figure 9.1 shows a container of water with points A, B, and C at different depths. Point A is at the surface, B is at depth \(h\), and C is at depth \(2h\).

  1. Compare the pressures at A, B, and C.

  2. If the pressure at B is 5000 Pa, what is the pressure at C?

Solution:

(a) Pressure comparison:

  • Pressure increases with depth: \(p_C > p_B > p_A\)

  • Since \(p \propto \Delta h\), and depth of C is twice depth of B: \(p_C \approx 2p_B\) (ignoring atmospheric pressure)

(b) Pressure at C:

If \(p_B = 5000 \text{ Pa}\) (gauge pressure), then:

\[p_C = \rho g (2h) = 2(\rho g h) = 2 \times 5000 = \boxed{10\,000 \text{ Pa}}\]

12.6 Key Concepts Summary

12.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Pressure = force per unit area: \(p = \frac{F}{A}\); unit: pascal (Pa) = N/m²

  2. Pressure varies inversely with area: For constant force, smaller area → greater pressure

  3. Pressure varies directly with force: For constant area, greater force → greater pressure

  4. Liquid pressure increases with depth: More liquid above → greater weight → greater pressure

  5. Liquid pressure increases with density: Denser liquid → greater mass per volume → greater pressure at same depth

12.6.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Change in liquid pressure: \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\); depends only on density and depth change

  2. Pressure is isotropic in liquids: At a given depth, pressure acts equally in all directions

  3. Total pressure = atmospheric pressure + liquid pressure (for open containers)

12.7 Common Misconceptions

12.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Pressure and force are the same thing”

Incorrect: “A large force always means large pressure”

Correct:

  • Force is a push or pull (unit: N)

  • Pressure is force per unit area (unit: Pa = N/m²)

  • A small force on a very small area can produce very high pressure (e.g., needle piercing skin)

12.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Liquid pressure depends on the total amount of liquid”

Incorrect: “A wide tank has more pressure at the bottom than a narrow tube at the same depth”

Correct:

  • Liquid pressure depends only on depth and density, not on container shape or total volume

  • This is why dams are thicker at the bottom (greater pressure) regardless of lake size

12.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Pressure acts only downwards in liquids”

Incorrect: “Water pressure only pushes down on objects”

Correct:

  • Pressure in a liquid acts equally in all directions at a given depth

  • This is why submarines experience pressure on all sides, not just the top

12.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Atmospheric pressure is negligible compared to liquid pressure”

Incorrect: “We can ignore air pressure when calculating underwater pressure”

Correct:

  • Atmospheric pressure (~101 kPa) is significant and adds to liquid pressure

  • Total pressure at depth = atmospheric pressure + \(\rho g \Delta h\)

12.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Denser objects always sink because they experience more pressure”

Incorrect: “High pressure makes objects sink”

Correct:

  • Whether an object floats or sinks depends on density comparison, not pressure magnitude

  • Pressure increases with depth for all objects, but buoyant force depends on displaced fluid weight

12.7.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Pressure in a liquid is the same at all depths”

Incorrect: “Water pressure is constant throughout a swimming pool”

Correct:

  • Pressure increases linearly with depth: \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\)

  • Divers feel increasing pressure on their ears as they descend

12.9 Laboratory Investigation

12.9.1 Experiment 9.1: Investigating Pressure in Liquids

Objective:

To investigate how pressure in a liquid varies with depth.

Apparatus:

  • Tall transparent container (e.g., measuring cylinder or plastic bottle)

  • Water

  • Three small holes drilled at different heights (e.g., 5 cm, 10 cm, 15 cm from bottom)

  • Ruler

  • Tray to catch water

  • Marker pen

Variables:

  • Independent: Depth of hole below liquid surface (\(\Delta h\))

  • Dependent: Horizontal distance water spurts (\(d\)) as indicator of pressure

  • Controlled: Liquid density, hole diameter, container shape, water level maintained

Procedure:

  1. Drill three small holes of equal diameter at different heights on one side of the container.

  2. Place the container in a tray and fill with water to a fixed level above the highest hole.

  3. Quickly remove tape covering the holes and observe the water jets.

  4. Measure the horizontal distance each jet travels before hitting the tray.

  5. Repeat three times for reliability.

  6. Record depth of each hole below water surface and corresponding jet distance.

Data Table:

Hole Depth below surface \(\Delta h\) (cm) Jet distance \(d_1\) (cm) \(d_2\) (cm) \(d_3\) (cm) Average \(d\) (cm)
A 5.0
B 10.0
C 15.0

Analysis:

  1. Plot a graph of average jet distance \(d\) (y-axis) against depth \(\Delta h\) (x-axis).

  2. Describe the relationship observed.

  3. Explain how jet distance relates to pressure at each depth.

Precautions:

  • ✓ Ensure holes are of equal diameter for fair comparison

  • ✓ Maintain constant water level during observations

  • ✓ Measure jet distance from hole exit point horizontally

  • ✓ Perform experiment quickly to minimise water level change

Expected Results:

  • Jet from lowest hole (greatest depth) travels furthest horizontally

  • Jet distance increases with depth, indicating pressure increases with depth

Conclusion Template:

“The experiment demonstrated that pressure in a liquid increases with depth. The horizontal distance travelled by water jets increased as the depth of the hole below the surface increased, confirming that greater depth produces greater pressure.”

Extension (Supplement):

  • Repeat with salt water to investigate effect of density on pressure

  • Use a pressure sensor to obtain quantitative pressure readings at different depths

12.11 Chapter Summary

12.11.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Pressure = force per unit area: \(p = \frac{F}{A}\); unit: pascal (Pa) = N/m²

✓ Pressure ∝ force: For constant area, greater force → greater pressure

✓ Pressure ∝ 1/area: For constant force, smaller area → greater pressure

✓ Liquid pressure increases with depth: \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\)

✓ Liquid pressure increases with density: At same depth, denser liquid → greater pressure

✓ Pressure acts equally in all directions at a given point in a liquid

✓ Total pressure = atmospheric pressure + liquid pressure (for open systems)

12.11.2 Formulae Sheet

Quantity Formula Variables
Pressure \(p = \frac{F}{A}\) \(p\)=pressure (Pa), \(F\)=force (N), \(A\)=area (m²)
Change in liquid pressure \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\) \(\rho\)=density (kg/m³), \(g\)=field strength (N/kg), \(\Delta h\)=depth change (m)
Weight \(W = mg\) \(W\)=weight (N), \(m\)=mass (kg), \(g\)=field strength (N/kg)

12.13 Glossary

Term Definition
Atmospheric pressure Pressure exerted by the weight of air above a point; approximately 101 kPa at sea level
Density (\(\rho\)) Mass per unit volume; \(\rho = m/V\); unit: kg/m³
Hydraulic system Device that transmits pressure through a liquid to multiply force
Pascal (Pa) SI unit of pressure; 1 Pa = 1 N/m²
Pressure (\(p\)) Force per unit area acting perpendicular to a surface; \(p = F/A\); unit: Pa
Pressure in liquids Increases with depth and density; given by \(\Delta p = \rho g \Delta h\)
Total pressure Sum of atmospheric pressure and liquid pressure at a point in an open container
