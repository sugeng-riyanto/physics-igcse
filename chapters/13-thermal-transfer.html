16.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (2.3.1 Conduction):

  • Describe experiments to demonstrate the properties of good thermal conductors and bad thermal conductors (thermal insulators)

Core Content (2.3.2 Convection):

  • Know that convection is an important method of thermal energy transfer in liquids and gases

  • Explain convection in liquids and gases in terms of density changes and describe experiments to illustrate convection

Core Content (2.3.3 Radiation):

  • Know that thermal radiation is infrared radiation and that all objects emit this radiation

  • Know that thermal energy transfer by thermal radiation does not require a medium

  • Describe the effect of surface colour (black or white) and texture (dull or shiny) on the emission, absorption and reflection of infrared radiation

  • Describe experiments to distinguish between good and bad emitters of infrared radiation

  • Describe experiments to distinguish between good and bad absorbers of infrared radiation

  • Describe how the rate of emission of radiation depends on the surface temperature and surface area of an object

Core Content (2.3.4 Consequences of thermal energy transfer):

  • Explain some of the basic everyday applications and consequences of conduction, convection and radiation, including heating objects such as kitchen pans and heating a room by convection

Supplement (Extended):

  • Describe thermal conduction in all solids in terms of atomic or molecular lattice vibrations and also in terms of the movement of free (delocalised) electrons in metallic conductors

  • Describe, in terms of particles, why thermal conduction is bad in gases and most liquids

  • Know that there are many solids that conduct thermal energy better than thermal insulators but do so less well than good thermal conductors

  • Know that for an object to be at a constant temperature it needs to transfer energy away from the object at the same rate that it receives energy

  • Know what happens to an object if the rate at which it receives energy is less or more than the rate at which it transfers energy away from the object

  • Know how the temperature of the Earth is affected by factors controlling the balance between incoming radiation and radiation emitted from the Earth’s surface

  • Explain some of the complex applications and consequences of conduction, convection and radiation where more than one type of thermal energy transfer is significant, including a fire burning wood or coal and a radiator in a car

16.2 Introduction to Thermal Energy Transfer

16.2.1 Why Does Thermal Energy Transfer Occur?

Thermal energy always flows from a region of higher temperature to a region of lower temperature.

  • When you touch a hot object, thermal energy flows from the object to your hand → your hand feels hot

  • When you touch a cold object, thermal energy flows from your hand to the object → your hand feels cold

  • When two objects are at the same temperature, there is no net flow of thermal energy between them → they are in thermal equilibrium

Note

Key Concept: Thermal energy transfer continues until thermal equilibrium is reached (both objects at the same temperature).

16.2.2 Three Methods of Thermal Energy Transfer

Method Medium Required How Energy Is Transferred
Conduction Solid (best), liquid, gas Through particle vibrations and free electron movement
Convection Fluid (liquid or gas) only Through bulk movement of fluid due to density differences
Radiation None (can travel through vacuum) Through electromagnetic waves (infrared radiation)

16.3 Conduction

16.3.1 What Is Conduction?

Conduction is the transfer of thermal energy through a substance from a hotter region to a colder region without any bulk movement of the substance itself.

  • Conduction occurs mainly in solids

  • Metals are good thermal conductors; non-metals are generally poor thermal conductors (thermal insulators)

16.3.2 Comparing Thermal Conductors: An Experiment

Apparatus:

  • Four rods of equal dimensions made of copper, steel, aluminium, and glass

  • Bunsen burner, tripod stand, stopwatch

  • Wax, drawing pins

Procedure:

  1. Attach a drawing pin to one end of each rod using a small amount of wax

  2. Heat the opposite ends of all rods simultaneously with a Bunsen burner

  3. Record the time taken for each drawing pin to fall

Observation:

  • The pin on the copper rod falls first → copper conducts thermal energy fastest

  • The pin on the glass rod falls last (or not at all) → glass is a poor conductor

Conclusion:

Different materials conduct thermal energy at different rates. Metals conduct better than non-metals.

16.3.3 Thermal Conductivity of Common Materials

Material Thermal Conductivity Classification
Diamond Very high Best conductor
Copper High Good conductor
Aluminium High Good conductor
Steel Moderate Moderate conductor
Ice Low Poor conductor
Glass Low Poor conductor (insulator)
Water Very low Poor conductor (insulator)
Air Extremely low Best insulator
Polystyrene Extremely low Best insulator
Warning

Important: Still air is an excellent thermal insulator. Many insulating materials (e.g., fibreglass, polystyrene) work by trapping air in small pockets.

16.3.4 How Conduction Works: Particle Explanation (Extended)

16.3.4.1 In Non-Metals

  1. Particles at the hot end vibrate more vigorously about their fixed positions

  2. These vibrating particles collide with neighbouring particles, transferring kinetic energy

  3. The process continues along the material → thermal energy is transferred from hot to cold

  4. No particles move from one place to another; only energy is transferred

16.3.4.2 In Metals

Metals have an additional mechanism that makes them excellent conductors:

  1. Metals contain free (delocalised) electrons that can move freely throughout the material

  2. At the hot end, free electrons gain kinetic energy and move rapidly towards cooler regions

  3. These fast-moving electrons collide with atoms in cooler regions, transferring energy

  4. Free electron diffusion transfers energy much faster than lattice vibrations alone

Note

Key Insight: This is why metals are good conductors of both thermal energy and electricity — both processes rely on free electron movement.

16.3.5 Why Are Liquids and Gases Poor Conductors?

  • Particles in liquids and gases are farther apart than in solids

  • Collisions between particles are less frequent

  • Transfer of kinetic energy from hot to cold regions is slower

  • Therefore, conduction is inefficient in fluids

Warning

Important: Although conduction is poor in fluids, thermal energy can still be transferred efficiently by convection (see Section 13.3).

16.4 Convection

16.4.1 What Is Convection?

Convection is the transfer of thermal energy in a fluid (liquid or gas) by means of convection currents due to differences in density.

  • Convection cannot occur in solids because particles cannot move freely

  • Convection involves the bulk movement of the fluid itself

16.4.2 How Convection Currents Form

Step-by-step explanation:

  1. Fluid at the bottom is heated → particles gain kinetic energy and move faster

  2. Heated fluid expands → same mass occupies larger volume → density decreases

  3. Less dense (warmer) fluid rises because it is lighter than surrounding cooler fluid

  4. Cooler, denser fluid sinks to replace the rising warm fluid

  5. The cycle repeats → convection current is established

16.4.3 Demonstrating Convection in Liquids

Apparatus:

  • Beaker of water, Bunsen burner, potassium permanganate crystals

Procedure:

  1. Place a few potassium permanganate crystals at the bottom of a beaker of water

  2. Gently heat the beaker from below

Observation:

  • Purple streams rise from the crystals, fan out at the surface, then sink down the sides

  • This shows the circular motion of convection currents

16.4.4 Demonstrating Convection in Gases

Apparatus:

  • Convection box with two chimneys, candle, incense stick

Procedure:

  1. Light a candle under one chimney

  2. Hold a smoking incense stick over the other chimney

Observation:

  • Smoke is drawn down the cool chimney, across the box, and rises above the candle

  • This traces the convection current in air

16.4.5 Everyday Examples of Convection

Example Explanation
Sea breeze (daytime) Land heats faster than sea → warm air over land rises → cool air from sea flows in
Land breeze (night-time) Land cools faster than sea → warm air over sea rises → cool air from land flows out
Electric kettle Heating element at bottom → warm water rises → convection currents heat water evenly
Air conditioner Installed near ceiling → cool air sinks → warm air rises to be cooled → room cools evenly
Hot water radiator Heats air nearby → warm air rises → cool air drawn in → convection circulates warm air
Note

Key Insight: Convection is the dominant method of thermal energy transfer in fluids because it transfers energy much faster than conduction alone.

16.5 Radiation

16.5.1 What Is Thermal Radiation?

Thermal radiation (also called infrared radiation) is the transfer of thermal energy in the form of electromagnetic waves.

  • All objects emit and absorb infrared radiation

  • Thermal radiation does not require a medium → it can travel through a vacuum

  • The Earth receives thermal energy from the Sun only by radiation (space is a vacuum)

16.5.2 Detecting Infrared Radiation

  • Infrared radiation is invisible to the human eye

  • Infrared cameras can detect and visualise infrared radiation

  • Warmer objects appear brighter (or in warmer colours) on infrared images

16.5.3 Factors Affecting Emission and Absorption of Infrared Radiation

16.5.3.1 1. Surface Colour and Texture

Surface Type Emission Absorption Reflection
Dull, black Good emitter Good absorber Poor reflector
Shiny, silver Poor emitter Poor absorber Good reflector

Experiment: Comparing Emission

  • Fill two identical tins (one dull black, one shiny silver) with hot water

  • Record temperature decrease over time

  • Result: Dull black tin cools faster → better emitter of infrared radiation

Experiment: Comparing Absorption

  • Place two temperature sensors (one wrapped in dull black foil, one in shiny silver foil) at equal distance from a heat source

  • Record temperature increase over time

  • Result: Dull black foil heats faster → better absorber of infrared radiation

16.5.3.2 2. Surface Temperature

  • The higher the temperature of an object (relative to surroundings), the higher the rate of emission of infrared radiation

  • As an object cools, its rate of emission decreases

16.5.3.3 3. Surface Area

  • For objects of the same mass and material, the object with larger surface area emits or absorbs infrared radiation at a higher rate

16.5.4 Thermal Equilibrium and Radiation

All objects continuously emit and absorb infrared radiation. The net effect determines whether the object warms, cools, or stays at constant temperature:

Situation Absorption vs. Emission Result
Object cooler than surroundings Absorption > Emission Object warms up
Object hotter than surroundings Emission > Absorption Object cools down
Object same temperature as surroundings Absorption = Emission Object stays at constant temperature (thermal equilibrium)

16.5.5 The Greenhouse Effect and Earth’s Temperature (Extended)

How the greenhouse effect works:

  1. Solar radiation (visible light, UV) passes through the atmosphere and warms the Earth’s surface

  2. The Earth emits infrared radiation back towards space

  3. Greenhouse gases (CO₂, methane, water vapour) in the atmosphere absorb and re-emit some of this infrared radiation

  4. Some of this re-emitted radiation returns to Earth → additional warming

Natural greenhouse effect:

  • Keeps Earth’s average temperature at about 15°C (instead of −18°C without it)

  • Essential for life as we know it

Enhanced greenhouse effect (global warming):

  • Human activities increase greenhouse gas concentrations

  • More infrared radiation is trapped → Earth’s temperature rises

Warning

Important: The greenhouse effect is a natural and necessary process. The problem is the enhancement of this effect due to human activities.

16.6 Applications and Consequences of Thermal Energy Transfer

16.6.1 Applications of Conduction

Application Explanation
Cooking utensils Metal pans conduct heat quickly from stove to food; handles made of wood/plastic (insulators) protect hands
Soldering irons Copper tip conducts heat rapidly to melt solder for electronic repairs
Heat sinks Metal fins with large surface area conduct heat away from electronic components

16.6.2 Applications of Convection

Application Explanation
Room heating/cooling Radiators heat air → warm air rises → convection circulates warmth; air conditioners cool air → cool air sinks → convection circulates cool air
Refrigerators Freezing compartment at top → cool air sinks → convection currents cool the entire fridge
Weather systems Convection currents in atmosphere drive wind patterns, cloud formation, and weather

16.6.3 Applications of Radiation

Application Explanation
Solar panels Blackened surfaces absorb infrared radiation from Sun to heat water
Space blankets Shiny silver surfaces reflect infrared radiation back to body → reduce heat loss
Infrared thermometers Detect infrared radiation emitted by objects to measure temperature without contact

16.6.4 Complex Applications Involving Multiple Methods

16.6.4.1 Example 1: Cooking Over a Campfire

  • Conduction: Heat transfers from fire to metal pot; from pot to food

  • Convection: Hot air and smoke rise above fire; convection currents heat water inside pot

  • Radiation: Infrared radiation from flames warms hands and surroundings

16.6.4.2 Example 2: Car Radiator Cooling System

  • Conduction: Hot coolant transfers heat through metal tubes to fins

  • Convection: Air blown by fan carries heat away from fins; coolant circulates through engine

  • Radiation: Black-painted fins emit infrared radiation to surroundings

16.7 Key Concepts Summary

16.7.1 Core Concepts

  1. Thermal energy flows from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium is reached

  2. Conduction: Transfer through solids via particle vibrations and (in metals) free electron movement

  3. Convection: Transfer in fluids via density-driven currents; warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks

  4. Radiation: Transfer via infrared electromagnetic waves; requires no medium; all objects emit and absorb

  5. Surface properties: Dull black surfaces are good emitters/absorbers; shiny silver surfaces are good reflectors

  6. Rate of radiation emission depends on surface temperature and surface area

  7. Thermal equilibrium: Object temperature constant when absorption rate = emission rate

16.7.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Conduction mechanisms: Lattice vibrations (all solids) + free electron diffusion (metals only)

  2. Poor conduction in fluids: Due to large particle separation and infrequent collisions

  3. Greenhouse effect: Natural process that warms Earth; enhanced by human activities → global warming

  4. Complex applications: Real-world systems often involve multiple transfer methods simultaneously

16.8 Common Misconceptions

16.8.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Heat rises”

Incorrect: “Heat always moves upwards”

Correct: - Thermal energy flows from hot to cold, regardless of direction - Warm fluids rise because they are less dense, but this is convection, not heat itself rising - In conduction and radiation, energy can flow in any direction from hot to cold

16.8.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Metals feel colder because they are at a lower temperature”

Incorrect: “A metal spoon is colder than a plastic spoon at room temperature”

Correct: - Both objects are at the same temperature (room temperature) - Metal conducts thermal energy away from your hand faster than plastic - Your hand loses heat more quickly to metal → feels colder - This is about rate of energy transfer, not actual temperature

16.8.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Convection can occur in solids”

Incorrect: “Solids can transfer heat by convection if heated enough”

Correct: - Convection requires bulk movement of fluid - Particles in solids are fixed in position and cannot flow - Solids transfer thermal energy only by conduction (and radiation from their surface)

16.8.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Black objects are always hotter”

Incorrect: “A black car is always hotter than a white car in the Sun”

Correct: - Black surfaces absorb infrared radiation better → heat up faster in sunlight - But black surfaces also emit infrared radiation better → cool down faster in shade - The final temperature depends on the balance of absorption and emission, and the environment

16.8.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Radiation only occurs at high temperatures”

Incorrect: “Only hot objects emit infrared radiation”

Correct: - All objects with temperature above absolute zero emit infrared radiation - Cooler objects emit less radiation and at longer wavelengths - Your body, a cup of tea, and even ice cubes emit infrared radiation

16.8.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “The greenhouse effect is bad”

Incorrect: “The greenhouse effect causes global warming and should be eliminated”

Correct: - The natural greenhouse effect is essential for life on Earth - Without it, Earth’s average temperature would be about −18°C - The problem is the enhanced greenhouse effect due to increased greenhouse gases from human activities

16.8.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “Still air is a good conductor”

Incorrect: “Air conducts heat well, so it’s not useful for insulation”

Correct: - Still air is actually an excellent thermal insulator (very poor conductor) - Insulating materials (fibreglass, double-glazed windows) work by trapping air in small pockets - Moving air (wind) transfers heat by convection, but still air minimises all three transfer methods

16.9 Worked Examples

16.9.1 Example 1: Conduction Calculation (Extended)

Question: A copper rod of length 0.5 m and cross-sectional area 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ m² has one end at 100°C and the other at 20°C. The thermal conductivity of copper is 400 W/(m·K). Calculate the rate of thermal energy transfer through the rod.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify the formula for rate of conduction: \[\frac{Q}{t} = kA\frac{\Delta T}{L}\]

Step 2: Substitute values: - \(k = 400 \text{ W/(m·K)}\) - \(A = 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \text{ m}^2\) - \(\Delta T = 100 - 20 = 80 \text{ K}\) - \(L = 0.5 \text{ m}\)

\[\frac{Q}{t} = 400 \times (2.0 \times 10^{-4}) \times \frac{80}{0.5}\] \[\frac{Q}{t} = 400 \times 2.0 \times 10^{-4} \times 160 = \boxed{12.8 \text{ W}}\]

Interpretation: Thermal energy flows through the rod at 12.8 joules per second.

16.9.2 Example 2: Convection Current Explanation

Question: Explain why an electric kettle has its heating element at the bottom, not the top.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify the transfer method: convection in water

Step 2: Explain the process: - Heating element at bottom heats water nearby - Heated water expands → density decreases → rises - Cooler, denser water sinks to replace it - Convection current circulates water → entire kettle heats evenly

Step 3: Contrast with top heating: - If element were at top, heated water would stay at top (already least dense) - No convection current would form - Water at bottom would heat only by slow conduction → inefficient heating

Conclusion: Bottom placement enables efficient heating via convection currents.

16.9.3 Example 3: Radiation and Surface Properties

Question: Two identical metal cans, one painted dull black and one shiny silver, are filled with hot water at 80°C and left in a room at 20°C. After 10 minutes, the black can is at 55°C and the silver can is at 65°C. Explain the difference.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify the dominant transfer method: radiation (and some convection) from can surface to room

Step 2: Apply surface property knowledge: - Dull black surface: good emitter of infrared radiation → loses thermal energy faster - Shiny silver surface: poor emitter (good reflector) → loses thermal energy slower

Step 3: Relate to observations: - Black can cooled more (80°C → 55°C = 25°C drop) - Silver can cooled less (80°C → 65°C = 15°C drop) - Difference due to different emission rates

Conclusion: Surface colour and texture significantly affect the rate of thermal energy loss by radiation.

16.9.4 Example 4: Thermal Equilibrium (Extended)

Question: A cup of coffee at 70°C is left in a room at 20°C. Describe what happens to the rate of cooling over time.

Solution:

Step 1: Initial state: - Coffee much hotter than room → large temperature difference - High rate of emission of infrared radiation - High rate of convection from coffee surface to air - Coffee cools rapidly at first

Step 2: As coffee cools: - Temperature difference decreases - Rate of emission decreases (emission ∝ temperature difference) - Rate of convection decreases - Coffee cools more slowly as it approaches room temperature

Step 3: Final state: - Coffee reaches 20°C (same as room) - Absorption rate = emission rate - No net thermal energy transfer - Coffee stays at constant temperature (thermal equilibrium)

Conclusion: Rate of cooling decreases over time as temperature difference decreases.

16.10 Practice Questions

2. Explain why a metal spoon in a hot cup of tea feels hot at the handle end, even though only the bowl end is in the tea.

3. Describe how convection currents form in a liquid when it is heated from below.

4. Why can thermal radiation travel through a vacuum, but conduction and convection cannot?

16.11 Laboratory Investigation

16.11.1 Experiment 13.1: Comparing Thermal Conductors

Objective: To compare the thermal conductivity of different materials and identify good conductors and insulators.

Apparatus: - Four rods of equal dimensions: copper, aluminium, steel, glass - Bunsen burner, tripod stand, heatproof mat - Stopwatch - Wax, drawing pins or small paper clips - Safety goggles, heat-resistant gloves

Variables: - Independent: Material of rod - Dependent: Time taken for drawing pin to fall - Controlled: Rod dimensions, amount of wax, distance from flame, initial temperature

Procedure: 1. Attach a drawing pin to one end of each rod using a small, equal amount of melted wax

  1. Set up the rods on a tripod stand so that the ends without pins are aligned

  2. Place a Bunsen burner under the aligned ends (pins at opposite end)

  3. Light the burner and start the stopwatch simultaneously

  4. Record the time taken for each drawing pin to fall

  5. Repeat the experiment twice for reliability

  6. Calculate average time for each material

Data Table:

Material Time 1 (s) Time 2 (s) Time 3 (s) Average Time (s) Rank (1 = fastest)
Copper
Aluminium
Steel
Glass

Analysis: 1. Rank materials from best to worst conductor based on average time 2. Explain the ranking in terms of material properties (metal vs. non-metal) 3. Suggest why copper and aluminium are commonly used in cooking utensils

Precautions: - ✓ Wear safety goggles and heat-resistant gloves - ✓ Ensure rods are securely clamped to prevent falling - ✓ Keep flammable materials away from Bunsen burner - ✓ Allow rods to cool completely before handling

16.13 Chapter Summary

16.13.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Thermal energy flows from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium
✓ Conduction: Transfer through solids via particle vibrations + free electrons (metals)
✓ Convection: Transfer in fluids via density-driven currents; warm rises, cool sinks
✓ Radiation: Transfer via infrared waves; no medium needed; all objects emit/absorb
✓ Surface properties: Dull black = good emitter/absorber; shiny silver = good reflector
✓ Emission rate depends on surface temperature and surface area
✓ Thermal equilibrium: Absorption rate = emission rate → constant temperature
✓ Greenhouse effect: Natural process that warms Earth; enhanced by human activities
✓ Applications: Cooking (conduction), room heating (convection), solar panels (radiation)

16.15 Glossary

Term Definition
Conduction Transfer of thermal energy through a substance from hot to cold without bulk movement of the substance
Convection Transfer of thermal energy in a fluid by means of convection currents due to density differences
Convection current Circular movement of fluid caused by heating from below: warm fluid rises, cool fluid sinks
Emission Process by which an object gives out infrared radiation
Free electron Electron in a metal that is not bound to a specific atom and can move freely through the material
Greenhouse effect Natural process where greenhouse gases in the atmosphere absorb and re-emit infrared radiation, warming the Earth
Infrared radiation Electromagnetic radiation emitted by all objects with temperature above absolute zero; carries thermal energy
Lattice vibration Vibration of atoms or molecules about fixed positions in a solid; mechanism of conduction in non-metals
Radiation Transfer of thermal energy by electromagnetic waves (infrared); requires no medium
Thermal conductor Material that transfers thermal energy easily (e.g., metals)
Thermal equilibrium State where two objects at the same temperature have no net flow of thermal energy between them
Thermal insulator Material that transfers thermal energy poorly (e.g., wood, plastic, air)
Thermal radiation Another term for infrared radiation; transfer of thermal energy by electromagnetic waves
