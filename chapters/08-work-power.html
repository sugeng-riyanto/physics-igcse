11.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (1.7.2 Work):

  • Understand that mechanical or electrical work done is equal to the energy transferred

  • Recall and use the equation for mechanical working: \(W = Fd = \Delta E\)

Core Content (1.7.4 Power):

  • Define power as work done per unit time and also as energy transferred per unit time

  • Recall and use the equations:

    • \(P = \frac{W}{t}\)

    • \(P = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\)

11.2 What Is Work?

11.2.1 Definition of Work in Physics

In everyday language, we use the word “work” to describe any activity that requires effort. However, in physics, work has a precise scientific meaning.

Work is done when a force causes an object to move in the direction of the force.

The amount of work done depends on two factors:

  1. The magnitude of the force applied (in newtons, N)

  2. The distance moved by the object in the direction of the force (in metres, m)

11.2.2 The Work–Energy Relationship

A fundamental principle in physics states:

Mechanical or electrical work done is equal to the energy transferred.

\[W = \Delta E\]

Where: - \(W\) = work done (J) - \(\Delta E\) = energy transferred (J)

This means that when work is done on an object, energy is transferred to that object. Conversely, when an object does work, it transfers energy to something else.

11.2.3 Calculating Work Done

The work done by a constant force is calculated using:

\[W = F \times d\]

Where: - \(W\) = work done (J) - \(F\) = constant force in the direction of motion (N) - \(d\) = distance moved in the direction of the force (m)

Note

Key Concept: One joule (1 J) is defined as the work done when a force of one newton moves an object through a distance of one metre in the direction of the force:

\[1 \text{ J} = 1 \text{ N} \times 1 \text{ m}\]

11.2.4 When Is Work NOT Done?

Work is not done in the following situations:

Situation Explanation
Force applied but no movement Pushing against a wall that does not move: \(d = 0\), so \(W = 0\)
Movement perpendicular to force Carrying a bag horizontally: the upward force is perpendicular to horizontal motion, so no work is done by that force
Object moving at constant velocity with balanced forces The net work done by the resultant force is zero (though individual forces may do work)
Warning

Common Error: Confusing “effort” with “work”. Holding a heavy object stationary requires effort but does no work in the physics sense because there is no displacement.

11.3 Worked Examples: Calculating Work

11.3.1 Example 1: Basic Work Calculation

Question: A librarian pushes a trolley of books with a horizontal force of 8 N. The trolley moves a distance of 5 m in the direction of the force. Calculate: (a) the work done on the trolley (b) the energy transferred to the trolley

Solution:

(a) Work done: \[W = F \times d = 8 \times 5 = \boxed{40 \text{ J}}\]

(b) Energy transferred: By the work–energy relationship: \[\Delta E = W = \boxed{40 \text{ J}}\]

Interpretation: The 40 J of mechanical work done by the librarian is transferred into kinetic energy of the trolley (and some thermal energy due to friction).

11.3.2 Example 2: Work Against Gravity

Question: A 50 N package is lifted vertically through a height of 10 m at constant speed. Calculate: (a) the work done by the lifting force (b) the gravitational potential energy gained by the package

Solution:

(a) Work done: The lifting force must equal the weight (50 N) to move at constant speed. \[W = F \times d = 50 \times 10 = \boxed{500 \text{ J}}\]

(b) Gravitational potential energy gained: By conservation of energy (neglecting losses): \[\Delta E_p = W = \boxed{500 \text{ J}}\]

11.3.3 Example 3: Work on an Incline

Question: A trolley of mass 80 kg is pushed up a gentle incline. The librarian exerts a force of 40 N parallel to the incline, moving the trolley a distance of 10 m along the slope. The vertical height gained is 0.5 m. Taking \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\): (a) Calculate the work done by the 40 N force (b) Calculate the gain in gravitational potential energy of the trolley (c) Explain any difference between your answers to (a) and (b)

Solution:

(a) Work done by applied force: \[W = F \times d = 40 \times 10 = \boxed{400 \text{ J}}\]

(b) Gain in gravitational potential energy: \[\Delta E_p = mg\Delta h = 80 \times 10 \times 0.5 = \boxed{400 \text{ J}}\]

(c) Explanation: In this idealised example (neglecting friction and air resistance), the work done by the applied force equals the gain in gravitational potential energy. In reality, some work would be dissipated as thermal energy due to friction, so the gain in \(E_p\) would be slightly less than the work input.

11.4 What Is Power?

11.4.1 Definition of Power

While work measures the total amount of energy transferred, power measures the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

Power is defined as work done per unit time, or equivalently, energy transferred per unit time.

11.4.2 Equations for Power

\[P = \frac{W}{t} \quad \text{or} \quad P = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\]

Where: - \(P\) = power (W) - \(W\) = work done (J) - \(\Delta E\) = energy transferred (J) - \(t\) = time taken (s)

Note

Key Concept: One watt (1 W) is defined as the power when one joule of work is done (or energy transferred) per second:

\[1 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ J/s}\]

11.4.3 Understanding Power Through Examples

Scenario Explanation
Two boys climbing stairs (same mass, same height) The boy who climbs faster has greater power because he does the same work in less time
Two kettles boiling water (same amount of water) The 1000 W kettle boils water faster than the 500 W kettle because it transfers energy at a higher rate
Lifting bricks with a machine A machine that lifts 200 kg of bricks 30 m in 50 s has power \(P = \frac{mgh}{t} = \frac{200 \times 10 \times 30}{50} = 1200 \text{ W} = 1.2 \text{ kW}\)

11.5 8.4 Worked Examples: Calculating Power

11.5.1 Example 4: Power of a Person

Question: Eugene, who weighs 450 N, runs up ten steps. Each step is 0.20 m high. Calculate Eugene’s power if he takes 5.0 seconds to run up the steps at constant speed.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate total vertical height: \[h = 10 \times 0.20 = 2.0 \text{ m}\]

Step 2: Calculate work done (against gravity): \[W = F \times d = 450 \times 2.0 = 900 \text{ J}\]

Step 3: Calculate power: \[P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{900}{5.0} = \boxed{180 \text{ W}}\]

11.5.2 Example 5: Power of an Electrical Device

Question: A filament lamp rated at 40 W converts 10% of its electrical energy supply to light energy. Calculate: (a) the total electrical energy used by the lamp in 5 minutes (b) the quantity of light energy given off in this time

Solution:

(a) Total electrical energy used: \[t = 5 \times 60 = 300 \text{ s}\] \[E_{\text{total}} = P \times t = 40 \times 300 = 1.2 \times 10^4 \text{ J} = \boxed{12 \text{ kJ}}\]

(b) Light energy output: \[E_{\text{light}} = 10\% \times E_{\text{total}} = 0.10 \times 12000 = \boxed{1.2 \text{ kJ}}\]

Interpretation: The remaining 10.8 kJ is dissipated as thermal energy, which is why the lamp becomes hot during operation.

11.5.3 Example 6: Comparing Power of Machines

Question: Machine A lifts a 500 N load through 4 m in 10 s. Machine B lifts the same load through the same height in 5 s. Calculate: (a) the work done by each machine (b) the power of each machine (c) State which machine is more powerful and explain why

Solution:

(a) Work done (same for both): \[W = F \times d = 500 \times 4 = \boxed{2000 \text{ J}}\]

(b) Power of each machine:

  • Machine A: \(P_A = \frac{2000}{10} = \boxed{200 \text{ W}}\)

  • Machine B: \(P_B = \frac{2000}{5} = \boxed{400 \text{ W}}\)

(c) Comparison: Machine B is more powerful because it transfers the same amount of energy in half the time. Power depends on both the amount of work done and the time taken.

11.6 Key Concepts Summary

11.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Work is done when a force causes displacement in its direction: \(W = Fd\)

  2. Work–energy relationship: Work done = energy transferred (\(W = \Delta E\))

  3. Unit of work and energy: joule (J); \(1 \text{ J} = 1 \text{ N} \times 1 \text{ m}\)

  4. Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy: \(P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\)

  5. Unit of power: watt (W); \(1 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ J/s}\)

  6. Work is not done when: (a) force causes no displacement, or (b) displacement is perpendicular to force

11.6.2 Extended Insights

  1. When work is done against gravity: \(W = mg\Delta h = \Delta E_p\)

  2. In real systems, not all work input appears as useful energy output due to dissipation (friction, heating)

  3. Higher power means the same work can be done in less time, or more work can be done in the same time

11.7 Common Misconceptions

11.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Holding a heavy object requires work”

Incorrect: “I am doing work holding this 20 kg box”

Correct:

  • In physics, work requires displacement: \(W = Fd\)

  • If the box is held stationary, \(d = 0\), so \(W = 0\)

  • Your muscles expend chemical energy to maintain the force, but no mechanical work is done on the box

11.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Work and power are the same thing”

Incorrect: “A 100 W bulb does more work than a 60 W bulb”

Correct:

  • Work measures total energy transferred (J)

  • Power measures the rate of energy transfer (W = J/s)

  • A 100 W bulb transfers energy faster than a 60 W bulb, but the total work depends on how long each bulb operates

11.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Any force applied does work”

Incorrect: “Pushing a wall does work because I apply force”

Correct:

  • Work requires both force and displacement in the direction of the force

  • If the wall does not move (\(d = 0\)), then \(W = F \times 0 = 0\)

  • Effort ≠ work in the physics sense

11.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Power depends only on force”

Incorrect: “A larger force always means more power”

Correct:

  • Power depends on work done per unit time: \(P = \frac{Fd}{t}\)

  • A small force acting over a large distance quickly can produce more power than a large force acting slowly over a short distance

  • Example: A cyclist pedalling lightly but rapidly may have higher power output than one pushing hard but slowly

11.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Work done always equals useful energy output”

Incorrect: “If I do 100 J of work lifting a box, the box gains 100 J of potential energy”

Correct:

  • In ideal conditions (no friction), \(W = \Delta E_p\)

  • In real situations, some work is dissipated as thermal energy due to friction or air resistance

  • Useful energy output < total work input; efficiency = \(\frac{\text{useful output}}{\text{total input}} \times 100\%\)

11.8 Practice Questions

11.8.1 Core Level

1. Define the term work in physics. State its SI unit.

2. A force of 25 N moves an object through a distance of 4.0 m in the direction of the force. Calculate the work done.

3. A student carries a 30 N school bag horizontally for a distance of 50 m. Explain why the work done by the upward supporting force is zero.

4. A crane lifts a 5000 N load vertically through 12 m. Calculate:

    1. the work done by the crane
    1. the gravitational potential energy gained by the load

5. Define power. State its SI unit and express it in terms of base units.

6. A motor does 1200 J of work in 30 seconds. Calculate its power output.

7. Two electric kettles, one rated at 500 W and the other at 1000 W, are used to boil the same amount of water. Explain which kettle will boil the water faster and why.

8. A person weighing 600 N climbs a flight of stairs of vertical height 3.0 m in 6.0 seconds. Calculate:

    1. the work done against gravity
    1. the person’s average power output

11.8.2 Extended Level

9. A box is pulled along a horizontal floor by a force of 80 N at an angle of 30° to the horizontal. The box moves 5.0 m. Calculate the work done by the force. (Hint: Use the component of force in the direction of motion.)

10. A car engine has a power output of 60 kW. Calculate:

    1. the work done by the engine in 2.0 minutes
    1. the distance the car can travel in this time if the total resistive force is 1500 N and the car moves at constant speed

11. A pump lifts 200 kg of water vertically through 15 m in 30 seconds. Taking \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\):

    1. Calculate the work done against gravity
    1. Calculate the minimum power output required by the pump
    1. Explain why the actual power input to the pump must be greater than your answer to (b)

12. Two students, A and B, each lift a 40 kg box vertically through 2.0 m. Student A takes 4.0 s; Student B takes 2.0 s.

    1. Compare the work done by each student
    1. Compare the power output of each student
    1. Explain your answers

13. A filament lamp is rated at 60 W and has an efficiency of 8%. Calculate:

    1. the electrical energy consumed in 1 hour
    1. the useful light energy output in this time
    1. the energy dissipated as heat

14. Explain, using the equations for work and power, why a more powerful engine allows a car to accelerate faster (assuming constant mass and resistive forces).

11.9 Laboratory Investigation

11.9.1 Experiment 8.1: Measuring Human Power Output

Objective: To determine the average power output of a person climbing stairs.

Apparatus: - Metre rule or measuring tape - Stopwatch - Bathroom scale (to measure mass) - Flight of stairs with known number of steps

Variables: - Independent: None (single measurement per trial) - Dependent: Power output \(P\) (W) - Controlled: Same person, same stairs, same technique

Procedure: 1. Measure and record your mass \(m\) using the bathroom scale.

  1. Measure the vertical height \(h\) of one step using the metre rule.

  2. Count the total number of steps \(n\) in the flight.

  3. Calculate total vertical height: \(H = n \times h\).

  4. Stand at the bottom of the stairs. On a signal, climb the stairs at a steady, comfortable pace.

  5. Use the stopwatch to measure the time \(t\) taken to climb from bottom to top.

  6. Repeat the climb two more times and calculate the average time \(t_{\text{avg}}\).

  7. Calculate:

    • Weight: \(W = mg\) (take \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\))
    • Work done against gravity: \(W_{\text{done}} = mgH\)
    • Average power output: \(P = \frac{mgH}{t_{\text{avg}}}\)

11.11 Chapter Summary

11.11.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Work is done when a force causes displacement in its direction: \(W = Fd\)
✓ Work–energy principle: Work done = energy transferred (\(W = \Delta E\))
✓ Unit of work/energy: joule (J); \(1 \text{ J} = 1 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m}\)
✓ No work is done if: (a) \(d = 0\), or (b) force ⟂ displacement
✓ Power is the rate of doing work or transferring energy: \(P = \frac{W}{t} = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\)
✓ Unit of power: watt (W); \(1 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ J/s}\)
✓ Work against gravity: \(W = mg\Delta h = \Delta E_p\)
✓ Real systems: Useful energy output < work input due to dissipation; efficiency < 100%

11.11.2 Formulae Sheet

Quantity Formula Variables
Work done \(W = F \times d\) \(W\)=work (J), \(F\)=force in direction of motion (N), \(d\)=distance (m)
Work–energy \(W = \Delta E\) Energy transferred equals work done
Power \(P = \frac{W}{t}\) or \(P = \frac{\Delta E}{t}\) \(P\)=power (W), \(t\)=time (s)
Work against gravity \(W = mg\Delta h\) \(m\)=mass (kg), \(g\)=field strength (N/kg), \(\Delta h\)=height change (m)

11.13 Glossary

Term Definition
Dissipated energy Energy that is spread out into the surroundings (usually as thermal energy) and is no longer available to do useful work
Efficiency Ratio of useful energy output to total energy input, expressed as a percentage
Joule (J) SI unit of work and energy; \(1 \text{ J} = 1 \text{ N} \cdot \text{m}\)
Power Rate of doing work or transferring energy; \(P = \frac{W}{t}\); SI unit: watt (W)
Watt (W) SI unit of power; \(1 \text{ W} = 1 \text{ J/s}\)
Work Energy transferred when a force causes displacement in its direction; \(W = Fd\); SI unit: joule (J)
Work–energy principle The work done on an object equals the change in its energy: \(W = \Delta E\)
