22.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (4.2.1 Electric Charge):

  • State that there are positive and negative charges

  • State that positive charges repel other positive charges, negative charges repel other negative charges, but positive charges attract negative charges

  • Describe simple experiments to show the production of electrostatic charges by friction and to show the detection of electrostatic charges

  • Explain that charging of solids by friction involves only a transfer of negative charge (electrons)

  • Describe an experiment to distinguish between electrical conductors and insulators

  • Recall and use a simple electron model to explain the difference between electrical conductors and insulators and give typical examples

Core Content (4.2.2 Electric Current):

  • Know that electric current is related to the flow of charge

  • Describe the use of ammeters (analogue and digital) with different ranges

  • Describe electrical conduction in metals in terms of the movement of free electrons

  • Know the difference between direct current (d.c.) and alternating current (a.c.)

Core Content (4.2.3 Electromotive Force and Potential Difference):

  • Define electromotive force (e.m.f.) as the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit

  • Know that e.m.f. is measured in volts (V)

  • Define potential difference (p.d.) as the work done by a unit charge passing through a component

  • Know that the p.d. between two points is measured in volts (V)

  • Describe the use of voltmeters (analogue and digital) with different ranges

Core Content (4.2.4 Resistance):

  • Recall and use the equation for resistance R = V/I

  • Describe an experiment to determine resistance using a voltmeter and an ammeter and do the appropriate calculations

  • State, qualitatively, the relationship of the resistance of a metallic wire to its length and to its cross-sectional area

Core Content (4.2.5 Electrical Energy and Electrical Power):

  • Understand that electric circuits transfer energy from a source of electrical energy, such as an electrical cell or mains supply, to the circuit components and then into the surroundings

  • Recall and use the equation for electrical power P = IV

  • Recall and use the equation for electrical energy E = IVt

  • Define the kilowatt-hour (kW h) and calculate the cost of using electrical appliances where the energy unit is the kW h

Core Content (4.3.1 Circuit Diagrams and Circuit Components):

  • Draw and interpret circuit diagrams containing cells, batteries, power supplies, generators, potential dividers, switches, resistors (fixed and variable), heaters, thermistors (NTC only), light-dependent resistors (LDRs), lamps, motors, bells, ammeters, voltmeters, magnetising coils, transformers, fuses and relays and know how these components behave in the circuit

Core Content (4.3.2 Series and Parallel Circuits):

  • Know that the current at every point in a series circuit is the same

  • Know how to construct and use series and parallel circuits

  • Calculate the combined e.m.f. of several sources in series

  • Calculate the combined resistance of two or more resistors in series

  • State that, for a parallel circuit, the current from the source is larger than the current in each branch

  • State that the combined resistance of two resistors in parallel is less than that of either resistor by itself

  • State the advantages of connecting lamps in parallel in a lighting circuit

Supplement (Extended):

  • State that charge is measured in coulombs

  • Describe an electric field as a region in which an electric charge experiences a force

  • State that the direction of an electric field at a point is the direction of the force on a positive charge at that point

  • Describe simple electric field patterns, including the direction of the field: (a) around a point charge (b) around a charged conducting sphere (c) between two oppositely charged parallel conducting plates

  • Define electric current as the charge passing a point per unit time; recall and use the equation I = Q/t

  • State that conventional current is from positive to negative and that the flow of free electrons is from negative to positive

  • Recall and use the equation for e.m.f. E = W/Q

  • Recall and use the equation for p.d. V = W/Q

  • Sketch and explain the current–voltage graphs for a resistor of constant resistance, a filament lamp and a diode

  • Recall and use the following relationship for a metallic electrical conductor: (a) resistance is directly proportional to length (b) resistance is inversely proportional to cross-sectional area

  • Draw and interpret circuit diagrams containing diodes and light-emitting diodes (LEDs) and know how these components behave in the circuit

  • Recall and use in calculations, the fact that: (a) the sum of the currents entering a junction in a parallel circuit is equal to the sum of the currents that leave the junction (b) the total p.d. across the components in a series circuit is equal to the sum of the individual p.d.s across each component (c) the p.d. across an arrangement of parallel resistances is the same as the p.d. across one branch in the arrangement of the parallel resistances

  • Explain that the sum of the currents into a junction is the same as the sum of the currents out of the junction

  • Calculate the combined resistance of two resistors in parallel

  • Describe the action of a variable potential divider

  • Recall and use the equation for two resistors used as a potential divider: R1/R2 = V1/V2

22.2 19.1 Electric Charge

22.2.1 What Is Electric Charge?

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter. There are two types of electric charge:

  • Positive charge (+)

  • Negative charge (–)

Key properties of electric charge:

  • Like charges repel each other (positive–positive or negative–negative)

  • Unlike charges attract each other (positive–negative)

  • Charge is conserved — it cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred

Note

Key Concept: The SI unit of electric charge is the coulomb (C). One coulomb is the charge transported by a constant current of one ampere in one second.

22.2.2 Charging by Friction

When two different insulating materials are rubbed together, electrons may be transferred from one material to the other.

Example: Charging a plastic rod with a cloth

  1. Rub a plastic rod with a dry cloth

  2. Electrons are transferred from the cloth to the plastic rod

  3. The plastic rod gains electrons → becomes negatively charged

  4. The cloth loses electrons → becomes positively charged

Detection of electrostatic charges:

  • A gold-leaf electroscope can detect the presence of charge

  • When a charged object is brought near the electroscope, the gold leaf diverges due to repulsion of like charges

Warning

Important: Only electrons (negative charges) are transferred during charging by friction. Protons remain fixed in the atomic nucleus.

22.2.3 Conductors and Insulators

Type Description Examples
Electrical conductors Materials that allow electric charge to flow easily through them Metals (copper, aluminium), graphite, salt water
Electrical insulators Materials that do not allow electric charge to flow easily through them Plastic, rubber, glass, dry wood, air

Electron model explanation:

  • In conductors (metals): Some electrons are free to move throughout the material (free electrons). When a potential difference is applied, these free electrons drift, creating an electric current.

  • In insulators: Electrons are tightly bound to their atoms and cannot move freely. No significant current flows even when a potential difference is applied.

Experiment: Distinguishing conductors and insulators

Apparatus:

  • Battery, bulb, connecting wires, test materials

Procedure:

  1. Set up a simple circuit with a battery and bulb

  2. Insert the test material into the circuit

  3. Observe whether the bulb lights up

Observation:

  • Bulb lights up → material is a conductor

  • Bulb does not light up → material is an insulator

22.3 19.2 Electric Current

22.3.1 What Is Electric Current?

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge.

\[I = \frac{Q}{t}\]

Where: - \(I\) = electric current (A) - \(Q\) = charge flowing (C) - \(t\) = time (s)

Note

Key Concept: One ampere (1 A) is defined as one coulomb of charge passing a point per second: \(1 \text{ A} = 1 \text{ C/s}\).

22.3.2 Conventional Current vs Electron Flow

Concept Direction Explanation
Conventional current Positive → Negative Historical convention established before electrons were discovered
Electron flow Negative → Positive Actual movement of free electrons in metallic conductors
Warning

Important: In circuit analysis and diagrams, we use conventional current direction (positive to negative), even though electrons actually flow in the opposite direction.

22.3.3 Direct Current (d.c.) vs Alternating Current (a.c.)

Type Description Examples
Direct current (d.c.) Current flows in one direction only Batteries, solar cells, d.c. power supplies
Alternating current (a.c.) Current reverses direction periodically Mains electricity (50 Hz or 60 Hz), a.c. generators

22.3.4 Measuring Electric Current: Ammeters

  • Ammeters measure electric current in amperes (A)

  • Must be connected in series with the component being measured

  • Analogue ammeters: Use a moving coil mechanism; read from a scale

  • Digital ammeters: Display numerical values; often more precise

Precautions when using ammeters:

  • ✓ Always connect in series, never in parallel

  • ✓ Select an appropriate range to avoid damaging the meter

  • ✓ For analogue meters, avoid parallax error by viewing the scale perpendicularly

22.4 19.3 Electromotive Force and Potential Difference

22.4.1 What Is Electromotive Force (e.m.f.)?

Electromotive force (e.m.f.) is the electrical work done by a source in moving a unit charge around a complete circuit.

\[E = \frac{W}{Q}\]

Where: - \(E\) = e.m.f. (V) - \(W\) = work done or energy transferred (J) - \(Q\) = charge (C)

Key points about e.m.f.:

  • Measured in volts (V)

  • Represents the total energy per unit charge supplied by the source

  • Measured across the terminals of a source when no current is flowing (open circuit)

22.4.2 What Is Potential Difference (p.d.)?

Potential difference (p.d.) is the work done by a unit charge passing through a component.

\[V = \frac{W}{Q}\]

Where: - \(V\) = potential difference (V) - \(W\) = work done or energy transferred (J) - \(Q\) = charge (C)

Key points about p.d.:

  • Also measured in volts (V)

  • Represents the energy transferred per unit charge by a component

  • Measured across a component when current is flowing

22.4.3 Measuring Potential Difference: Voltmeters

  • Voltmeters measure potential difference in volts (V)

  • Must be connected in parallel across the component being measured

  • Analogue voltmeters: Use a moving coil mechanism; read from a scale

  • Digital voltmeters: Display numerical values; often more precise

Precautions when using voltmeters:

  • ✓ Always connect in parallel, never in series

  • ✓ Select an appropriate range to avoid damaging the meter

  • ✓ For analogue meters, avoid parallax error by viewing the scale perpendicularly

22.5 19.4 Resistance and Ohm’s Law

22.5.1 What Is Electrical Resistance?

Resistance is a measure of how much a component opposes the flow of electric current.

\[R = \frac{V}{I}\]

Where: - \(R\) = resistance (Ω) - \(V\) = potential difference across the component (V) - \(I\) = current through the component (A)

Note

Key Concept: One ohm (1 Ω) is defined as the resistance when a potential difference of one volt produces a current of one ampere: \(1 \text{ Ω} = 1 \text{ V/A}\).

22.5.2 Ohm’s Law

For a metallic conductor at constant temperature:

The current through the conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it.

\[V \propto I \quad \text{or} \quad V = IR\]

Conditions for Ohm’s Law to apply:

  • Temperature must remain constant

  • The conductor must be ohmic (e.g., a fixed resistor)

22.5.3 Factors Affecting the Resistance of a Wire

For a metallic wire at constant temperature:

Factor Effect on Resistance Explanation
Length (\(L\)) \(R \propto L\) (directly proportional) Longer wire → more collisions for electrons → higher resistance
Cross-sectional area (\(A\)) \(R \propto \frac{1}{A}\) (inversely proportional) Thicker wire → more paths for electrons → lower resistance
Material Depends on resistivity (\(\rho\)) Different materials have different numbers of free electrons
Temperature For metals: \(R\) increases with temperature Higher temperature → more lattice vibrations → more electron collisions

22.5.4 Current–Voltage (I–V) Characteristics

Component I–V Graph Shape Explanation
Fixed resistor Straight line through origin Ohmic conductor; constant resistance
Filament lamp Curve with decreasing gradient Resistance increases as filament heats up
Diode Conducts in one direction only; very high resistance in reverse Allows current in forward bias; blocks current in reverse bias

22.6 19.5 Electrical Energy and Power

22.6.1 Electrical Power

Electrical power is the rate at which electrical energy is transferred.

\[P = IV\]

Where: - \(P\) = power (W) - \(I\) = current (A) - \(V\) = potential difference (V)

Alternative forms (using \(V = IR\)):

\[P = I^2R \quad \text{or} \quad P = \frac{V^2}{R}\]

22.6.2 Electrical Energy

Electrical energy transferred by a component:

\[E = IVt\]

Where: - \(E\) = energy (J) - \(I\) = current (A) - \(V\) = potential difference (V) - \(t\) = time (s)

22.6.3 The Kilowatt-Hour (kW h)

The kilowatt-hour is a practical unit for measuring electrical energy used in homes.

\[1 \text{ kW h} = 1000 \text{ W} \times 3600 \text{ s} = 3.6 \times 10^6 \text{ J}\]

Calculating cost of electricity:

\[\text{Cost} = \text{Energy used (kW h)} \times \text{Cost per kW h}\]

Note

Key Insight: The kilowatt-hour is used because the joule is too small for practical household energy measurements.

22.8 19.7 Series and Parallel Circuits

22.8.1 Series Circuits

Key properties:

  • Current is the same at every point: \(I_{\text{total}} = I_1 = I_2 = I_3\)

  • Total p.d. is the sum of individual p.d.s: \(V_{\text{total}} = V_1 + V_2 + V_3\)

  • Total resistance is the sum of individual resistances: \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3\)

  • If one component fails (e.g., a lamp blows), the entire circuit stops working

22.8.2 Parallel Circuits

Key properties:

  • Total current from source is the sum of branch currents: \(I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 + I_3\)

  • p.d. across each branch is the same: \(V_{\text{total}} = V_1 = V_2 = V_3\)

  • Combined resistance is less than the smallest individual resistance: \(\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3}\)

  • If one component fails, other branches continue to work

22.8.3 Advantages of Parallel Connection for Lamps

  1. Each lamp receives the full supply voltage → operates at rated brightness

  2. Lamps can be switched on/off independently

  3. If one lamp fails, others continue to work

  4. Adding more lamps does not reduce brightness of existing lamps

22.9 19.8 Potential Dividers

22.9.1 What Is a Potential Divider?

A potential divider is a circuit that produces an output voltage that is a fraction of the input voltage.

Basic potential divider circuit:

     V_in
      │
     ┌┴┐
     │R1│
     └┬┘
      ├─── V_out
     ┌┴┐
     │R2│
     └┬┘
      │
     GND

Equation for output voltage:

\[V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\]

Alternative form:

\[\frac{R_1}{R_2} = \frac{V_1}{V_2}\]

22.9.2 Applications of Potential Dividers

Application Explanation
Volume control Variable resistor adjusts output voltage to amplifier
Light sensor LDR in potential divider; output voltage changes with light intensity
Temperature sensor Thermistor in potential divider; output voltage changes with temperature
Reference voltage Provides stable reference voltage for electronic circuits

22.10 Key Concepts Summary

22.10.1 Core Concepts

  1. Electric charge: Two types (+ and –); like charges repel, unlike attract; measured in coulombs (C)

  2. Charging by friction: Transfer of electrons only; insulators can be charged, conductors cannot (unless insulated)

  3. Conductors vs insulators: Conductors have free electrons; insulators have bound electrons

  4. Electric current: Rate of flow of charge; \(I = Q/t\); measured in amperes (A) with ammeters in series

  5. d.c. vs a.c.: d.c. flows one way; a.c. reverses direction periodically

  6. e.m.f. and p.d.: Both measured in volts; e.m.f. is energy supplied per charge by source; p.d. is energy transferred per charge by component

  7. Resistance: Opposition to current flow; \(R = V/I\); measured in ohms (Ω)

  8. Ohm’s Law: \(V = IR\) for ohmic conductors at constant temperature

  9. Factors affecting resistance: \(R \propto L\), \(R \propto 1/A\), depends on material and temperature

  10. Electrical power and energy: \(P = IV\); \(E = IVt\); kilowatt-hour (kW h) for practical energy measurement

  11. Circuit symbols: Standard symbols for components; ammeters in series, voltmeters in parallel

  12. Series circuits: Same current; p.d.s add; resistances add

  13. Parallel circuits: Currents add; same p.d.; combined resistance less than smallest

  14. Potential dividers: Produce fraction of input voltage; \(V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\)

22.10.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Electric fields: Region where a charge experiences a force; direction = force on positive charge

  2. Field patterns: Radial around point charge; uniform between parallel plates

  3. I–V characteristics: Resistor (linear), filament lamp (curve), diode (one-way conduction)

  4. Kirchhoff’s laws: Sum of currents at junction = 0; sum of p.d.s around loop = 0

  5. Combined resistance in parallel: \(\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2}\)

22.11 Common Misconceptions

22.11.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Current is used up as it flows through a circuit”

Incorrect: “The current is smaller after passing through a lamp”

Correct: - Current is conserved in a series circuit; the same current flows through all components - What is “used up” is energy, not charge or current - The p.d. across a component represents energy transferred per charge, not current loss

22.11.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Electrons flow from positive to negative”

Incorrect: “Electrons move from the positive terminal of a battery to the negative terminal”

Correct: - Electrons (negative charges) flow from negative to positive - Conventional current is defined as flowing from positive to negative (historical convention) - In circuit analysis, we use conventional current direction

22.11.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “A voltmeter should be connected in series”

Incorrect: “To measure voltage, connect the voltmeter in the same path as the current”

Correct: - Voltmeters must be connected in parallel across the component - Connecting a voltmeter in series would break the circuit (very high resistance) - Ammeters are connected in series; voltmeters in parallel

22.11.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Resistance depends only on the material”

Incorrect: “All copper wires have the same resistance”

Correct: - Resistance depends on four factors: length, cross-sectional area, material, and temperature - For a given material at constant temperature: \(R \propto \frac{L}{A}\) - A longer or thinner wire has higher resistance

22.11.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Ohm’s Law applies to all components”

Incorrect: “All electrical components obey \(V = IR\)”

Correct: - Ohm’s Law applies only to ohmic conductors (e.g., fixed resistors) at constant temperature - Filament lamps, diodes, and thermistors are non-ohmic; their I–V graphs are not straight lines - Always check the I–V characteristic before applying Ohm’s Law

22.11.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Adding resistors in parallel increases total resistance”

Incorrect: “More resistors in parallel means more resistance”

Correct: - Adding resistors in parallel decreases total resistance - Each new branch provides an additional path for current - Formula: \(\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots\) → \(R_{\text{total}}\) is less than the smallest \(R\)

22.11.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “The kilowatt-hour is a unit of power”

Incorrect: “My electricity bill charges me per kilowatt-hour of power used”

Correct: - The kilowatt-hour (kW h) is a unit of energy, not power - Power is measured in watts (W) or kilowatts (kW) - Energy = Power × Time → kW h = kW × h

22.12 Worked Examples

22.12.1 Example 1: Charge and Current Calculation

Question: A current of 0.5 A flows through a wire for 2 minutes. Calculate: (a) the charge that passes a point in the wire (b) the number of electrons that pass that point (charge of one electron = \(1.6 \times 10^{-19}\) C)

Solution:

(a) Charge: \[I = 0.5 \text{ A}, \quad t = 2 \times 60 = 120 \text{ s}\] \[Q = It = 0.5 \times 120 = \boxed{60 \text{ C}}\]

(b) Number of electrons: \[\text{Number} = \frac{Q}{e} = \frac{60}{1.6 \times 10^{-19}} = \boxed{3.75 \times 10^{20} \text{ electrons}}\]

22.12.2 Example 2: Resistance Calculation

Question: A resistor has a potential difference of 6.0 V across it when a current of 0.25 A flows through it. Calculate: (a) the resistance of the resistor (b) the power dissipated by the resistor (c) the energy transferred in 5 minutes

Solution:

(a) Resistance: \[R = \frac{V}{I} = \frac{6.0}{0.25} = \boxed{24 \text{ Ω}}\]

(b) Power: \[P = IV = 0.25 \times 6.0 = \boxed{1.5 \text{ W}}\]

(c) Energy: \[t = 5 \times 60 = 300 \text{ s}\] \[E = IVt = 0.25 \times 6.0 \times 300 = \boxed{450 \text{ J}}\]

22.12.3 Example 3: Series Circuit Analysis

Question: Two resistors, \(R_1 = 10 \text{ Ω}\) and \(R_2 = 20 \text{ Ω}\), are connected in series to a 12 V battery. Calculate: (a) the total resistance (b) the current in the circuit (c) the p.d. across each resistor

Solution:

(a) Total resistance: \[R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 = 10 + 20 = \boxed{30 \text{ Ω}}\]

(b) Current: \[I = \frac{V}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{12}{30} = \boxed{0.4 \text{ A}}\]

(c) p.d. across each resistor: \[V_1 = IR_1 = 0.4 \times 10 = \boxed{4.0 \text{ V}}\] \[V_2 = IR_2 = 0.4 \times 20 = \boxed{8.0 \text{ V}}\] Check: \(V_1 + V_2 = 4.0 + 8.0 = 12.0 \text{ V}\) ✓

22.12.4 Example 4: Parallel Circuit Analysis (Extended)

Question: Two resistors, \(R_1 = 6 \text{ Ω}\) and \(R_2 = 3 \text{ Ω}\), are connected in parallel to a 12 V supply. Calculate: (a) the combined resistance (b) the current through each resistor (c) the total current from the supply

Solution:

(a) Combined resistance: \[\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{1}{3} = \frac{1}{6} + \frac{2}{6} = \frac{3}{6} = \frac{1}{2}\] \[R_{\text{total}} = \boxed{2 \text{ Ω}}\]

(b) Current through each resistor: \[I_1 = \frac{V}{R_1} = \frac{12}{6} = \boxed{2.0 \text{ A}}\] \[I_2 = \frac{V}{R_2} = \frac{12}{3} = \boxed{4.0 \text{ A}}\]

(c) Total current: \[I_{\text{total}} = I_1 + I_2 = 2.0 + 4.0 = \boxed{6.0 \text{ A}}\] Check: \(I_{\text{total}} = \frac{V}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{12}{2} = 6.0 \text{ A}\) ✓

22.12.5 Example 5: Potential Divider Calculation (Extended)

Question: A potential divider circuit has \(R_1 = 400 \text{ Ω}\) and \(R_2 = 600 \text{ Ω}\) connected to a 10 V supply. Calculate: (a) the output voltage \(V_{\text{out}}\) across \(R_2\) (b) the current through the divider

Solution:

(a) Output voltage: \[V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2} = 10 \times \frac{600}{400 + 600} = 10 \times \frac{600}{1000} = \boxed{6.0 \text{ V}}\]

(b) Current through divider: \[R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 = 400 + 600 = 1000 \text{ Ω}\] \[I = \frac{V_{\text{in}}}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{10}{1000} = \boxed{0.01 \text{ A} = 10 \text{ mA}}\]

22.13 Practice Questions

22.13.1 Core Level

  1. State the two types of electric charge and the rule for attraction and repulsion.

  2. Explain why a plastic rod becomes negatively charged when rubbed with a cloth.

  3. Define electric current and state its SI unit.

  4. A charge of 30 C passes a point in a circuit in 15 s. Calculate the current.

  5. State the difference between direct current (d.c.) and alternating current (a.c.).

  6. Define potential difference and state its SI unit.

22.16 Chapter Summary

22.16.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Electric charge: Two types (+ and –); like repel, unlike attract; measured in coulombs (C)

✓ Charging by friction: Transfer of electrons only; insulators can be charged

✓ Conductors vs insulators: Conductors have free electrons; insulators have bound electrons

✓ Electric current: \(I = Q/t\); measured in amperes (A) with ammeters in series

✓ d.c. vs a.c.: d.c. one direction; a.c. reverses periodically

✓ e.m.f. and p.d.: Both in volts; e.m.f. = energy supplied per charge; p.d. = energy transferred per charge

✓ Resistance: \(R = V/I\); measured in ohms (Ω); \(R \propto L\), \(R \propto 1/A\)

✓ Ohm’s Law: \(V = IR\) for ohmic conductors at constant temperature

✓ Electrical power and energy: \(P = IV\); \(E = IVt\); kW h for practical energy measurement

✓ Circuit symbols: Standard symbols; ammeters in series, voltmeters in parallel

✓ Series circuits: Same current; p.d.s add; \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + \dots\)

✓ Parallel circuits: Currents add; same p.d.; \(\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \dots\)

✓ Potential dividers: \(V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\)

22.16.2 Formulae Sheet

Quantity Formula Variables
Electric current \(I = \frac{Q}{t}\) \(I\)=current (A), \(Q\)=charge (C), \(t\)=time (s)
Resistance \(R = \frac{V}{I}\) \(R\)=resistance (Ω), \(V\)=p.d. (V), \(I\)=current (A)
Electrical power \(P = IV = I^2R = \frac{V^2}{R}\) \(P\)=power (W)
Electrical energy \(E = IVt = Pt\) \(E\)=energy (J)
e.m.f. / p.d. \(E \text{ or } V = \frac{W}{Q}\) \(W\)=work/energy (J), \(Q\)=charge (C)
Combined resistance (series) \(R_{\text{total}} = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + \dots\) —
Combined resistance (parallel) \(\frac{1}{R_{\text{total}}} = \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} + \frac{1}{R_3} + \dots\) —
Potential divider \(V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times \frac{R_2}{R_1 + R_2}\) \(R_1\), \(R_2\) = divider resistances

22.18 Glossary

Term Definition
Ammeter Instrument for measuring electric current; connected in series
Conventional current Conventional direction of current flow from positive to negative terminal
Conductor Material that allows electric charge to flow easily (e.g., metals)
Coulomb (C) SI unit of electric charge; charge transported by 1 A in 1 s
Direct current (d.c.) Electric current that flows in one direction only
Electric current (\(I\)) Rate of flow of electric charge; \(I = Q/t\); unit: ampere (A)
Electric field Region in which an electric charge experiences a force
Electromotive force (e.m.f.) Electrical work done by a source per unit charge around a complete circuit; unit: volt (V)
Electron Negatively charged subatomic particle; carrier of charge in metallic conductors
Insulator Material that does not allow electric charge to flow easily (e.g., plastic, rubber)
Kilowatt-hour (kW h) Unit of electrical energy; energy used by a 1 kW device in 1 hour
Ohm (Ω) SI unit of resistance; \(1 \text{ Ω} = 1 \text{ V/A}\)
Ohm’s Law For an ohmic conductor at constant temperature: \(V = IR\)
Potential difference (p.d.) Work done per unit charge passing through a component; unit: volt (V)
Resistance (\(R\)) Opposition to the flow of electric current; \(R = V/I\); unit: ohm (Ω)
Volt (V) SI unit of e.m.f. and p.d.; \(1 \text{ V} = 1 \text{ J/C}\)
Voltmeter Instrument for measuring potential difference; connected in parallel
