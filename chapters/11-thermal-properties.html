14.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (2.2.1 Thermal Expansion):

  • Describe, qualitatively, the thermal expansion of solids, liquids and gases at constant pressure

  • Describe some of the everyday applications and consequences of thermal expansion

Core Content (2.2.2 Specific Heat Capacity):

  • Know that a rise in the temperature of an object increases its internal energy

Core Content (2.2.3 Melting, Boiling and Evaporation):

  • Describe melting and boiling in terms of energy input without a change in temperature

  • Know the melting and boiling temperatures for water at standard atmospheric pressure

  • Describe condensation and solidification in terms of particles

  • Describe evaporation in terms of the escape of more-energetic particles from the surface of a liquid

  • Know that evaporation causes cooling of a liquid

Supplement (Extended):

  • Explain, in terms of the motion and arrangement of particles, the relative order of magnitudes of the expansion of solids, liquids and gases as their temperatures rise

  • Describe an increase in temperature of an object in terms of an increase in the average kinetic energies of all of the particles in the object

  • Define specific heat capacity as the energy required per unit mass per unit temperature increase; recall and use the equation \(c = \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta}\)

  • Describe experiments to measure the specific heat capacity of a solid and a liquid

  • Describe the differences between boiling and evaporation

  • Describe how temperature, surface area and air movement over a surface affect evaporation

  • Explain the cooling of an object in contact with an evaporating liquid

14.2 Thermal Expansion of Solids, Liquids and Gases

14.2.1 What Happens When Materials Are Heated?

When solids, liquids and gases are heated, they increase in volume or expand. When cooled, they decrease in volume or contract.

State Amount of Expansion Particle-Level Explanation
Solids Smallest expansion Particles vibrate more vigorously about fixed positions; strong intermolecular forces limit expansion
Liquids Greater expansion than solids Particles move faster and further apart; weaker intermolecular forces allow more expansion
Gases Greatest expansion Particles move at high speeds with negligible forces between them; large increase in volume for small temperature rise
Note

Key Concept: The particles themselves do not expand when heated. Instead, the average distance between particles increases, causing the material to occupy a larger volume.

14.2.2 Everyday Applications and Consequences of Thermal Expansion

Application Explanation
Railway tracks Expansion gaps allow rails to expand in hot weather without buckling; modern tracks are tensioned to fit tightly on hot days
Bridges Expansion joints or rollers at one end allow the bridge to expand and contract without damage
Liquid-in-glass thermometers The liquid (alcohol or mercury) expands more than the glass when heated, rising up the capillary tube
Shrink fitting A metal part is cooled to contract, fitted into another part, then warms and expands to form a tight joint
Overhead cables Electrical wires are hung loosely in summer to allow for contraction in winter without snapping
Warning

Important: If expansion is prevented, very large forces can develop. This is why engineers must design structures to accommodate thermal expansion.

14.3 Specific Heat Capacity

14.3.1 Internal Energy and Temperature

Internal energy is the total energy of all the particles in a substance.

  • When the temperature of an object increases, its internal energy increases

  • This is because the average kinetic energy of the particles increases

  • Temperature is a measure of the average kinetic energy of particles

Note

Key Insight: Two objects of the same mass and temperature have the same average particle kinetic energy, but may have different internal energies if they contain different numbers of particles.

14.3.2 What Is Specific Heat Capacity?

When you heat different substances, you notice that:

  • The more mass you heat, the more energy is needed

  • The greater the temperature change required, the more energy is needed

  • Different materials require different amounts of energy for the same temperature change

Specific heat capacity (\(c\)) is defined as:

The energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K).

Formula: \[c = \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta}\]

Where: - \(c\) = specific heat capacity (J/(kg °C) or J/(kg K))

  • \(\Delta E\) = thermal energy supplied (J)

  • \(m\) = mass of substance (kg)

  • \(\Delta\theta\) = temperature change (°C or K)

Rearranged for energy calculations: \[\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta\]

Note

Important: A temperature change of 1 °C is equal to a temperature change of 1 K. Only the zero points of the scales differ.

14.3.3 Specific Heat Capacities of Common Materials

Material Specific Heat Capacity (J/(kg K))
Lead 130
Mercury 140
Brass 380
Copper 400
Iron 460
Glass 670
Aluminium 900
Methylated spirit 2400
Seawater 3900
Water 4200

Key observation: Water has a very high specific heat capacity. This means:

  • Water heats up and cools down slowly compared to most substances

  • This explains why coastal areas have milder climates than inland areas

  • Water is used as a coolant in car engines and power stations

14.4 Worked Examples: Specific Heat Capacity

14.4.1 Example 1: Calculating Energy Required

Question: Calculate the thermal energy required to raise the temperature of 2.0 kg of water from 20 °C to 80 °C. (Take \(c_{\text{water}} = 4200 \text{ J/(kg K)}\))

Solution:

Step 1: Identify values - \(m = 2.0 \text{ kg}\)

  • \(\Delta\theta = 80 - 20 = 60 \text{ °C}\) (or 60 K)

  • \(c = 4200 \text{ J/(kg K)}\)

Step 2: Apply formula \[\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta = 2.0 \times 4200 \times 60 = \boxed{504\,000 \text{ J} = 504 \text{ kJ}}\]

14.4.2 Example 2: Finding Specific Heat Capacity

Question: A 0.50 kg metal block is heated using a 60 W heater for 5.0 minutes. The temperature of the block rises from 25 °C to 85 °C. Calculate the specific heat capacity of the metal. State any assumption made.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate energy supplied by heater \[\Delta E = P \times t = 60 \text{ W} \times (5.0 \times 60) \text{ s} = 18\,000 \text{ J}\]

Step 2: Identify other values - \(m = 0.50 \text{ kg}\)

  • \(\Delta\theta = 85 - 25 = 60 \text{ °C}\)

Step 3: Apply formula (assuming no heat loss to surroundings) \[c = \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta} = \frac{18\,000}{0.50 \times 60} = \boxed{600 \text{ J/(kg K)}}\]

Assumption: All thermal energy from the heater is absorbed by the metal block (no heat loss to surroundings).

14.4.3 Example 3: Temperature Change Calculation

Question: A 1.2 kg aluminium block (\(c = 900 \text{ J/(kg K)}\)) absorbs 27 000 J of thermal energy. Calculate the temperature rise of the block.

Solution:

Step 1: Rearrange formula for \(\Delta\theta\) \[\Delta\theta = \frac{\Delta E}{mc}\]

Step 2: Substitute values \[\Delta\theta = \frac{27\,000}{1.2 \times 900} = \frac{27\,000}{1080} = \boxed{25 \text{ °C}}\]

14.5 11.4 Changes of State: Melting and Boiling

14.5.1 What Happens During a Change of State?

When a substance changes state (e.g., solid → liquid or liquid → gas), energy is supplied but the temperature remains constant.

Heating curve of water:

Temperature/°C
    ^
100 |----------------------- E (boiling: liquid → gas)
    |                       |
    |                       |
    |              D        |
    |             /         |
  0 |----- C     /          |
    |      |    /           |
    |      |   /            |
    |  B   |  /             |
    | /    | /              |
-10 |/     |/               |
    +------------------------> Time
      A
Segment Process Temperature Energy Use
A → B Ice warming Increases from −10 °C to 0 °C Increases kinetic energy of particles
B → C Melting (solid → liquid) Constant at 0 °C Breaks bonds between particles
C → D Water warming Increases from 0 °C to 100 °C Increases kinetic energy of particles
D → E Boiling (liquid → gas) Constant at 100 °C Breaks bonds and overcomes atmospheric pressure
Note

Key Concept: During melting or boiling, the supplied energy is used to overcome intermolecular forces, not to increase particle kinetic energy. This is why temperature remains constant.

14.5.2 Melting and Boiling Points of Water

At standard atmospheric pressure (1 atmosphere):

  • Melting point of pure water: 0 °C

  • Boiling point of pure water: 100 °C

Note: These values change with pressure. For example:

  • At high altitude (lower pressure), water boils below 100 °C

  • In a pressure cooker (higher pressure), water boils above 100 °C

14.6 11.5 Condensation and Solidification

14.6.1 Reverse Processes: Energy Release

When a substance changes from a higher-energy state to a lower-energy state, energy is released:

Process Change of State Energy Transfer Particle Explanation
Condensation Gas → Liquid Energy released to surroundings Particles move closer; attractive forces pull them together, releasing energy
Solidification (Freezing) Liquid → Solid Energy released to surroundings Particles settle into fixed positions; strong bonds form, releasing energy
Warning

Important: The energy released during condensation or freezing is equal to the energy absorbed during boiling or melting for the same mass of substance.

Real-world example: A burn from steam at 100 °C is more severe than a burn from boiling water at 100 °C because:

  1. Steam condenses on the skin, releasing latent heat

  2. The condensed water then cools, releasing additional thermal energy

14.7 11.6 Evaporation

14.7.1 What Is Evaporation?

Evaporation is the change of state from liquid to gas that occurs at the surface of a liquid, at any temperature (not just at the boiling point).

14.7.2 How Does Evaporation Occur? (Kinetic Particle Model)

  1. Particles in a liquid move randomly with a range of kinetic energies

  2. At the surface, some particles have enough kinetic energy to overcome attractive forces from other particles and atmospheric pressure

  3. These high-energy particles escape into the air as gas molecules

  4. The remaining particles have a lower average kinetic energy

  5. Therefore, the temperature of the liquid decreases

Note

Key Insight: Evaporation causes cooling because the most energetic particles escape, lowering the average kinetic energy of the remaining liquid.

14.7.3 Evaporation Causes Cooling: Everyday Examples

Example Explanation
Sweating Sweat evaporates from skin, removing thermal energy and cooling the body
Wet cloth on forehead Evaporation of water from the cloth cools the skin
Drying clothes on a line Water evaporates from fabric; clothes feel cool while drying
Alcohol on skin Alcohol evaporates quickly, causing a noticeable cooling sensation

14.8 11.7 Factors Affecting the Rate of Evaporation

Factor Effect on Evaporation Rate Particle-Level Explanation
Temperature Higher temperature → faster evaporation More particles have sufficient kinetic energy to escape the liquid surface
Surface area Larger surface area → faster evaporation More particles are at the surface and can escape
Air movement Moving air (wind) → faster evaporation Escaped particles are carried away, preventing them from returning to the liquid; maintains a concentration gradient
Humidity Lower humidity → faster evaporation Drier air can accept more water vapour; fewer returning particles
Warning

Important: Evaporation occurs at any temperature, while boiling occurs only at the boiling point throughout the liquid.

14.9 11.8 Boiling vs. Evaporation: Key Differences

Feature Boiling Evaporation
Temperature Occurs at a specific temperature (boiling point) Occurs at any temperature
Location Occurs throughout the liquid (bubbles form) Occurs only at the liquid surface
Rate Rapid Relatively slow
Bubbles Bubbles of vapour form within the liquid No bubbles form
Temperature change Temperature remains constant during boiling Temperature of liquid may decrease
Energy source Requires external heat supply Can occur without external heat (uses internal energy of liquid)

14.10 Key Concepts Summary

14.10.1 Core Concepts

  1. Thermal expansion: Solids, liquids and gases expand when heated; gases expand most, solids least

  2. Applications of expansion: Railway gaps, bridge joints, thermometers, shrink fitting

  3. Internal energy: Total energy of all particles; increases with temperature

  4. Specific heat capacity: \(c = \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta}\); energy to raise 1 kg by 1 °C

  5. Changes of state: Melting and boiling occur at constant temperature; energy breaks intermolecular bonds

  6. Melting/boiling points of water: 0 °C and 100 °C at standard atmospheric pressure

  7. Condensation/solidification: Reverse processes that release energy to surroundings

  8. Evaporation: Surface phenomenon at any temperature; causes cooling

14.10.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Particle explanation of expansion: Increased particle motion increases average separation; weaker forces in liquids/gases allow greater expansion

  2. Temperature and kinetic energy: Temperature measures average kinetic energy of particles

  3. Specific heat capacity experiments: Electrical method using \(IVt = mc\Delta\theta\) (with insulation to minimise heat loss)

  4. Boiling vs. evaporation: Differences in location, temperature dependence, rate, and bubble formation

  5. Factors affecting evaporation: Temperature, surface area, air movement, humidity

  6. Cooling by evaporation: High-energy particles escape, lowering average kinetic energy of remaining liquid

14.11 Common Misconceptions

14.11.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Particles expand when heated”

Incorrect: “When a metal rod is heated, the metal particles get bigger”

Correct:

  • Particles themselves do not change size when heated

  • The average distance between particles increases due to more vigorous vibration/motion

  • This increased separation causes the material to expand

14.11.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Temperature increases during melting or boiling”

Incorrect: “When ice melts, its temperature rises from 0 °C”

Correct:

  • During a change of state, temperature remains constant even though energy is supplied

  • Energy is used to overcome intermolecular forces, not to increase particle kinetic energy

  • Temperature only rises once the change of state is complete

14.11.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Evaporation and boiling are the same process”

Incorrect: “Evaporation is just slow boiling”

Correct:

  • Boiling: Occurs at boiling point, throughout liquid, with bubble formation

  • Evaporation: Occurs at any temperature, only at surface, no bubbles

  • Different particle mechanisms: boiling requires energy to overcome atmospheric pressure throughout; evaporation involves only surface particles with sufficient energy

14.11.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Specific heat capacity depends on the amount of substance”

Incorrect: “A larger mass of water has a higher specific heat capacity”

Correct:

  • Specific heat capacity is an intensive property—it depends only on the material, not the amount

  • 1 kg and 10 kg of water both have \(c = 4200 \text{ J/(kg K)}\)

  • The total energy required depends on mass, but \(c\) itself does not

14.11.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Evaporation only happens on hot days”

Incorrect: “Water only evaporates when it is warm”

Correct:

  • Evaporation occurs at any temperature, even below 0 °C (e.g., ice sublimating)

  • The rate of evaporation increases with temperature, but the process itself occurs at all temperatures

  • Puddles dry up on cold days, just more slowly

14.11.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Condensation is the same as evaporation, just reversed”

Incorrect: “Condensation is evaporation going backwards”

Correct:

  • While condensation is the reverse change of state (gas → liquid), the energy transfer is opposite

  • Evaporation absorbs energy from the liquid (causing cooling)

  • Condensation releases energy to the surroundings (causing warming)

  • Different particle mechanisms: evaporation involves escape of high-energy particles; condensation involves capture of particles by attractive forces

14.11.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “All materials expand by the same amount when heated”

Incorrect: “Heating any material by 10 °C causes the same expansion”

Correct:

  • Different materials have different coefficients of thermal expansion

  • For the same temperature change: gases expand most, liquids less, solids least

  • Even among solids, aluminium expands more than steel for the same temperature rise

  • Engineers must select materials with compatible expansion properties for combined structures

14.12 Worked Examples

14.12.1 Example 4: Thermal Expansion Application

Question: A steel railway rail is 30.0 m long at 10 °C. If the coefficient of linear expansion for steel is \(1.2 \times 10^{-5} \text{ /°C}\), calculate the increase in length when the temperature rises to 40 °C.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify values - Original length \(L_0 = 30.0 \text{ m}\)

  • Temperature change \(\Delta\theta = 40 - 10 = 30 \text{ °C}\)

  • Coefficient \(\alpha = 1.2 \times 10^{-5} \text{ /°C}\)

Step 2: Apply linear expansion formula \[\Delta L = \alpha L_0 \Delta\theta = (1.2 \times 10^{-5}) \times 30.0 \times 30 = \boxed{0.0108 \text{ m} = 1.08 \text{ cm}}\]

Interpretation: The rail expands by just over 1 cm. Without expansion gaps, this could cause buckling.

14.12.2 Example 5: Specific Heat Capacity Comparison

Question: Equal masses of aluminium (\(c = 900 \text{ J/(kg K)}\)) and copper (\(c = 400 \text{ J/(kg K)}\)) are supplied with the same amount of thermal energy. Which metal experiences the greater temperature rise? Explain.

Solution:

Step 1: Recall formula \[\Delta\theta = \frac{\Delta E}{mc}\]

Step 2: Analyse - \(\Delta E\) and \(m\) are the same for both metals

  • \(\Delta\theta\) is inversely proportional to \(c\)

  • Copper has lower \(c\) (400 < 900)

Conclusion: Copper experiences the greater temperature rise because it requires less energy per kg per degree.

Quantitative comparison: \[\frac{\Delta\theta_{\text{Cu}}}{\Delta\theta_{\text{Al}}} = \frac{c_{\text{Al}}}{c_{\text{Cu}}} = \frac{900}{400} = 2.25\]

Copper’s temperature rises 2.25 times more than aluminium’s for the same energy input.

14.12.3 Example 6: Evaporation Cooling Calculation

Question: A person sweats 0.5 kg of water during exercise. If the specific latent heat of vaporisation of water is \(2.3 \times 10^6 \text{ J/kg}\), calculate the thermal energy removed from the body by evaporation.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify values - Mass of water \(m = 0.5 \text{ kg}\)

  • Specific latent heat \(L_v = 2.3 \times 10^6 \text{ J/kg}\)

Step 2: Calculate energy removed \[\Delta E = mL_v = 0.5 \times 2.3 \times 10^6 = \boxed{1.15 \times 10^6 \text{ J} = 1150 \text{ kJ}}\]

Interpretation: Evaporation of sweat removes over 1 MJ of thermal energy, providing significant cooling.

14.14 Laboratory Investigation

14.14.1 Experiment 11.1: Measuring the Specific Heat Capacity of a Solid

Objective: To determine the specific heat capacity of a metal block using an electrical heating method.

Apparatus: - Metal block with holes for heater and thermometer

  • Electrical immersion heater (known power rating)

  • Thermometer or temperature sensor with data logger

  • d.c. power supply, ammeter, voltmeter

  • Insulating material (felt or polystyrene)

  • Electronic balance

  • Stopwatch

Variables: - Independent: Time of heating (or energy supplied)

  • Dependent: Temperature change of block

  • Controlled: Mass of block, insulation, initial temperature, heater power

Procedure: 1. Measure and record the mass \(m\) of the metal block.

  1. Wrap the block in insulating material to minimise heat loss.

  2. Insert the heater and thermometer into the drilled holes.

  3. Connect the heater to the power supply via ammeter and voltmeter.

  4. Record the initial temperature \(\theta_1\).

  5. Switch on the heater and start the stopwatch simultaneously.

  6. After a measured time \(t\) (e.g., 300 s), switch off the heater.

  7. Continue monitoring temperature and record the highest temperature \(\theta_2\) reached.

  8. Record the ammeter reading \(I\) and voltmeter reading \(V\) during heating.

Data Table:

Quantity Symbol Value Unit
Mass of block \(m\) kg
Initial temperature \(\theta_1\) °C
Final temperature \(\theta_2\) °C
Temperature change \(\Delta\theta = \theta_2 - \theta_1\) °C
Current \(I\) A
Voltage \(V\) V
Time \(t\) s
Energy supplied \(\Delta E = IVt\) J

Calculations: 1. Energy supplied by heater: \(\Delta E = IVt\)

  1. Assuming all energy is absorbed by block: \(\Delta E = mc\Delta\theta\)

  2. Specific heat capacity: \(c = \frac{IVt}{m\Delta\theta}\)

Precautions: - ✓ Insulate the block well to minimise heat loss to surroundings

  • ✓ Ensure good thermal contact between heater/thermometer and block

  • ✓ Stir (if liquid) or wait for temperature to stabilise before reading

  • ✓ Use a data logger for more accurate temperature tracking

Sources of Error: - Heat loss to surroundings (even with insulation)

  • Energy absorbed by heater and thermometer, not just block

  • Temperature not uniform throughout block during heating

  • Delay between switching off heater and recording maximum temperature

Conclusion Template: “The specific heat capacity of the metal was determined to be ______ J/(kg K). This value is [close to/different from] the accepted value for [metal name], which may be due to [heat loss/measurement uncertainties/etc.].”

Extension (Supplement): - Repeat the experiment with a liquid (using a polystyrene cup)

  • Investigate how the calculated \(c\) changes with different heating times (to assess heat loss effects)

  • Compare results for different metals to explore the relationship between \(c\) and material properties

14.16 Chapter Summary

14.16.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Thermal expansion: All materials expand when heated; gases > liquids > solids

✓ Expansion applications: Railway gaps, bridge joints, thermometers, shrink fitting

✓ Internal energy: Total energy of particles; increases with temperature

✓ Specific heat capacity: \(c = \frac{\Delta E}{m\Delta\theta}\); material property, not mass-dependent

✓ Changes of state: Melting/boiling at constant temperature; energy breaks intermolecular bonds

✓ Water phase changes: Melts at 0 °C, boils at 100 °C (standard pressure)

✓ Condensation/solidification: Release energy to surroundings

✓ Evaporation: Surface phenomenon at any temperature; causes cooling

✓ Evaporation factors: ↑ temperature, ↑ surface area, ↑ air movement, ↓ humidity → faster evaporation

✓ Boiling vs. evaporation: Different in location, temperature dependence, rate, bubble formation

14.18 Glossary

Term Definition
Boiling Change of state from liquid to gas occurring throughout the liquid at the boiling point; bubbles of vapour form
Condensation Change of state from gas to liquid; energy is released to the surroundings
Evaporation Change of state from liquid to gas occurring only at the surface, at any temperature; causes cooling
Internal energy Total energy (kinetic + potential) of all particles in a substance
Melting Change of state from solid to liquid at the melting point; energy is absorbed to break bonds
Specific heat capacity (\(c\)) Energy required to raise the temperature of 1 kg of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K); unit: J/(kg K)
Solidification (Freezing) Change of state from liquid to solid; energy is released as particles form fixed positions
Thermal expansion Increase in volume of a substance when heated, due to increased average separation of particles
Temperature Measure of the average kinetic energy of particles in a substance
