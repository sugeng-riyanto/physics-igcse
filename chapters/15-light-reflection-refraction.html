18.2 Introduction to Light

18.2.1 What Is Light?

Light is a form of electromagnetic radiation that is visible to the human eye. It travels in straight lines and can be reflected, refracted, and dispersed.

Key properties of light:

  • Light travels at approximately \(3.0 \times 10^8\) m/s in a vacuum

  • Light travels in straight lines (rectilinear propagation)

  • Light can be reflected, refracted, absorbed, or transmitted

  • Light exhibits both wave and particle properties (beyond IGCSE scope)

Note

Key Concept: We see objects because light from them enters our eyes. Luminous objects (e.g., the Sun, a lamp) emit their own light. Non-luminous objects (e.g., a book, a wall) are visible because they reflect light from a luminous source into our eyes.

18.2.2 Representing Light: Rays and Beams

In physics, we use light rays to represent the path of light:

Term Definition Diagram Representation
Ray A straight line with an arrow indicating the direction of light travel Single line with arrow
Beam A bundle of parallel, convergent, or divergent rays Multiple parallel/convergent/divergent lines
Parallel beam Rays that are parallel to each other Parallel lines with arrows
Convergent beam Rays that meet at a point Lines converging to a point
Divergent beam Rays that spread out from a point Lines spreading from a point

18.3 Reflection of Light

18.3.1 The Law of Reflection

When light strikes a smooth, polished surface (e.g., a plane mirror), it is reflected in a predictable manner.

Key terms for reflection:

  • Incident ray: The ray of light that strikes the reflecting surface

  • Reflected ray: The ray of light that bounces off the reflecting surface

  • Point of incidence: The point where the incident ray strikes the surface

  • Normal: An imaginary line perpendicular to the reflecting surface at the point of incidence

  • Angle of incidence (\(i\)): The angle between the incident ray and the normal

  • Angle of reflection (\(r\)): The angle between the reflected ray and the normal

The Law of Reflection states:

  1. The angle of incidence is equal to the angle of reflection: \(i = r\)

  2. The incident ray, reflected ray, and normal all lie in the same plane

Warning

Important: Angles are always measured from the normal, not from the surface itself.

18.3.2 Images Formed by Plane Mirrors

When you look into a plane mirror, you see an image of yourself. This image has specific characteristics:

Characteristic Description
Same size The image is the same size as the object
Same distance The image is as far behind the mirror as the object is in front
Virtual The image cannot be captured on a screen; light rays do not actually meet at the image position
Upright The image is the right way up (not inverted)
Laterally inverted Left and right are swapped (e.g., text appears reversed)

18.3.3 Ray Diagrams for Plane Mirrors

To locate the position of an image formed by a plane mirror:

Step 1: Draw the mirror as a straight line with shading on the back (silvered side).

Step 2: Mark the object position in front of the mirror.

Step 3: Draw the image behind the mirror at the same distance as the object is in front.

Step 4: From the top of the image, draw two rays to the eye (dotted lines behind the mirror, solid lines in front).

Step 5: Draw the incident rays from the object to the mirror, ensuring \(i = r\) at each point of incidence.

Note

Key Insight: The image is virtual because the rays only appear to come from behind the mirror. In reality, the light rays reflect off the mirror and enter the eye.

18.3.4 Applications of Plane Mirrors

Application Explanation
Periscope Two plane mirrors at 45° allow viewing over obstacles (e.g., in submarines)
Rear-view mirrors Drivers use plane or slightly curved mirrors to see behind vehicles
Optical instruments Mirrors redirect light in telescopes, microscopes, and cameras
Vision testing Mirrors extend the apparent distance of eye charts in small rooms
Blind corner mirrors Curved mirrors help drivers see around corners in shops or on roads

18.4 Refraction of Light

18.4.1 What Is Refraction?

Refraction is the bending of light as it passes from one transparent medium to another with a different optical density.

Why does refraction occur?

  • Light travels at different speeds in different media

  • When light enters a denser medium (e.g., air → glass), it slows down and bends towards the normal

  • When light enters a less dense medium (e.g., glass → air), it speeds up and bends away from the normal

Key terms for refraction:

  • Incident ray: The ray approaching the boundary between two media

  • Refracted ray: The ray that has passed into the second medium and changed direction

  • Normal: An imaginary line perpendicular to the boundary at the point of incidence

  • Angle of incidence (\(i\)): The angle between the incident ray and the normal

  • Angle of refraction (\(r\)): The angle between the refracted ray and the normal

18.4.2 Snell’s Law and Refractive Index

For light passing from air (or vacuum) into another medium:

\[n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\]

Where: - \(n\) = refractive index of the medium (no unit) - \(i\) = angle of incidence in air/vacuum - \(r\) = angle of refraction in the medium

Refractive index can also be defined as:

\[n = \frac{\text{speed of light in vacuum}}{\text{speed of light in medium}} = \frac{c}{v}\]

Medium Refractive Index (\(n\)) Speed of Light (\(\times 10^8\) m/s)
Vacuum 1.00 (exact) 3.00
Air ≈ 1.00 ≈ 3.00
Water 1.33 2.25
Glass ≈ 1.50 ≈ 2.00
Diamond 2.42 1.24
Note

Key Insight: A higher refractive index means light travels slower in that medium and bends more towards the normal when entering from air.

18.4.3 Apparent Depth and Real Depth

When viewing an object underwater, it appears shallower than it actually is due to refraction.

Relationship: \[n = \frac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}\]

Example: A coin at the bottom of a pool appears closer to the surface than it really is. Light rays from the coin refract away from the normal as they leave the water, making the coin appear at a shallower position.

18.5 Total Internal Reflection

18.5.1 What Is the Critical Angle?

When light travels from a denser medium to a less dense medium (e.g., glass → air):

  • As the angle of incidence increases, the angle of refraction increases more rapidly

  • At a certain angle of incidence, the angle of refraction becomes 90° (the refracted ray travels along the boundary)

  • This specific angle of incidence is called the critical angle (\(c\))

Definition: The critical angle is the angle of incidence in the denser medium for which the angle of refraction in the less dense medium is 90°.

18.5.2 Total Internal Reflection

When the angle of incidence in the denser medium exceeds the critical angle:

  • No refraction occurs

  • All the light is reflected back into the denser medium

  • This phenomenon is called total internal reflection

Conditions for total internal reflection:

  1. Light must travel from a denser medium to a less dense medium

  2. The angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle

18.5.3 Calculating Critical Angle

For light travelling from a medium of refractive index \(n\) to air:

\[n = \frac{1}{\sin c} \quad \text{or} \quad \sin c = \frac{1}{n}\]

Example: For glass with \(n = 1.5\): \[\sin c = \frac{1}{1.5} = 0.667 \quad \Rightarrow \quad c = \sin^{-1}(0.667) \approx 42^\circ\]

18.5.4 Applications of Total Internal Reflection

Application Explanation
Optical fibres Light is trapped inside a glass/plastic core by total internal reflection; used for high-speed data transmission in telecommunications
Prisms in binoculars Right-angled prisms reflect light by total internal reflection, producing upright images without silvered surfaces
Periscopes Prisms can replace mirrors for clearer, more durable image reflection
Endoscopes Flexible optical fibres allow doctors to see inside the body without invasive surgery
Reflectors on bicycles Prismatic reflectors return light to its source using total internal reflection
Warning

Important: Total internal reflection is 100% efficient (no light is lost), unlike reflection from a silvered mirror which absorbs some light.

18.6 Key Concepts Summary

18.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Reflection: Light bounces off a surface; \(i = r\); incident ray, reflected ray, and normal lie in same plane

  2. Plane mirror image: Same size, same distance behind mirror, virtual, upright, laterally inverted

  3. Refraction: Light bends when passing between media of different optical density due to change in speed

  4. Snell’s Law: \(n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\) for light entering from air/vacuum

  5. Refractive index: \(n = \frac{c}{v}\); higher \(n\) means slower light speed and greater bending

  6. Critical angle: Angle of incidence in denser medium for which refraction angle = 90°

  7. Total internal reflection: Complete reflection when \(i > c\) and light travels denser → less dense medium

18.6.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Apparent depth: \(n = \frac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}\); objects underwater appear shallower

  2. Optical fibres: Use total internal reflection to transmit light/data over long distances with minimal loss

  3. Ray diagram constructions: Using geometry and \(i = r\) to locate images and trace light paths

18.7 Common Misconceptions

18.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “The angle of incidence is measured from the mirror surface”

Incorrect: “Angle of incidence is the angle between the incident ray and the mirror”

Correct: - Angles of incidence and reflection are always measured from the normal (perpendicular to the surface)

  • Measuring from the surface gives the complement of the correct angle

  • This error leads to incorrect ray diagrams and calculations

18.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Virtual images can be captured on a screen”

Incorrect: “A mirror image can be projected onto a wall”

Correct: - Virtual images are formed where light rays appear to come from, but do not actually meet

  • Only real images (where rays actually converge) can be captured on a screen

  • Plane mirrors always produce virtual images

18.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Refraction changes the frequency of light”

Incorrect: “When light enters glass, its frequency decreases”

Correct: - Frequency remains constant during refraction

  • Speed and wavelength change, but frequency (determined by the source) does not

  • This is why the colour of light does not change when it refracts

18.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Total internal reflection can occur when light travels from air to glass”

Incorrect: “Light can undergo total internal reflection entering a denser medium”

Correct: - Total internal reflection only occurs when light travels from a denser to a less dense medium

  • When entering a denser medium (air → glass), light always refracts (bends towards normal)

  • The critical angle concept only applies for denser → less dense transitions

18.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “A higher refractive index means light travels faster”

Incorrect: “Diamond has a high refractive index, so light travels fast in it”

Correct: - Refractive index \(n = \frac{c}{v}\); higher \(n\) means slower light speed in the medium

  • Diamond (\(n = 2.42\)) slows light to about \(1.24 \times 10^8\) m/s, much slower than in air

  • Greater slowing causes greater bending towards the normal

18.7.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Lateral inversion means the image is upside down”

Incorrect: “A mirror turns your image upside down”

Correct: - Lateral inversion means left and right are swapped, not top and bottom

  • Your right hand appears as the left hand in the mirror image

  • The image remains upright; only the horizontal orientation is reversed

18.7.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “Critical angle is the same for all materials”

Incorrect: “All glass has a critical angle of 42°”

Correct: - Critical angle depends on refractive index: \(\sin c = \frac{1}{n}\)

  • Different types of glass have different \(n\) values (1.48–1.96), so critical angles vary (~32°–43°)

  • Water (\(n = 1.33\)) has \(c \approx 49^\circ\); diamond (\(n = 2.42\)) has \(c \approx 24^\circ\)

18.8 Worked Examples

18.8.1 Example 1: Law of Reflection Calculation

Question: A ray of light strikes a plane mirror at an angle of 35° to the normal. (a) What is the angle of reflection? (b) What is the angle between the incident ray and the reflected ray?

Solution:

(a) Angle of reflection: By the law of reflection: \(r = i = \boxed{35^\circ}\)

(b) Angle between incident and reflected rays: Angle = \(i + r = 35^\circ + 35^\circ = \boxed{70^\circ}\)

18.8.2 Example 2: Plane Mirror Image Distance

Question: A person stands 2.5 m in front of a plane mirror. (a) How far is the image from the mirror? (b) How far is the person from their image? (c) If the person moves 0.5 m closer to the mirror, what is the new distance between the person and their image?

Solution:

(a) Image distance from mirror: Image distance = object distance = \(\boxed{2.5 \text{ m}}\)

(b) Person-to-image distance: Distance = \(2.5 + 2.5 = \boxed{5.0 \text{ m}}\)

(c) New distance after moving closer: New object distance = \(2.5 - 0.5 = 2.0 \text{ m}\) New image distance = \(2.0 \text{ m}\) New person-to-image distance = \(2.0 + 2.0 = \boxed{4.0 \text{ m}}\)

18.8.3 Example 3: Snell’s Law Calculation

Question: Light travels from air into glass (\(n = 1.50\)) at an angle of incidence of 40°. Calculate the angle of refraction.

Solution:

Step 1: Write Snell’s Law: \[n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\]

Step 2: Rearrange for \(\sin r\): \[\sin r = \frac{\sin i}{n} = \frac{\sin 40^\circ}{1.50}\]

Step 3: Calculate: \[\sin r = \frac{0.643}{1.50} = 0.429\] \[r = \sin^{-1}(0.429) = \boxed{25.4^\circ}\]

Interpretation: The ray bends towards the normal as expected when entering a denser medium.

18.8.4 Example 4: Critical Angle and Total Internal Reflection

Question: The refractive index of a certain type of glass is 1.60. (a) Calculate the critical angle for this glass. (b) A ray of light inside this glass strikes the glass-air boundary at an angle of incidence of 45°. Will total internal reflection occur? Explain.

Solution:

(a) Critical angle: \[\sin c = \frac{1}{n} = \frac{1}{1.60} = 0.625\] \[c = \sin^{-1}(0.625) = \boxed{38.7^\circ}\]

(b) Will TIR occur? - Angle of incidence \(i = 45^\circ\)

  • Critical angle \(c = 38.7^\circ\)

  • Since \(i > c\) (\(45^\circ > 38.7^\circ\)) and light is travelling from denser (glass) to less dense (air) medium:

  • Yes, total internal reflection will occur.

18.8.5 Example 5: Apparent Depth Calculation

Question: A fish is actually 1.2 m below the surface of a pond. The refractive index of water is 1.33. Calculate the apparent depth of the fish as seen by an observer directly above.

Solution:

Step 1: Use the apparent depth formula: \[n = \frac{\text{real depth}}{\text{apparent depth}}\]

Step 2: Rearrange for apparent depth: \[\text{apparent depth} = \frac{\text{real depth}}{n} = \frac{1.2}{1.33} = \boxed{0.90 \text{ m}}\]

Interpretation: The fish appears to be only 0.90 m deep, not 1.2 m, due to refraction at the water-air boundary.

18.10 Laboratory Investigation

18.10.1 Experiment 15.1: Investigating Refraction Using a Rectangular Glass Block

Objective: To verify Snell’s Law and determine the refractive index of glass by measuring angles of incidence and refraction.

Apparatus: - Rectangular glass block

  • Ray box with single slit

  • White paper

  • Protractor

  • Ruler

  • Pencil

  • Optical pins (optional alternative method)

Variables: - Independent: Angle of incidence (\(i\))

  • Dependent: Angle of refraction (\(r\))

  • Controlled: Same glass block, same wavelength of light, same experimental setup

Procedure: 1. Place the glass block on a sheet of white paper and trace its outline.

  1. Remove the block and draw a normal line at the centre of one of the longer faces.

  2. Draw an incident ray at an angle of incidence \(i = 30^\circ\) to the normal.

  3. Replace the glass block exactly on its outline.

  4. Direct a narrow ray of light from the ray box along the incident ray line.

  5. Mark the point where the ray emerges from the opposite face of the block.

  6. Remove the block and draw the refracted ray inside the block and the emergent ray.

  7. Measure the angle of refraction \(r\) inside the block.

  8. Repeat steps 3–8 for angles of incidence: 40°, 50°, 60°, 70°.

  9. Record results in a table and calculate \(\sin i\), \(\sin r\), and \(\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\) for each trial.

Data Table:

Trial \(i\) (°) \(r\) (°) \(\sin i\) \(\sin r\) \(n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\)
1 30
2 40
3 50
4 60
5 70

Analysis: 1. Calculate the average value of \(n\) from your results.

  1. Plot a graph of \(\sin i\) (y-axis) against \(\sin r\) (x-axis).

  2. Determine the gradient of the best-fit line; this should equal the refractive index \(n\).

  3. Compare your experimental \(n\) with the accepted value for glass (~1.50).

Precautions: - ✓ Ensure the glass block is placed exactly on its outline for each trial

  • ✓ Use a narrow, well-defined ray for accurate angle measurement

  • ✓ Measure angles from the normal, not from the surface

  • ✓ Avoid parallax error when reading the protractor

Expected Results: - The graph of \(\sin i\) against \(\sin r\) should be a straight line through the origin

  • The gradient should be approximately 1.50 for typical glass

  • The emergent ray should be parallel to the incident ray but laterally displaced

Conclusion Template: “The experiment verified Snell’s Law: the ratio \(\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\) was approximately constant, giving a refractive index of ______ for the glass block. This value is [close to/different from] the accepted value of 1.50, which may be due to [measurement uncertainties/variations in glass composition/etc.].”

Extension (Supplement): - Repeat the experiment with a semicircular glass block to study the critical angle

  • Investigate refraction for light travelling from glass to air to observe total internal reflection

  • Compare refractive indices of different transparent materials (e.g., acrylic, water)

18.12 Chapter Summary

18.12.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Reflection: \(i = r\); incident ray, reflected ray, normal in same plane

✓ Plane mirror image: Same size, same distance behind mirror, virtual, upright, laterally inverted

✓ Refraction: Light bends due to change in speed when passing between media

✓ Snell’s Law: \(n = \frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\) for air/vacuum → medium

✓ Refractive index: \(n = \frac{c}{v}\); higher \(n\) = slower light = greater bending

✓ Critical angle: \(\sin c = \frac{1}{n}\); angle for which refraction angle = 90°

✓ Total internal reflection: Occurs when \(i > c\) and light travels denser → less dense

✓ Applications: Optical fibres, prisms, endoscopes, reflectors

Normal An imaginary line perpendicular to a surface or boundary at the point of incidence
Refracted ray The ray of light that has passed into a second medium and changed direction
Refractive index (\(n\)) Ratio of speed of light in vacuum to speed in medium; or \(\frac{\sin i}{\sin r}\) for air → medium
Reflection The rebounding of light at a surface; governed by \(i = r\)
Refraction The bending of light as it passes from one medium to another due to a change in speed
Total internal reflection Complete reflection of light within a denser medium when \(i > c\) at a boundary with a less dense medium
Virtual image An image formed where light rays appear to come from but do not actually meet; cannot be captured on a screen
