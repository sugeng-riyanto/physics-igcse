25.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (5.1.1 The Atom):

  • Describe the structure of an atom in terms of a positively charged nucleus and negatively charged electrons in orbit around the nucleus

  • Know how atoms may form positive ions by losing electrons or form negative ions by gaining electrons

Core Content (5.1.2 The Nucleus):

  • Describe the composition of the nucleus in terms of protons and neutrons

  • State the relative charges of protons, neutrons and electrons as +1, 0 and –1 respectively

  • Define the terms proton number (atomic number) Z and nucleon number (mass number) A and be able to calculate the number of neutrons in a nucleus

  • Use the nuclide notation \(^A_ZX\)

  • Explain what is meant by an isotope and state that an element may have more than one isotope

Core Content (5.2.1 Detection of Radioactivity):

  • Know what is meant by background radiation

  • Know the sources that make a significant contribution to background radiation including: radon gas (in the air), rocks and buildings, food and drink, cosmic rays

  • Know that ionising nuclear radiation can be measured using a detector connected to a counter

  • Use count rate measured in counts/s or counts/min

Core Content (5.2.2 The Three Types of Nuclear Emission):

  • Describe the emission of radiation from a nucleus as spontaneous and random in direction

  • Identify alpha (α-), beta (β-) and gamma (γ-) emissions from the nucleus by recalling:

      1. their nature
      1. their relative ionising effects
      1. their relative penetrating abilities (β+ are not included, β-particles will be taken to refer to β–)

Core Content (5.2.3 Radioactive Decay):

  • Know that radioactive decay is a change in an unstable nucleus that can result in the emission of α-particles or β-particles and/or γ-radiation and know that these changes are spontaneous and random

  • State that during α-decay or β-decay, the nucleus changes to that of a different element

Core Content (5.2.4 Half-life):

  • Define the half-life of a particular isotope as the time taken for half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay; recall and use this definition in simple calculations, which might involve information in tables or decay curves (calculations will not include background radiation)

Core Content (5.2.5 Safety Precautions):

  • State the effects of ionising nuclear radiations on living things, including cell death, mutations and cancer

  • Describe how radioactive materials are moved, used and stored in a safe way

Supplement (Extended):

  • Describe how the scattering of alpha (α) particles by a sheet of thin metal supports the nuclear model of the atom, by providing evidence for:

      1. a very small nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space
      1. a nucleus containing most of the mass of the atom
      1. a nucleus that is positively charged

  • Describe the processes of nuclear fission and nuclear fusion as the splitting or joining of nuclei, to include the nuclide equation and qualitative description of mass and energy changes without values

  • Know the relationship between the proton number and the relative charge on a nucleus

  • Know the relationship between the nucleon number and the relative mass of a nucleus

  • Describe the deflection of α-particles, β-particles and γ-radiation in electric fields and magnetic fields

  • Explain their relative ionising effects with reference to:

      1. kinetic energy
      1. electric charge

  • Know that isotopes of an element may be radioactive due to an excess of neutrons in the nucleus and/or the nucleus being too heavy

  • Describe the effect of α-decay, β-decay and γ-emissions on the nucleus, including an increase in stability and a reduction in the number of excess neutrons; the following change in the nucleus occurs during β-emission: neutron → proton + electron

  • Use decay equations, using nuclide notation, to show the emission of α-particles, β-particles and γ-radiation

  • Calculate half-life from data or decay curves from which background radiation has not been subtracted

  • Explain how the type of radiation emitted and the half-life of an isotope determine which isotope is used for applications including:

      1. household fire (smoke) alarms
      1. irradiating food to kill bacteria
      1. sterilisation of equipment using gamma rays
      1. measuring and controlling thicknesses of materials with the choice of radiations used linked to penetration and absorption
      1. diagnosis and treatment of cancer using gamma rays

  • Explain safety precautions for all ionising radiation in terms of reducing exposure time, increasing distance between source and living tissue and using shielding to absorb radiation

25.2 The Nuclear Model of the Atom

25.2.1 What Is an Atom?

An atom is the smallest unit of a chemical element that retains the chemical properties of that element. However, atoms are not indivisible — they consist of even smaller particles.

Structure of an atom:

  • Nucleus: A small, dense, positively charged centre containing protons and neutrons

  • Electrons: Negatively charged particles that orbit the nucleus at relatively large distances

                    Electron orbit
                    ↗
    ┌─────────────────────────┐
    │                         │
    │    ● ● ● ● ● ● ● ●     │  ← Electrons in orbit
    │                         │
    │         [Nucleus]       │  ← Protons (+) and neutrons (0)
    │                         │
    └─────────────────────────┘
Note

Key Concept: The nucleus contains almost all the mass of the atom but occupies a tiny fraction of its volume. If an atom were the size of a football stadium, the nucleus would be about the size of a marble at the centre!

25.2.2 Relative Charges and Masses of Subatomic Particles

Particle Relative Charge Relative Mass (atomic mass units) Location
Proton +1 1 Nucleus
Neutron 0 1 Nucleus
Electron –1 \(\frac{1}{2000}\) (≈ 0.0005) Orbiting nucleus
Warning

Important: The relative mass of an electron is so small that it is often considered negligible when calculating the mass of an atom. The mass of an atom is essentially the mass of its nucleus.

25.2.3 Proton Number and Nucleon Number

Proton number (atomic number) Z:

  • The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom

  • Determines the identity of the element (e.g., all carbon atoms have Z = 6)

  • In a neutral atom, the number of electrons equals the proton number

Nucleon number (mass number) A:

  • The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus

  • A = number of protons + number of neutrons

Calculating the number of neutrons:

\[\text{Number of neutrons} = A - Z\]

25.2.4 Nuclide Notation

Atoms are represented using nuclide notation:

\[^A_ZX\]

Where: - \(X\) = chemical symbol of the element - \(A\) = nucleon number (mass number) - \(Z\) = proton number (atomic number)

Example: Carbon-12 is written as \(^{12}_6\text{C}\)

  • 6 protons in the nucleus
  • 12 – 6 = 6 neutrons in the nucleus
  • 6 electrons orbiting the nucleus (in a neutral atom)

25.2.5 Isotopes

Isotopes are atoms of the same element that have:

  • The same proton number (same number of protons)

  • Different nucleon numbers (different numbers of neutrons)

Example: Isotopes of carbon

Isotope Nuclide Notation Protons Neutrons Electrons
Carbon-12 \(^{12}_6\text{C}\) 6 6 6
Carbon-13 \(^{13}_6\text{C}\) 6 7 6
Carbon-14 \(^{14}_6\text{C}\) 6 8 6
Note

Key Insight: Isotopes of the same element have identical chemical properties (because they have the same number of electrons) but may have different physical properties (e.g., mass, stability).

25.2.6 Formation of Ions

Atoms can gain or lose electrons to form ions:

Positive ions (cations):

  • Formed when an atom loses electrons

  • Has more protons than electrons → net positive charge

  • Example: Sodium atom (Na) loses 1 electron → sodium ion (Na\(^+\))

Negative ions (anions):

  • Formed when an atom gains electrons

  • Has more electrons than protons → net negative charge

  • Example: Chlorine atom (Cl) gains 1 electron → chloride ion (Cl\(^-\))

Warning

Important: Only electrons are transferred when atoms form ions. Protons remain fixed in the nucleus and do not participate in ion formation.

25.2.7 Evidence for the Nuclear Model: Rutherford’s Scattering Experiment

In 1911, Ernest Rutherford, with his assistants Geiger and Marsden, conducted an experiment that provided crucial evidence for the nuclear model of the atom.

The experiment:

  1. A beam of alpha (α) particles was directed at a very thin sheet of gold foil

  2. A fluorescent screen surrounding the foil detected where the α-particles landed

  3. The scattering pattern was observed and recorded

Observations:

  • Most α-particles passed straight through the foil with little or no deflection

  • Some α-particles were deflected at small angles

  • A very few α-particles (about 1 in 8000) were deflected at angles greater than 90° — some even bounced straight back!

Conclusions:

Observation Conclusion
Most α-particles passed through The atom is mostly empty space
Some α-particles deflected at small angles The nucleus is positively charged (like charges repel)
Very few α-particles deflected at large angles The nucleus is very small and contains most of the mass
Note

Key Concept: Rutherford’s experiment overturned the earlier “plum pudding” model and established that atoms have a tiny, dense, positively charged nucleus surrounded by mostly empty space where electrons orbit.

25.3 Radioactivity

25.3.1 What Is Radioactivity?

Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of radiation from the nucleus of an unstable atom.

Key characteristics:

  • Spontaneous: Cannot be influenced by external factors (temperature, pressure, chemical state)

  • Random: Impossible to predict which nucleus will decay next or when it will decay

  • Ionising: The emitted radiation has enough energy to knock electrons from atoms, forming ions

25.3.2 Background Radiation

Background radiation is ionising nuclear radiation that is present in the environment from natural and artificial sources, even when no radioactive source is deliberately introduced.

Sources of background radiation:

Source Contribution Description
Radon gas (in air) ~50% Radioactive gas from decay of uranium in rocks; can accumulate in buildings
Rocks and buildings ~15% Natural radioactive materials (uranium, thorium) in soil and construction materials
Food and drink ~10% Naturally occurring radioactive isotopes (e.g., potassium-40 in bananas)
Cosmic rays ~10% High-energy particles from space; intensity increases with altitude
Medical procedures ~10% X-rays, nuclear medicine scans, radiotherapy
Other artificial sources ~5% Nuclear power, fallout from weapons testing, consumer products
Warning

Important: Background radiation levels vary by location. Areas with granite rock or high altitude have higher background radiation. However, typical levels are well below those that cause harm.

25.3.3 Measuring Radioactivity

Geiger-Müller (GM) tube and counter:

  • Detects ionising radiation by measuring the electrical pulses produced when radiation ionises gas in the tube

  • Displays count rate in counts per second (counts/s) or counts per minute (counts/min)

Corrected count rate:

\[\text{Corrected count rate} = \text{Measured count rate} - \text{Background count rate}\]

Note

Key Concept: Always measure background radiation first and subtract it from measurements with a radioactive source to obtain the true count rate from the source alone.

25.4 Types of Nuclear Emission

25.4.1 The Three Types of Nuclear Radiation

Radiation Type Nature Relative Charge Relative Mass Symbol
Alpha (α) Helium nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons) +2 4 \(^4_2\alpha\) or \(^4_2\text{He}\)
Beta (β) Fast-moving electron ejected from nucleus –1 \(\frac{1}{2000}\) \(^0_{-1}\beta\) or \(^0_{-1}\text{e}\)
Gamma (γ) High-energy electromagnetic wave 0 0 \(\gamma\)

25.4.2 Relative Ionising Effects

Ionising effect: The ability of radiation to knock electrons from atoms, creating ions.

Radiation Relative Ionising Effect Explanation
Alpha (α) Highest Large mass and +2 charge → strong electric force transfers more energy to electrons in atoms
Beta (β) Medium Smaller mass and –1 charge → less energy transferred per collision
Gamma (γ) Lowest No charge, no mass → interacts less frequently with matter; transfers energy via photoelectric effect, Compton scattering, or pair production

25.4.3 Relative Penetrating Abilities

Radiation Penetrating Ability Stopped By
Alpha (α) Least A few cm of air, a sheet of paper, or human skin
Beta (β) Medium A few mm of aluminium or plastic
Gamma (γ) Highest Several cm of lead or very thick concrete; intensity reduced but not completely stopped
Warning

Important: Alpha particles are the most dangerous if ingested or inhaled because they cause intense ionisation in a small volume of tissue. Outside the body, alpha particles are stopped by skin and pose little risk.

25.4.4 Deflection in Electric and Magnetic Fields

In an electric field:

  • Alpha particles (+2 charge): Deflected towards the negative plate; small deflection due to large mass

  • Beta particles (–1 charge): Deflected towards the positive plate; large deflection due to small mass

  • Gamma rays (no charge): Not deflected; travel in a straight line

In a magnetic field:

  • Alpha particles: Deflected according to Fleming’s left-hand rule; circular path with large radius (due to large mass)

  • Beta particles: Deflected in the opposite direction to alpha; circular path with small radius (due to small mass)

  • Gamma rays: Not deflected; travel in a straight line

Note

Key Insight: The direction and amount of deflection can be used to identify the type of radiation and distinguish between α, β, and γ emissions.

25.5 Radioactive Decay

25.5.1 What Is Radioactive Decay?

Radioactive decay is a spontaneous change in an unstable nucleus that results in the emission of α-particles, β-particles, and/or γ-radiation.

Key points:

  • Decay is spontaneous: Cannot be sped up, slowed down, or stopped by external conditions

  • Decay is random: Impossible to predict which specific nucleus will decay next

  • Decay increases stability: The nucleus moves towards a more stable configuration

25.5.2 Alpha Decay

Process:

  • Nucleus emits an alpha particle (\(^4_2\alpha\))

  • Nucleon number decreases by 4; proton number decreases by 2

  • Element changes to one two places earlier in the periodic table

Nuclide equation:

\[^A_ZX \rightarrow ^{A-4}_{Z-2}Y + ^4_2\alpha\]

Example: Radon-222 decays to polonium-218:

\[^{222}_{86}\text{Rn} \rightarrow ^{218}_{84}\text{Po} + ^4_2\alpha\]

25.5.3 Beta Decay

Process:

  • A neutron in the nucleus changes into a proton and an electron: \(n \rightarrow p + e^-\)

  • The proton remains in the nucleus; the electron is emitted as a beta particle

  • Nucleon number remains unchanged; proton number increases by 1

  • Element changes to one place later in the periodic table

Nuclide equation:

\[^A_ZX \rightarrow ^A_{Z+1}Y + ^0_{-1}\beta\]

Example: Carbon-14 decays to nitrogen-14:

\[^{14}_6\text{C} \rightarrow ^{14}_7\text{N} + ^0_{-1}\beta\]

25.5.4 Gamma Emission

Process:

  • Nucleus releases excess energy after α- or β-decay

  • No change in nucleon number or proton number

  • Element does not change

Nuclide equation:

\[^A_ZX^* \rightarrow ^A_ZX + \gamma\]

Where \(X^*\) indicates an excited (high-energy) nucleus.

Note

Key Concept: Gamma emission often accompanies α- or β-decay. The nucleus first decays by emitting α or β, then releases excess energy as γ-rays to reach a more stable state.

25.5.5 Why Are Some Isotopes Radioactive?

Isotopes may be radioactive due to:

  1. Excess neutrons: Nucleus has too many neutrons relative to protons → unstable → β– decay reduces neutron excess

  2. Too massive: Very heavy nuclei (e.g., uranium, radium) are unstable → α decay reduces mass and increases stability

  3. High energy state: Nucleus has excess energy after another decay → γ emission releases energy

Warning

Important: Radioactive decay is a natural process that moves nuclei towards greater stability. It cannot be controlled or influenced by chemical or physical conditions.

25.6 Half-life

25.6.1 What Is Half-life?

Half-life (\(t_{1/2}\)) of a radioactive isotope is the time taken for:

  • Half the nuclei of that isotope in any sample to decay, or

  • The count rate (or activity) of the isotope to fall to half its initial value

Key properties:

  • Half-life is constant for a given isotope (cannot be changed)

  • Half-life is independent of the amount of substance, temperature, pressure, or chemical state

  • Different isotopes have vastly different half-lives (from fractions of a second to billions of years)

25.6.2 Calculations Using Half-life

Method 1: Using the definition directly

After each half-life, the number of undecayed nuclei (or count rate) is halved:

Number of half-lives Fraction remaining Percentage remaining
0 1 100%
1 \(\frac{1}{2}\) 50%
2 \(\frac{1}{4}\) 25%
3 \(\frac{1}{8}\) 12.5%
\(n\) \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n\) \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n \times 100\%\)

Method 2: Using decay curves

A decay curve plots count rate (or number of nuclei) against time. The half-life can be determined by:

  1. Finding the initial count rate

  2. Finding the time when count rate has fallen to half this value

  3. This time interval is the half-life

Note

Key Concept: The decay curve is exponential — the count rate never reaches zero but approaches it asymptotically. Background radiation must be subtracted before analysing decay curves.

25.7 Applications of Radioactivity

25.7.1 Choosing Isotopes for Applications

The suitability of a radioactive isotope for a particular application depends on:

  1. Type of radiation emitted: Linked to penetrating ability and ionising effect

  2. Half-life: Must be appropriate for the intended use

25.7.2 Common Applications

Application Isotope Used Radiation Type Half-life Why Suitable
Smoke alarms Americium-241 α 432 years α-particles ionise air; smoke disrupts ionisation current; long half-life means source lasts for years
Sterilising medical equipment Cobalt-60 γ 5.3 years γ-rays penetrate packaging to kill bacteria; half-life long enough for practical use but not too long for disposal
Irradiating food Cobalt-60 γ 5.3 years γ-rays kill bacteria and pests without making food radioactive; penetrates packaging
Thickness control (paper/plastic) Strontium-90 β 29 years β-particles partially absorbed; count rate indicates thickness; half-life suitable for industrial use
Thickness control (metal) Cobalt-60 γ 5.3 years γ-rays penetrate metal; count rate indicates thickness
Medical diagnosis (tracers) Technetium-99m γ 6 hours γ-rays detected outside body; short half-life minimises patient exposure; decays quickly after procedure
Cancer treatment (radiotherapy) Cobalt-60, Iodine-131 γ, β 5.3 years, 8 days Radiation damages cancer cells; γ penetrates to reach tumours; β used for thyroid cancer (iodine concentrates in thyroid)
Warning

Important: For medical tracers, isotopes with short half-lives are preferred to minimise radiation exposure to the patient. The isotope should decay quickly after the diagnostic procedure is complete.

25.8 Nuclear Fission and Fusion (Extended)

25.8.1 Nuclear Fission

Definition: Nuclear fission is the splitting of a heavy nucleus into two lighter nuclei, accompanied by the release of energy and (usually) neutrons.

Example: Uranium-235 fission

\[^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^1_0\text{n} \rightarrow ^{144}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{90}_{36}\text{Kr} + 2^1_0\text{n} + \text{energy}\]

Key points:

  • Triggered by neutron absorption

  • Releases additional neutrons → can sustain a chain reaction

  • Mass of products < mass of reactants → mass converted to energy (\(E = mc^2\))

  • Used in nuclear power stations and nuclear weapons

25.8.2 Nuclear Fusion

Definition: Nuclear fusion is the joining of two light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, accompanied by the release of energy.

Example: Deuterium-tritium fusion

\[^2_1\text{H} + ^3_1\text{H} \rightarrow ^4_2\text{He} + ^1_0\text{n} + \text{energy}\]

Key points:

  • Occurs naturally in stars (including the Sun) at extremely high temperatures and pressures

  • Releases more energy per unit mass than fission

  • Produces less long-lived radioactive waste than fission

  • Not yet achieved for sustained power generation on Earth (research ongoing, e.g., ITER project)

25.8.3 Mass–Energy Conversion

In both fission and fusion:

  • Total mass of products < total mass of reactants

  • The “missing” mass is converted to energy according to Einstein’s equation:

\[E = mc^2\]

Where:

  • \(E\) = energy released (J)

  • \(m\) = mass defect (kg)

  • \(c\) = speed of light in vacuum (\(3.0 \times 10^8\) m/s)

Note

Key Insight: Because \(c^2\) is an enormous number (\(9 \times 10^{16}\) m²/s²), even a tiny mass defect produces a huge amount of energy. This explains why nuclear reactions release vastly more energy than chemical reactions.

25.9 Safety Precautions

25.9.1 Effects of Ionising Radiation on Living Things

Ionising radiation can damage living cells by:

  1. Direct damage: Breaking chemical bonds in DNA or other critical molecules

  2. Indirect damage: Ionising water molecules to produce reactive free radicals that damage cells

Consequences of exposure:

Exposure Level Effect
Low dose Possible cell mutation; increased long-term cancer risk
Moderate dose Cell death; tissue damage; radiation sickness (nausea, fatigue, hair loss)
High dose Severe radiation sickness; organ failure; death

25.9.2 Principles of Radiation Protection

Three key principles to minimise exposure:

  1. Time: Reduce the time spent near radioactive sources

  2. Distance: Increase distance from sources (intensity decreases with square of distance)

  3. Shielding: Use appropriate materials to absorb radiation:

    • α: Paper, skin, a few cm of air

    • β: Aluminium, plastic, a few mm of metal

    • γ: Lead, thick concrete, several cm of dense material

25.9.3 Safe Handling of Radioactive Materials

Storage:

  • Keep sources in labelled, shielded containers (e.g., lead-lined boxes for γ-emitters)

  • Store in secure, access-controlled areas

Use:

  • Use tongs or remote-handling tools to increase distance

  • Wear protective clothing (lab coat, gloves, safety glasses)

  • Work behind shielding (e.g., lead glass screens for γ-sources)

  • Monitor exposure with personal dosimeters

Disposal:

  • Follow institutional and regulatory procedures

  • Store waste in appropriate shielded containers until decay or disposal

Warning

Important: Never eat, drink, or apply cosmetics in areas where radioactive materials are used. Always wash hands after handling sources. Report spills or accidents immediately.

25.10 Key Concepts Summary

25.10.1 Core Concepts

  1. Atomic structure: Atom = small, dense, positively charged nucleus (protons + neutrons) + orbiting electrons

  2. Proton number (Z): Number of protons; defines the element; equals electrons in neutral atom

  3. Nucleon number (A): Total protons + neutrons; number of neutrons = \(A - Z\)

  4. Nuclide notation: \(^A_ZX\) represents an atom of element X with mass number A and atomic number Z

  5. Isotopes: Atoms of same element (same Z) with different numbers of neutrons (different A)

  6. Ions: Formed by loss (positive ion) or gain (negative ion) of electrons; protons unchanged

  7. Background radiation: Natural and artificial ionising radiation present in environment; sources include radon, rocks, food, cosmic rays

  8. Detection: GM tube + counter measures count rate (counts/s or counts/min); subtract background for corrected count rate

  9. Three emissions:

    • α: Helium nucleus, +2 charge, high ionising, low penetrating
    • β: Fast electron, –1 charge, medium ionising, medium penetrating
    • γ: EM wave, 0 charge, low ionising, high penetrating

  10. Radioactive decay: Spontaneous, random change in unstable nucleus; α- or β-decay changes element; γ emission releases excess energy

  11. Half-life: Time for half the nuclei in a sample to decay (or count rate to halve); constant for a given isotope

  12. Applications: Smoke alarms (α), sterilisation (γ), medical tracers (γ, short \(t_{1/2}\)), thickness control (β/γ), cancer treatment (γ/β)

  13. Safety: Effects include cell death, mutation, cancer; protect by reducing time, increasing distance, using shielding

25.10.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Rutherford scattering: α-particle experiment provided evidence for small, massive, positively charged nucleus surrounded by empty space

  2. Deflection in fields: α (+2) and β (–1) deflected in opposite directions in electric/magnetic fields; γ (0) not deflected

  3. Ionising effect explanation: Depends on charge (electric force) and kinetic energy; α has highest due to +2 charge and high KE

  4. Decay equations: Use nuclide notation to show α, β, γ emissions; conserve nucleon number and charge

  5. Why isotopes are radioactive: Excess neutrons → β– decay; too massive → α decay; excited state → γ emission

  6. Half-life with background: Subtract background count rate before determining half-life from decay data or curves

  7. Choosing isotopes: Match radiation type (penetration/ionisation) and half-life to application requirements

  8. Nuclear fission: Heavy nucleus splits into lighter nuclei + neutrons + energy; used in power stations

  9. Nuclear fusion: Light nuclei join to form heavier nucleus + energy; powers stars; research ongoing for power generation

  10. Mass–energy conversion: \(E = mc^2\); small mass defect → large energy release in nuclear reactions

25.11 Common Misconceptions

25.11.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Atoms are solid spheres with electrons embedded in them”

Incorrect: “The atom is like a plum pudding — electrons stuck in a positive sphere”

Correct:

  • Rutherford’s scattering experiment showed atoms have a tiny, dense nucleus containing protons and neutrons

  • Electrons orbit at relatively large distances; the atom is mostly empty space

  • The plum pudding model was disproved in 1911

25.11.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Isotopes have different chemical properties”

Incorrect: “Carbon-12 and carbon-14 behave differently in chemical reactions”

Correct:

  • Isotopes have the same number of protons and electrons → identical electron configuration

  • Chemical properties depend on electron arrangement → isotopes have identical chemical properties

  • Physical properties (e.g., mass, nuclear stability) may differ

25.11.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Radioactive decay can be sped up or slowed down”

Incorrect: “Heating a radioactive sample makes it decay faster”

Correct:

  • Radioactive decay is spontaneous and random; cannot be influenced by temperature, pressure, or chemical state

  • Half-life is a fixed property of each isotope

  • Only the probability of decay per nucleus is fixed; we cannot control when individual nuclei decay

25.11.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Alpha particles are the most dangerous type of radiation”

Incorrect: “Alpha radiation is always the most harmful”

Correct:

  • Danger depends on exposure pathway:

    • Outside body: α stopped by skin → low risk; γ most penetrating → highest external risk

    • Inside body: α causes intense ionisation in small volume → highest internal risk if ingested/inhaled

  • Risk assessment must consider radiation type, energy, exposure route, and dose

25.11.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Gamma rays are particles like alpha and beta”

Incorrect: “Gamma rays are heavy particles emitted from the nucleus”

Correct:

  • Gamma rays are electromagnetic waves (like light or X-rays), not particles

  • They have no mass and no charge → not deflected by electric/magnetic fields

  • Emitted when an excited nucleus releases excess energy after α- or β-decay

25.11.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “After two half-lives, all the radioactive material has decayed”

Incorrect: “If half-life is 10 years, after 20 years the sample is no longer radioactive”

Correct:

  • After 1 half-life: 50% remains

  • After 2 half-lives: 25% remains

  • After \(n\) half-lives: \(\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^n\) remains

  • Radioactive material never completely disappears; it approaches zero asymptotically

25.11.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “Nuclear fusion is the same as nuclear fission”

Incorrect: “Fusion and fission are just different names for splitting atoms”

Correct:

  • Fission: Heavy nucleus splits into lighter nuclei (e.g., uranium → barium + krypton)

  • Fusion: Light nuclei join to form heavier nucleus (e.g., hydrogen → helium in the Sun)

  • Both release energy via \(E = mc^2\), but mechanisms, conditions, and applications differ greatly

25.12 Worked Examples

25.12.1 Example 1: Nuclide Notation and Neutron Calculation

Question: An atom of uranium has a nucleon number of 238 and a proton number of 92. (a) Write the nuclide notation for this atom. (b) Calculate the number of neutrons in its nucleus. (c) How many electrons does a neutral atom of this isotope have?

Solution:

(a) Nuclide notation: \[^{238}_{92}\text{U}\]

(b) Number of neutrons: \[\text{Neutrons} = A - Z = 238 - 92 = \boxed{146}\]

(c) Electrons in neutral atom: In a neutral atom, number of electrons = number of protons = \(\boxed{92}\)

25.12.2 Example 2: Identifying Decay Type from Nuclide Equation

Question: Complete the following decay equation and identify the type of decay: \[^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow ^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} + \text{?}\]

Solution:

Step 1: Check conservation of nucleon number (A): \[210 = 206 + A_{\text{?}} \Rightarrow A_{\text{?}} = 4\]

Step 2: Check conservation of charge/proton number (Z): \[84 = 82 + Z_{\text{?}} \Rightarrow Z_{\text{?}} = 2\]

Step 3: Identify particle with \(A = 4\), \(Z = 2\): This is an alpha particle: \(^4_2\alpha\)

Answer: \[^{210}_{84}\text{Po} \rightarrow ^{206}_{82}\text{Pb} + \boxed{^4_2\alpha}\] Type of decay: Alpha decay

25.12.3 Example 3: Half-life Calculation

Question: A radioactive source has an initial count rate of 800 counts/min. The background count rate is 50 counts/min. The half-life of the isotope is 15 minutes. (a) Calculate the corrected initial count rate. (b) Calculate the corrected count rate after 45 minutes. (c) Calculate the measured count rate after 45 minutes (including background).

Solution:

(a) Corrected initial count rate: \[\text{Corrected} = 800 - 50 = \boxed{750 \text{ counts/min}}\]

(b) Corrected count rate after 45 minutes: Number of half-lives: \(n = \frac{45}{15} = 3\) \[\text{Corrected after 45 min} = 750 \times \left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^3 = 750 \times \frac{1}{8} = \boxed{93.75 \text{ counts/min}}\]

(c) Measured count rate (including background): \[\text{Measured} = 93.75 + 50 = \boxed{143.75 \text{ counts/min} \approx 144 \text{ counts/min}}\]

25.12.4 Example 4: Choosing an Isotope for an Application (Extended)

Question: A company wants to use a radioactive source to monitor the thickness of aluminium foil during production. Suggest whether an alpha, beta, or gamma source would be most suitable and explain your choice.

Solution:

Analysis: - Aluminium foil is relatively thin but denser than paper/plastic - Need radiation that is partially absorbed so count rate changes with thickness - Radiation must penetrate the foil to reach the detector

Evaluation of options:

Radiation Suitability for aluminium foil Reason
Alpha ❌ Unsuitable Stopped by paper; cannot penetrate aluminium foil at all
Beta ✅ Suitable Partially absorbed by thin metal; count rate varies with thickness; aluminium is within β penetration range
Gamma ❌ Less suitable Too penetrating; most γ-rays pass through thin foil with little absorption → small change in count rate

Conclusion: A beta source (e.g., strontium-90) is most suitable because beta particles are partially absorbed by aluminium foil, allowing the count rate to vary measurably with thickness changes.

25.12.5 Example 5: Nuclear Fission Equation (Extended)

Question: In a nuclear reactor, uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and undergoes fission to produce barium-141, krypton-92, and three neutrons. Write the balanced nuclide equation for this reaction.

Solution:

Step 1: Write reactants: \[^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^1_0\text{n} \rightarrow \text{?}\]

Step 2: Write known products: \[\rightarrow ^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3^1_0\text{n}\]

Step 3: Verify conservation: - Nucleon number: \(235 + 1 = 236\); \(141 + 92 + 3(1) = 236\) ✓ - Proton number: \(92 + 0 = 92\); \(56 + 36 + 3(0) = 92\) ✓

Answer: \[\boxed{^{235}_{92}\text{U} + ^1_0\text{n} \rightarrow ^{141}_{56}\text{Ba} + ^{92}_{36}\text{Kr} + 3^1_0\text{n} + \text{energy}}\]

25.14 Laboratory Investigation

25.14.1 Experiment 22.1: Investigating Radioactive Decay and Half-life

Objective: To determine the half-life of a radioactive isotope using count rate measurements and to understand the random nature of radioactive decay.

Apparatus:

  • Geiger-Müller tube and counter (or radiation sensor with data logger)

  • Radioactive source with suitable half-life (e.g., protactinium-234 generator, or simulated data)

  • Retort stand and clamp

  • Stopwatch

  • Lead shielding (for safety)

  • Distance measuring tool

Variables:

  • Independent: Time

  • Dependent: Count rate (counts/min)

  • Controlled: Distance from source to detector, background radiation level, detector settings

Safety Precautions:

  • ✓ Handle radioactive sources with tongs; never touch with bare hands

  • ✓ Keep source in shielded container when not in use

  • ✓ Maintain maximum practical distance from source

  • ✓ Limit exposure time; plan measurements in advance

  • ✓ Wear personal dosimeter if available

  • ✓ Follow institutional radiation safety protocols

Procedure:

  1. Measure background radiation:

    • Remove all radioactive sources from the area

    • Record count rate for 5 minutes; calculate average background count rate in counts/min

  2. Set up the experiment:

    • Clamp the GM tube at a fixed height

    • Position the radioactive source at a fixed distance (e.g., 10 cm) from the detector

    • Ensure the setup is stable and will not be disturbed during measurements

  3. Collect decay data:

    • Start the counter and stopwatch simultaneously

    • Record the total counts for successive 1-minute intervals (or use data logger for continuous recording)

    • Continue for at least 5–6 half-lives of the isotope (or until count rate approaches background)

  4. Data processing:

    • Convert total counts per interval to count rate (counts/min)

    • Subtract background count rate to obtain corrected count rate

    • Tabulate time vs. corrected count rate

Data Table:

Time (min) Total counts in interval Count rate (counts/min) Corrected count rate (counts/min)
0–1
1–2
2–3
3–4
4–5
…

Analysis:

  1. Plot a graph of corrected count rate (y-axis) against time (x-axis)

  2. Determine the half-life by:

    • Finding the initial corrected count rate (\(R_0\))

    • Finding the time when count rate falls to \(\frac{1}{2}R_0\) → this is one half-life

    • Repeat for \(\frac{1}{4}R_0\), \(\frac{1}{8}R_0\) to check consistency

  3. Alternatively, plot ln(corrected count rate) against time; the gradient = \(-\lambda\) (decay constant), and \(t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda}\)

  4. Comment on the random variation in count rates and how averaging over longer intervals reduces this effect

Precautions:

  • ✓ Ensure detector is properly calibrated and zeroed

  • ✓ Keep geometry (distance, orientation) constant throughout

  • ✓ Account for dead time of GM tube if count rates are high

  • ✓ Repeat measurements to assess random uncertainty

Expected Results:

  • Graph shows exponential decay: count rate decreases by half every half-life

  • Random fluctuations around the smooth decay curve demonstrate the random nature of decay

  • Calculated half-life should agree with accepted value within experimental uncertainty

Conclusion Template: “The experiment demonstrated the exponential nature of radioactive decay. The corrected count rate decreased by approximately half every ______ minutes, giving a half-life of ______ min for the isotope. Random variations in count rate confirmed the spontaneous and random nature of nuclear decay. The measured half-life agreed with the accepted value of ______ min within experimental error.”

25.16 Chapter Summary

25.16.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Atomic structure: Nucleus (protons + neutrons) at centre; electrons orbit at distance; atom mostly empty space
✓ Proton number (Z): Number of protons; defines element; = electrons in neutral atom
✓ Nucleon number (A): Protons + neutrons; neutrons = \(A - Z\)
✓ Nuclide notation: \(^A_ZX\)
✓ Isotopes: Same Z, different A; same chemical properties, different nuclear properties
✓ Ions: Formed by electron loss/gain; protons unchanged
✓ Background radiation: Natural/artificial ionising radiation; measure and subtract for accurate source measurement
✓ Three emissions: α (He nucleus, +2, high ionising, low penetrating), β (electron, –1, medium), γ (EM wave, 0, low ionising, high penetrating)
✓ Radioactive decay: Spontaneous, random; α/β change element; γ releases excess energy
✓ Half-life: Time for half nuclei to decay (or count rate to halve); constant for given isotope
✓ Applications: Match radiation type and half-life to use (smoke alarms, sterilisation, medical tracers, thickness control, radiotherapy)
✓ Safety: Radiation can cause cell damage, mutation, cancer; protect by time, distance, shielding
✓ Rutherford scattering: Evidence for small, massive, positive nucleus in empty space (Extended)
✓ Deflection in fields: α and β deflected oppositely; γ not deflected (Extended)
✓ Decay equations: Conserve A and Z; use nuclide notation (Extended)
✓ Fission/fusion: Splitting/joining nuclei; \(E = mc^2\) explains energy release (Extended)

Number of neutrons \(N = A - Z\) \(A\)=nucleon number, \(Z\)=proton number
Corrected count rate \(R_{\text{corr}} = R_{\text{meas}} - R_{\text{bg}}\) \(R_{\text{meas}}\)=measured rate, \(R_{\text{bg}}\)=background rate

25.18 Glossary

Term Definition
Alpha particle (α) A helium nucleus (\(^4_2\text{He}\)) emitted during alpha decay; +2 charge, high ionising, low penetrating
Background radiation Ionising radiation present in the environment from natural and artificial sources
Beta particle (β) A high-speed electron (\(^0_{-1}\text{e}\)) emitted during beta decay; –1 charge, medium ionising and penetrating
Count rate Number of radiation detections per unit time (counts/s or counts/min) measured by a detector
Electron Negatively charged subatomic particle orbiting the nucleus; relative charge –1, relative mass ≈ \(\frac{1}{2000}\)
Gamma ray (γ) High-energy electromagnetic radiation emitted by excited nuclei; no charge or mass, low ionising, high penetrating
Geiger-Müller tube A detector that measures ionising radiation by counting electrical pulses from ionisation events
Half-life (\(t_{1/2}\)) Time taken for half the nuclei in a radioactive sample to decay, or for count rate to halve
Ion An atom or molecule with a net electric charge due to loss or gain of electrons
Ionising radiation Radiation with sufficient energy to remove electrons from atoms, creating ions
Isotope Atoms of the same element (same proton number) with different numbers of neutrons (different nucleon number)
Neutron Neutral subatomic particle in the nucleus; relative charge 0, relative mass 1
Nuclear fission Splitting of a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei, releasing energy and neutrons
Nuclear fusion Joining of light nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy
Nucleon A proton or neutron; constituent of atomic nuclei
Nucleon number (A) Total number of protons and neutrons in a nucleus (mass number)
Nucleus Small, dense, positively charged centre of an atom containing protons and neutrons
Nuclide notation Symbolic representation of an atom: \(^A_ZX\), where A = nucleon number, Z = proton number, X = element symbol
Proton Positively charged subatomic particle in the nucleus; relative charge +1, relative mass 1
Proton number (Z) Number of protons in a nucleus (atomic number); defines the element
Radioactive decay Spontaneous, random change in an unstable nucleus with emission of α, β, and/or γ radiation
Radioactivity The property of unstable nuclei to spontaneously emit ionising radiation
