8.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content (1.5.2 Turning Effect of Forces):

  • Describe the moment of a force as a measure of its turning effect and give everyday examples

  • Define the moment of a force as moment = force × perpendicular distance from the pivot; recall and use this equation

  • Apply the principle of moments to situations with one force each side of the pivot, including balancing of a beam

  • State that, when there is no resultant force and no resultant moment, an object is in equilibrium

Core Content (1.5.3 Centre of Gravity):

  • State what is meant by centre of gravity

  • Describe an experiment to determine the position of the centre of gravity of an irregularly shaped plane lamina

  • Describe, qualitatively, the effect of the position of the centre of gravity on the stability of simple objects

Supplement (Extended):

  • Apply the principle of moments to situations with more than one force each side of the pivot

  • Describe an experiment to demonstrate that there is no resultant moment on an object in equilibrium

8.2 The Turning Effect of Forces: Moment

8.2.1 What is a Moment?

A moment of a force (also called torque) is a measure of the turning effect of a force about a pivot.

Everyday examples:

  • Opening a door by pushing the handle (far from the hinge)

  • Turning a steering wheel

  • Using a spanner to tighten a bolt

  • Opening a bottle cap with a bottle opener

Note

Key Concept: The turning effect depends on two factors:

  1. The magnitude of the force applied

  2. The perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force

8.2.2 Definition and Formula

The moment of a force is defined as:

\[\text{Moment} = F \times d_{\perp}\]

Where: - \(F\) = force (N) - \(d_{\perp}\) = perpendicular distance from the pivot to the line of action of the force (m) - Unit: newton-metre (N m)

Direction of moment:

  • Clockwise moment: tends to rotate the object clockwise about the pivot

  • Anticlockwise moment: tends to rotate the object anticlockwise about the pivot

8.2.3 Calculating Moments: Worked Examples

8.2.3.1 Example 1: Basic Moment Calculation

Question: A force of 50 N is applied perpendicular to a door handle at a distance of 0.8 m from the hinge. Calculate the moment of the force about the hinge.

Solution: \[\text{Moment} = F \times d_{\perp} = 50 \times 0.8 = \boxed{40 \text{ N m (anticlockwise)}}\]

8.2.3.2 Example 2: Finding Perpendicular Distance

Question: A minimum moment of 25 N m is required to open a heavy gate. If a force of 100 N is applied, what is the minimum perpendicular distance from the pivot at which the force must be applied?

Solution: \[\text{Moment} = F \times d_{\perp}\] \[25 = 100 \times d_{\perp}\] \[d_{\perp} = \frac{25}{100} = \boxed{0.25 \text{ m}}\]

8.2.3.3 Example 3: Force Not Perpendicular (Extended)

Question: A force of 60 N is applied at an angle of 30° to a wrench of length 0.4 m. Calculate the moment about the pivot.

Solution: The perpendicular component of the force is: \[F_{\perp} = F \sin \theta = 60 \times \sin 30° = 60 \times 0.5 = 30 \text{ N}\]

\[\text{Moment} = F_{\perp} \times d = 30 \times 0.4 = \boxed{12 \text{ N m}}\]

Warning

Important: Always use the perpendicular distance or the perpendicular component of force. Using the wrong distance is a common error.

8.3 The Principle of Moments

8.3.1 Statement of the Principle

For an object in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments about any pivot equals the sum of anticlockwise moments.

\[\sum M_{\text{clockwise}} = \sum M_{\text{anticlockwise}}\]

8.3.2 Conditions for Complete Equilibrium

For an object to be in complete equilibrium, two conditions must be satisfied:

Condition Mathematical Expression Physical Meaning
Translational equilibrium \(\sum F = 0\) No resultant force → object does not accelerate linearly
Rotational equilibrium \(\sum M = 0\) No resultant moment → object does not rotate

8.3.3 Applying the Principle: Worked Examples

8.3.3.1 Example 4: Simple Beam Balance (One Force Each Side)

Question: A uniform metre rule is balanced at the 50 cm mark. A 4.0 N weight is hung at the 20 cm mark. Where must a 2.0 N weight be hung to balance the rule?

Solution:

Step 1: Identify distances from pivot (50 cm mark) - 4.0 N weight: \(d_1 = 50 - 20 = 30 \text{ cm}\) (left side → anticlockwise moment) - 2.0 N weight: let distance = \(d_2\) (right side → clockwise moment)

Step 2: Apply principle of moments \[\text{Anticlockwise moment} = \text{Clockwise moment}\] \[(4.0)(30) = (2.0)(d_2)\] \[120 = 2.0 d_2\] \[d_2 = \frac{120}{2.0} = 60 \text{ cm}\]

Step 3: Find position on rule Position = pivot + \(d_2\) = \(50 + 60 = \boxed{110 \text{ cm}}\)

Warning

Note: Since the rule is only 100 cm long, this arrangement is not physically possible on a standard metre rule. The 2.0 N weight would need to be hung beyond the end of the rule, or a heavier weight must be used.

8.3.3.2 Example 5: Multiple Forces (Extended)

Question: A uniform beam is balanced at its centre. Forces act as shown: - 10 N downward at 0.3 m left of pivot - 15 N downward at 0.2 m right of pivot - Unknown force \(F\) downward at 0.4 m right of pivot

Calculate \(F\) for equilibrium.

Solution:

Step 1: Assign moment directions - 10 N at 0.3 m left → anticlockwise moment - 15 N at 0.2 m right → clockwise moment - \(F\) at 0.4 m right → clockwise moment

Step 2: Apply principle of moments \[\sum M_{\text{acw}} = \sum M_{\text{cw}}\] \[(10)(0.3) = (15)(0.2) + (F)(0.4)\] \[3.0 = 3.0 + 0.4F\] \[0 = 0.4F\] \[F = \boxed{0 \text{ N}}\]

Interpretation: The 10 N and 15 N forces already balance each other. No additional force is required for rotational equilibrium.

8.3.3.3 Example 6: Beam with Weight (Extended)

Question: A uniform metre rule of weight 1.5 N is balanced at the 30 cm mark. A 3.0 N weight is hung at the 10 cm mark. Where must a 2.0 N weight be hung to balance the rule?

Solution:

Step 1: Identify all forces and their positions - Weight of rule (1.5 N) acts at centre of gravity = 50 cm mark - 3.0 N weight at 10 cm mark - 2.0 N weight at unknown position \(x\) cm - Pivot at 30 cm mark

Step 2: Calculate distances from pivot - Rule’s weight: \(d_{\text{rule}} = 50 - 30 = 20 \text{ cm}\) (right → clockwise) - 3.0 N weight: \(d_1 = 30 - 10 = 20 \text{ cm}\) (left → anticlockwise) - 2.0 N weight: \(d_2 = |x - 30|\) cm

Step 3: Determine which side the 2.0 N weight must be on Anticlockwise moment from 3.0 N: \((3.0)(20) = 60 \text{ N cm}\) Clockwise moment from rule’s weight: \((1.5)(20) = 30 \text{ N cm}\)

Since anticlockwise > clockwise, the 2.0 N weight must provide additional clockwise moment → hang on the right of pivot.

Step 4: Apply principle of moments \[\sum M_{\text{acw}} = \sum M_{\text{cw}}\] \[(3.0)(20) = (1.5)(20) + (2.0)(d_2)\] \[60 = 30 + 2.0 d_2\] \[30 = 2.0 d_2\] \[d_2 = 15 \text{ cm}\]

Step 5: Find position Position = pivot + \(d_2\) = \(30 + 15 = \boxed{45 \text{ cm mark}}\)

8.4 Centre of Gravity

8.4.1 Definition

The centre of gravity (CG) of an object is the point through which the entire weight of the object may be considered to act.

Object Type Location of CG
Regular, uniform object Geometric centre
Irregular object Determined experimentally
Hollow symmetric object (e.g., ring) May lie outside the material
Note

Key Insight: The CG is not necessarily within the material of the object. For a ring, the CG is at the centre—in empty space.

8.4.2 Locating the CG of an Irregular Lamina

Apparatus: Irregular cardboard lamina, pin, plumb line, retort stand, pencil

Procedure:

  1. Make three small holes near the edge of the lamina, well separated from each other

  2. Suspend the lamina freely from one hole using a pin

  3. Hang a plumb line from the same pin

  4. When steady, mark the line of the plumb line on the lamina with a pencil

  5. Repeat steps 2–4 for the other two holes

  6. The intersection of the three lines is the centre of gravity

Why three holes?

  • Using three lines provides a check: if all three intersect at one point, the result is reliable

  • If they form a small triangle, the CG is at the centre of that triangle

Precautions:

  • ✓ Ensure the lamina swings freely without obstruction

  • ✓ Allow the plumb line to come to rest before marking

  • ✓ Draw thin, accurate lines for precise intersection

  • ✓ Make holes small to avoid removing significant material

8.5 Stability and the Centre of Gravity

8.5.1 Factors Affecting Stability

The stability of an object depends on:

  1. Position of the centre of gravity (lower = more stable)

  2. Area of the base (wider = more stable)

8.5.2 Types of Equilibrium

Type Effect of Small Displacement CG Movement Example
Stable Object returns to original position CG rises then returns Cone on base, racing car
Unstable Object moves further from original position CG falls Cone on tip, pencil balanced vertically
Neutral Object stays in new position CG remains at same height Sphere on flat surface, wheel on axle

8.5.3 Design Applications

Application Stability Feature Purpose
Racing car Low CG + wide wheelbase Prevent rollover at high speed
Bunsen burner Heavy, wide base Resist tipping during use
Double-decker bus Passengers encouraged to use lower deck Lower overall CG for cornering safety
Table lamp Weighted base Prevent toppling when cord is pulled
Crane Counterweight + wide outriggers Maintain stability when lifting heavy loads

8.6 Key Concepts Summary

8.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Moment = \(F \times d_{\perp}\); unit: N m; direction: clockwise or anticlockwise

  2. Principle of moments: For equilibrium, \(\sum M_{\text{cw}} = \sum M_{\text{acw}}\)

  3. Complete equilibrium requires: \(\sum F = 0\) and \(\sum M = 0\)

  4. Centre of gravity: Point where entire weight acts; may lie outside the object

  5. Stability increases with lower CG and wider base

  6. Three types of equilibrium: stable (CG rises), unstable (CG falls), neutral (CG unchanged)

8.6.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Multiple-force problems: Apply principle of moments with several forces on each side of pivot

  2. Non-perpendicular forces: Use perpendicular component \(F_{\perp} = F \sin \theta\) or perpendicular distance

  3. Experimental verification: Demonstrate zero resultant moment using balanced beam apparatus

8.7 Common Misconceptions

8.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Moment depends only on force, not on where it is applied”

Incorrect: “Pushing a door near the hinge is as effective as pushing at the handle”

Correct:

  • Moment = \(F \times d_{\perp}\); distance from pivot is crucial

  • Pushing at the handle (large \(d_{\perp}\)) produces a larger moment for the same force

  • This is why door handles, spanners, and bottle openers are designed with long lever arms

8.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Centre of gravity is always inside the object”

Incorrect: “The CG of a ring is in the metal”

Correct:

  • The CG is the point where the entire weight may be considered to act

  • For symmetric hollow objects (ring, hollow sphere), the CG is at the geometric centre—in empty space

  • This is why a ring can balance on a fingertip at its centre

8.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “An object in equilibrium must be at rest”

Incorrect: “If an object is moving, it cannot be in equilibrium”

Correct:

  • Translational equilibrium (\(\sum F = 0\)) → constant velocity (which may be zero)

  • Rotational equilibrium (\(\sum M = 0\)) → no angular acceleration (may be rotating at constant angular velocity)

  • A car moving at constant speed on a straight road is in translational equilibrium

8.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “The weight of a uniform beam can be ignored in moment calculations”

Incorrect: “Only external loads matter for balancing”

Correct:

  • The weight of a uniform beam acts at its centre of gravity (geometric centre)

  • If the pivot is not at the CG, the beam’s weight produces a moment that must be included

  • Example: A metre rule balanced away from the 50 cm mark requires accounting for its own weight

8.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Stability depends only on mass”

Incorrect: “A heavier object is always more stable”

Correct:

  • Stability depends on CG position and base area, not absolute mass

  • A light object with a low CG and wide base (e.g., a plastic stool) can be more stable than a heavy object with a high CG and narrow base (e.g., a tall vase)

8.7.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Clockwise and anticlockwise are absolute directions”

Incorrect: “Clockwise always means to the right”

Correct:

  • Clockwise/anticlockwise are defined relative to the pivot and the observer’s viewpoint

  • Always specify the pivot and draw a clear diagram to avoid sign errors in calculations

8.8 Worked Examples

8.8.1 Example 7: Crane Balance (Extended)

Question: A crane arm is balanced at a pivot. A load of 5000 N is suspended 8.0 m from the pivot. A counterweight of mass \(M\) is placed 2.0 m from the pivot on the opposite side. The arm itself has a weight of 1000 N acting at its centre, 3.0 m from the pivot on the load side. Calculate \(M\) for equilibrium. Take \(g = 10 \text{ N/kg}\).

Solution:

Step 1: Identify forces and distances from pivot - Load: 5000 N at 8.0 m (load side → anticlockwise) - Arm weight: 1000 N at 3.0 m (load side → anticlockwise) - Counterweight: \(W = Mg = 10M\) N at 2.0 m (opposite side → clockwise)

Step 2: Apply principle of moments \[\sum M_{\text{acw}} = \sum M_{\text{cw}}\] \[(5000)(8.0) + (1000)(3.0) = (10M)(2.0)\] \[40000 + 3000 = 20M\] \[43000 = 20M\] \[M = \frac{43000}{20} = \boxed{2150 \text{ kg}}\]

8.8.2 Example 8: Stability Analysis

Question: A rectangular block (40 cm × 20 cm × 10 cm) is placed on a horizontal surface. Explain why it is more stable when resting on its 40 cm × 20 cm face than on its 20 cm × 10 cm face.

Solution:

Case 1: Resting on 40 cm × 20 cm face - Base area = \(40 \times 20 = 800 \text{ cm}^2\) (large) - Height of CG ≈ half of 10 cm = 5 cm (low)

Case 2: Resting on 20 cm × 10 cm face - Base area = \(20 \times 10 = 200 \text{ cm}^2\) (small) - Height of CG ≈ half of 40 cm = 20 cm (high)

Conclusion: Stability increases with larger base area and lower CG. Case 1 satisfies both conditions. The block can be tilted further before the line of action of weight falls outside the base, making it significantly more stable.

8.8.3 Example 9: Experimental Verification (Extended)

Question: Describe an experiment to demonstrate that there is no resultant moment on an object in equilibrium.

Solution:

Apparatus: Metre rule, optical pin (pivot), retort stand, known masses (50 g, 100 g), thread, plasticine

Procedure:

  1. Balance the metre rule horizontally on the optical pin at the 50 cm mark

  2. If the rule does not balance, attach a small amount of plasticine to the lighter end until it does

  3. Hang a 50 g mass (weight ≈ 0.5 N) at 40.0 cm from the pivot on one side

  4. Hang a 100 g mass (weight ≈ 1.0 N) on the opposite side and adjust its position until the rule balances horizontally

  5. Measure and record the distance \(d_2\) of the 100 g mass from the pivot

  6. Calculate moments: \(M_1 = 0.5 \times 40.0\), \(M_2 = 1.0 \times d_2\)

  7. Repeat for different masses and distances

Expected Result: Within experimental error, \(M_1 \approx M_2\), confirming that \(\sum M_{\text{cw}} = \sum M_{\text{acw}}\) for an object in rotational equilibrium.

Precautions:

  • ✓ Ensure the rule is horizontal before taking readings

  • ✓ Measure distances from the pivot to the centre of each mass

  • ✓ Avoid parallax error when reading positions

  • ✓ Use a sensitive pivot to minimise friction

8.10 Laboratory Investigation

8.10.1 Experiment 5.1: Verifying the Principle of Moments

Objective: To demonstrate that for an object in rotational equilibrium, the sum of clockwise moments equals the sum of anticlockwise moments.

Apparatus: - Uniform metre rule - Optical pin and retort stand (pivot) - Set of slotted masses (10 g to 100 g) - Thread loops - Plasticine (for initial balancing) - Metre rule for measuring distances

Variables: - Independent: Masses and their distances from pivot - Dependent: Position required for equilibrium - Controlled: Same pivot, same rule, same method of measuring distances

Procedure:

  1. Set up the metre rule balanced horizontally on the optical pin at the 50 cm mark

  2. If the rule does not balance, attach a small amount of plasticine to the lighter end until it does (this becomes part of the rule for the experiment)

  3. Hang a known mass \(m_1\) (e.g., 50 g → weight ≈ 0.5 N) at a measured distance \(d_1\) from the pivot on one side

  4. Hang another known mass \(m_2\) (e.g., 100 g → weight ≈ 1.0 N) on the opposite side

  5. Adjust the position of \(m_2\) until the rule balances horizontally

  6. Measure and record the distance \(d_2\) of \(m_2\) from the pivot

  7. Calculate moments: \(M_1 = m_1 g d_1\), \(M_2 = m_2 g d_2\)

  8. Repeat for at least four different combinations of masses and distances

Data Table:

Trial \(m_1\) (g) \(d_1\) (cm) \(M_1 = m_1 g d_1\) (N cm) \(m_2\) (g) \(d_2\) (cm) \(M_2 = m_2 g d_2\) (N cm) % Difference
1 50 100
2 50 150
3 100 100
4 100 200

Analysis:

  1. For each trial, calculate the percentage difference: \[\% \text{ difference} = \frac{|M_1 - M_2|}{(M_1 + M_2)/2} \times 100\%\]

  2. If all percentage differences are within ±10% (typical experimental accuracy), the principle of moments is verified

  3. Identify possible sources of error (e.g., friction at pivot, parallax in reading distances)

Precautions:

  • ✓ Ensure the rule is horizontal before taking readings

  • ✓ Measure distances from the pivot to the centre of each mass

  • ✓ Avoid parallax error: view the scale with eye perpendicular

  • ✓ Use a sensitive pivot to minimise friction

  • ✓ Allow the rule to settle completely before recording positions

Conclusion Template: “The experiment verified the principle of moments: within experimental error (±___%), the sum of clockwise moments equalled the sum of anticlockwise moments for a balanced beam. This confirms that an object in rotational equilibrium experiences no resultant moment.”

Extension (Supplement):

  • Investigate the effect of the beam’s own weight by balancing the rule away from its centre of gravity

  • Use three or more masses on each side to test the principle with multiple forces

8.12 Chapter Summary

8.12.1 Key Points to Remember

  • Moment = \(F \times d_{\perp}\) (unit: N m); direction: clockwise or anticlockwise

  • Principle of moments: For rotational equilibrium, \(\sum M_{\text{cw}} = \sum M_{\text{acw}}\)

  • Complete equilibrium: \(\sum F = 0\) and \(\sum M = 0\)

  • Centre of gravity: Point where entire weight acts; may lie outside the object

  • Stability ↑ with lower CG and wider base

  • Three equilibrium types: stable (CG rises), unstable (CG falls), neutral (CG unchanged)

  • Experimental verification: Balanced beam apparatus confirms zero resultant moment

  • Multiple-force problems: Sum all clockwise and anticlockwise moments separately
