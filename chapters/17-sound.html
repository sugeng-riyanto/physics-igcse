20.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content:

  • Describe the production of sound by vibrating sources

  • Describe the longitudinal nature of sound waves

  • State the approximate range of frequencies audible to humans as 20 Hz to 20 000 Hz

  • Know that a medium is needed to transmit sound waves

  • Know that the speed of sound in air is approximately 330–350 m/s

  • Describe a method involving a measurement of distance and time for determining the speed of sound in air

  • Describe how changes in amplitude and frequency affect the loudness and pitch of sound waves

  • Describe an echo as the reflection of sound waves

  • Define ultrasound as sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz

Supplement (Extended):

  • Describe compression and rarefaction

  • Know that, in general, sound travels faster in solids than in liquids and faster in liquids than in gases

  • Describe the uses of ultrasound in non-destructive testing of materials, medical scanning of soft tissue and sonar including calculation of depth or distance from time and wave speed

20.2 17.1 Production and Nature of Sound Waves

20.2.1 How Is Sound Produced?

Sound is produced by vibrating sources placed in a medium.

  • When a guitar string vibrates, it pushes and pulls on the surrounding air particles

  • When vocal cords vibrate, they create pressure variations in the air

  • When a drum skin is struck, it vibrates and transfers energy to the air

Note

Key Concept: Without vibration, there is no sound. If you stop a vibrating object (e.g., by placing your hand on a ringing bell), the sound stops because the vibration stops.

20.2.2 The Longitudinal Nature of Sound Waves

Sound waves are longitudinal waves. This means:

  • The direction of vibration of particles is parallel to the direction of wave propagation

  • Sound waves propagate as a series of compressions and rarefactions

Term Description Particle Arrangement
Compression Region where air pressure is higher than surrounding pressure Particles are close together
Rarefaction Region where air pressure is lower than surrounding pressure Particles are spread apart

Visual representation of a sound wave:

Direction of wave propagation →

Compression    Rarefaction    Compression    Rarefaction
[|||||]        [   ]          [|||||]        [   ]
particles      particles      particles      particles
close          spread         close          spread
together       apart          together       apart
Warning

Important: Do not confuse the motion of individual particles with the motion of the wave. Particles oscillate back and forth about fixed positions; they do not travel with the wave.

20.2.3 The Audible Range of Human Hearing

The human ear can detect sound waves within a specific range of frequencies:

  • Lower limit: approximately 20 Hz

  • Upper limit: approximately 20 000 Hz (or 20 kHz)

Terminology:

Term Frequency Range Audible to Humans?
Infrasound Below 20 Hz No
Audible sound 20 Hz to 20 kHz Yes
Ultrasound Above 20 kHz No

Note: The upper limit of hearing decreases with age. Many adults cannot hear sounds above 15–17 kHz.

20.3 17.2 Transmission of Sound

20.3.1 Why Is a Medium Needed?

Sound waves require a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to travel because:

  • Sound is produced by particle vibrations

  • These vibrations must be transferred from one particle to the next

  • In a vacuum, there are no particles to vibrate → sound cannot travel

Demonstration: The Bell Jar Experiment

  1. An electric bell is suspended inside a sealed bell jar

  2. When the bell rings with air inside, the sound is clearly heard

  3. As air is pumped out, the sound becomes fainter

  4. When a near-vacuum is achieved, the sound cannot be heard (though the bell is still visibly vibrating)

Conclusion: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

20.3.2 Speed of Sound in Different Media

The speed of sound depends on the medium through which it travels:

Medium Approximate Speed of Sound (m/s)
Air (at 20°C) 330–350
Water ≈ 1500
Steel ≈ 5000
Granite ≈ 5400

General rule: Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, and slowest in gases.

Explanation using particle model:

  • In solids, particles are closely packed → vibrations transfer quickly between particles

  • In liquids, particles are slightly further apart → transfer is slower

  • In gases, particles are far apart → transfer is slowest

Note

Key Insight: The speed of sound also depends on temperature. In air, sound travels faster at higher temperatures because particles have more kinetic energy and collide more frequently.

20.3.3 Measuring the Speed of Sound in Air

Method: Distance–Time Measurement

Apparatus:

  • Two observers (A and B) positioned a known distance apart (e.g., 500 m)

  • Electronic starting pistol (produces flash and sound simultaneously)

  • Stopwatch

  • Measuring tape

Procedure:

  1. Observer A fires the electronic starting pistol

  2. Observer B starts the stopwatch when seeing the flash (light travels almost instantaneously)

  3. Observer B stops the stopwatch when hearing the sound

  4. Record the time interval \(t\)

  5. Calculate speed: \(v = \frac{d}{t}\)

Example calculation:

  • Distance \(d = 500 \text{ m}\)

  • Time \(t = 1.5 \text{ s}\)

  • Speed \(v = \frac{500}{1.5} = 333 \text{ m/s}\)

Precautions:

  • ✓ Repeat the experiment and take an average to minimise random errors

  • ✓ Interchange positions of observers to cancel wind effects

  • ✓ Use a large distance to reduce timing error relative to total time

20.4 17.3 Loudness and Pitch

20.4.1 What Affects Loudness?

Loudness is related to the amplitude of a sound wave.

  • Larger amplitude → louder sound

  • Smaller amplitude → softer sound

Particle explanation:

  • Larger amplitude means particles oscillate with greater displacement

  • This creates larger pressure variations (greater difference between compressions and rarefactions)

  • The ear detects these larger pressure variations as a louder sound

20.4.2 What Affects Pitch?

Pitch is related to the frequency of a sound wave.

  • Higher frequency → higher pitch

  • Lower frequency → lower pitch

Particle explanation:

  • Higher frequency means particles oscillate more times per second

  • The ear interprets more frequent pressure variations as a higher-pitched sound

20.4.3 Visualising Sound Waves with a C.R.O.

A cathode-ray oscilloscope (c.r.o.) connected to a microphone can display sound waveforms:

Waveform Feature What It Represents
Height of wave (from rest position) Amplitude → loudness
Distance between successive peaks Wavelength → related to frequency and pitch
Number of waves per second on screen Frequency → pitch

Example comparisons:

  • A loud, low-pitched sound: large amplitude, widely spaced peaks

  • A soft, high-pitched sound: small amplitude, closely spaced peaks

20.5 17.4 Echoes and Ultrasound

20.5.1 What Is an Echo?

An echo is the reflection of sound waves from a hard, flat surface.

How echoes form:

  1. Sound waves travel from a source towards a surface (e.g., a wall or cliff)

  2. The waves strike the surface and are reflected back

  3. The reflected waves reach the listener after a time delay

Law of reflection for sound:

  • Angle of incidence = Angle of reflection

  • Both angles measured from the normal to the reflecting surface

Calculating distance using echoes:

\[d = \frac{v \times t}{2}\]

Where: - \(d\) = distance to reflecting surface (m)

  • \(v\) = speed of sound in the medium (m/s)

  • \(t\) = time for echo to return (s)

  • Divide by 2 because sound travels to the surface and back

Worked Example: Echo Calculation

Question: A ship sends a sound pulse downwards into the sea. An echo from the seabed is received 0.4 s later. If the speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m/s, calculate the depth of the sea.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify values - \(v = 1500 \text{ m/s}\)

  • \(t = 0.4 \text{ s}\)

Step 2: Apply formula \[d = \frac{v \times t}{2} = \frac{1500 \times 0.4}{2} = \boxed{300 \text{ m}}\]

20.5.2 What Is Ultrasound?

Ultrasound is sound with a frequency higher than 20 kHz (above the upper limit of human hearing).

20.5.3 Uses of Ultrasound

20.5.3.1 1. Non-Destructive Testing of Materials

  • Ultrasound is sent through materials such as metal pipes or concrete slabs

  • Defects (cracks, cavities) reflect or scatter the ultrasound

  • By analysing the reflected signals, engineers can locate and size defects without damaging the material

20.5.3.2 2. Medical Scanning of Soft Tissue

  • Ultrasound pulses are sent into the body via a transducer

  • Different tissues reflect ultrasound to different degrees

  • The time taken for echoes to return is used to build up an image of internal structures

  • Advantages over X-rays: Ultrasound is non-ionising and safer for scanning foetuses and soft tissues

20.5.3.3 3. Sonar (Sound Navigation and Ranging)

  • Ships use sonar to detect the depth of the seabed or the position of objects underwater

  • A pulse of ultrasound is sent downwards; the time for the echo to return is measured

  • Depth is calculated using \(d = \frac{v \times t}{2}\)

Worked Example: Sonar Calculation

Question: A submarine uses sonar to detect a shoal of fish. The ultrasound pulse returns after 0.12 s. If the speed of sound in seawater is 1500 m/s, calculate the distance to the shoal.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify values - \(v = 1500 \text{ m/s}\)

  • \(t = 0.12 \text{ s}\)

Step 2: Apply formula \[d = \frac{v \times t}{2} = \frac{1500 \times 0.12}{2} = \boxed{90 \text{ m}}\]

20.6 Key Concepts Summary

20.6.1 Core Concepts

  1. Sound production: Sound is produced by vibrating sources in a medium

  2. Longitudinal waves: Sound waves consist of compressions and rarefactions; particle vibration is parallel to wave propagation

  3. Audible range: Humans can hear frequencies from approximately 20 Hz to 20 kHz

  4. Medium required: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum; it requires a medium (solid, liquid, or gas)

  5. Speed of sound: Approximately 330–350 m/s in air; faster in liquids and solids

  6. Speed measurement: Use distance–time method with flash and sound from a starting pistol

  7. Loudness and pitch: Loudness depends on amplitude; pitch depends on frequency

  8. Echoes: Reflection of sound waves; used to calculate distances

  9. Ultrasound: Sound with frequency > 20 kHz; used in testing, medical scanning, and sonar

20.6.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Compression and rarefaction: Regions of high and low pressure in a longitudinal sound wave

  2. Speed comparison: Sound travels fastest in solids, slower in liquids, slowest in gases

  3. Ultrasound applications: Non-destructive testing, medical imaging, sonar depth/distance calculations

20.7 Common Misconceptions

20.7.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Sound can travel through a vacuum”

Incorrect: “Sound from explosions in space movies is realistic”

Correct: - Sound requires a medium (particles) to transfer vibrations

  • In a vacuum, there are no particles → sound cannot propagate

  • Space is a near-vacuum → no sound can travel through it

20.7.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Particles travel with the sound wave”

Incorrect: “Air molecules move from the speaker to your ear when you hear music”

Correct: - Particles oscillate back and forth about fixed positions

  • They transfer energy to neighbouring particles through collisions

  • Only the wave pattern and energy travel forward, not the particles themselves

20.7.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Loudness depends on frequency”

Incorrect: “High-pitched sounds are always louder than low-pitched sounds”

Correct: - Loudness depends on amplitude (size of pressure variations)

  • Pitch depends on frequency (number of vibrations per second)

  • A low-pitched sound can be very loud if it has large amplitude

20.7.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Ultrasound is harmful because it is ‘ultra’”

Incorrect: “Ultrasound is dangerous because it is very high frequency”

Correct: - Ultrasound is non-ionising radiation and is generally safe at diagnostic intensities

  • It is preferred over X-rays for scanning foetuses because it does not damage cells

  • Harm depends on intensity and exposure time, not just frequency

20.7.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Echoes are a different type of sound”

Incorrect: “An echo is a special kind of sound wave”

Correct: - An echo is simply a reflected sound wave

  • It has the same frequency, speed, and wavelength as the original sound

  • The only difference is the direction of propagation and the time delay

20.7.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “Sound travels at the same speed in all materials”

Incorrect: “Sound travels at 340 m/s everywhere”

Correct: - Speed of sound depends on the medium and its properties (density, elasticity, temperature)

  • In air: ~330–350 m/s; in water: ~1500 m/s; in steel: ~5000 m/s

  • Temperature also affects speed: sound travels faster in warmer air

20.7.7 ⚠️ Misconception 7: “Ultrasound cannot be used for distance measurement”

Incorrect: “Ultrasound is only for medical imaging”

Correct: - Ultrasound is ideal for distance measurement because: - High frequency → short wavelength → precise measurements

  • Can be directed in narrow beams

  • Reflects well from boundaries between different materials

  • Used in sonar, parking sensors, and industrial thickness gauging

20.8 Worked Examples

20.8.1 Example 1: Frequency and Period

Question: A tuning fork vibrates at a frequency of 440 Hz. Calculate: (a) the period of one vibration (b) the number of complete vibrations in 2.5 seconds

Solution:

(a) Period: \[T = \frac{1}{f} = \frac{1}{440} = \boxed{0.00227 \text{ s} = 2.27 \text{ ms}}\]

(b) Number of vibrations: \[\text{Number} = f \times t = 440 \times 2.5 = \boxed{1100 \text{ vibrations}}\]

20.8.2 Example 2: Speed of Sound Calculation

Question: A student stands 170 m from a large wall and claps her hands. She hears the echo 1.0 s later. Calculate the speed of sound in air.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify that sound travels to the wall and back \[\text{Total distance} = 2 \times 170 = 340 \text{ m}\]

Step 2: Apply speed formula \[v = \frac{\text{distance}}{\text{time}} = \frac{340}{1.0} = \boxed{340 \text{ m/s}}\]

20.8.3 Example 3: Ultrasound in Medical Scanning

Question: An ultrasound scanner sends a pulse into a patient’s abdomen. The pulse reflects off the foetus and returns to the scanner after 0.00012 s. If the speed of ultrasound in soft tissue is 1540 m/s, calculate the depth of the foetus below the scanner.

Solution:

Step 1: Identify values - \(v = 1540 \text{ m/s}\)

  • \(t = 0.00012 \text{ s}\)

Step 2: Apply echo formula \[d = \frac{v \times t}{2} = \frac{1540 \times 0.00012}{2} = \boxed{0.0924 \text{ m} = 9.24 \text{ cm}}\]

20.8.4 Example 4: Comparing Loudness and Pitch

Question: Two sound waves are displayed on a c.r.o. Wave A has an amplitude of 2.0 cm and a wavelength of 4.0 cm. Wave B has an amplitude of 1.0 cm and a wavelength of 2.0 cm. The time base is the same for both. Compare the loudness and pitch of the two sounds.

Solution:

Loudness comparison: - Amplitude of A (2.0 cm) > Amplitude of B (1.0 cm)

  • Therefore, Sound A is louder than Sound B

Pitch comparison: - Wavelength of A (4.0 cm) > Wavelength of B (2.0 cm)

  • Since \(v = f\lambda\) and \(v\) is constant, longer wavelength → lower frequency

  • Therefore, Sound A has a lower pitch than Sound B

Conclusion: Sound A is louder but lower-pitched than Sound B.

20.10 Laboratory Investigation

20.10.1 Experiment 17.1: Measuring the Speed of Sound Using Echoes

Objective: To determine the speed of sound in air by measuring the time for an echo to return from a reflecting surface.

Apparatus: - Two flat wooden boards (to create a sharp clap)

  • Stopwatch

  • Measuring tape (at least 50 m)

  • Large flat wall or building (reflecting surface)

Variables: - Independent: Distance to reflecting surface

  • Dependent: Time for echo to return

  • Controlled: Same clapping method, same observer timing, same environmental conditions

Procedure: 1. Measure and mark a distance \(d\) (e.g., 50 m) from a large flat wall.

  1. Stand at the marked position facing the wall.

  2. Clap the two boards together sharply and listen for the echo.

  3. Practise clapping rhythmically so that each clap coincides with the echo of the previous clap.

  4. Once a steady rhythm is achieved, have a partner time 20 claps using a stopwatch.

  5. Record the total time \(t_{\text{total}}\) for 20 claps.

  6. Calculate the time for one round trip: \(t = \frac{t_{\text{total}}}{20}\)

  7. Calculate the speed of sound: \(v = \frac{2d}{t}\)

  8. Repeat for different distances and calculate an average value.

Data Table:

Trial Distance \(d\) (m) Time for 20 claps \(t_{\text{total}}\) (s) Time for one round trip \(t\) (s) Calculated speed \(v\) (m/s)
1 50
2 60
3 70
Average — — — \(v_{\text{avg}}\) =

Analysis: 1. Calculate the speed of sound for each trial using \(v = \frac{2d}{t}\)

  1. Calculate the average speed from all trials

  2. Compare your result with the accepted value (~340 m/s) and discuss possible sources of error

Precautions: - ✓ Choose a quiet location to hear echoes clearly

  • ✓ Ensure the reflecting surface is large and flat for a clear echo

  • ✓ Practise the clapping rhythm before timing to reduce reaction time errors

  • ✓ Use a distance of at least 50 m to make timing errors relatively small

Expected Results: - The calculated speed should be close to 330–350 m/s, depending on air temperature

  • Repeating at different distances helps identify and reduce systematic errors

Conclusion Template: “The speed of sound in air was determined to be ______ m/s. This value is [close to/different from] the accepted value of 340 m/s, which may be due to [reaction time errors/temperature variations/wind effects/etc.].”

Extension (Supplement): - Investigate how temperature affects the speed of sound by conducting the experiment at different times of day

  • Compare the speed of sound in different media (e.g., through a metal rod vs. through air)

20.12 Chapter Summary

20.12.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Sound production: Vibrating sources in a medium produce sound waves
✓ Longitudinal waves: Sound consists of compressions and rarefactions; particle vibration ∥ wave propagation
✓ Audible range: ~20 Hz to ~20 kHz for healthy human hearing
✓ Medium required: Sound cannot travel through a vacuum; needs solid, liquid, or gas
✓ Speed of sound: ~330–350 m/s in air; faster in liquids and solids
✓ Speed measurement: Use distance–time method with flash and sound
✓ Loudness: Depends on amplitude; larger amplitude → louder sound
✓ Pitch: Depends on frequency; higher frequency → higher pitch
✓ Echoes: Reflection of sound; used to calculate distances: \(d = \frac{v \times t}{2}\)
✓ Ultrasound: Frequency > 20 kHz; used in testing, medical scanning, sonar

20.12.2 Formulae Sheet

Quantity Formula Variables

Frequency–period \(f = \frac{1}{T}\) or \(T = \frac{1}{f}\) \(T\)=period (s), \(f\)=frequency (Hz)
