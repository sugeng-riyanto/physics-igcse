17.1 Learning Objectives

By the end of this chapter, you will be able to:

Core Content:

  • Know that waves transfer energy without transferring matter

  • Describe what is meant by wave motion as illustrated by vibrations in ropes and springs, and by experiments using water waves

  • Describe the features of a wave in terms of wavefront, wavelength, frequency, crest (peak), trough, amplitude and wave speed

  • Recall and use the equation for wave speed: \(v = f\lambda\)

  • Know that for a transverse wave, the direction of vibration is at right angles to the direction of propagation and understand that electromagnetic radiation, water waves and seismic S-waves (secondary) can be modelled as transverse

  • Know that for a longitudinal wave, the direction of vibration is parallel to the direction of propagation and understand that sound waves and seismic P-waves (primary) can be modelled as longitudinal

  • Describe how waves can undergo: (a) reflection at a plane surface (b) refraction due to a change of speed (c) diffraction through a narrow gap

  • Describe the use of a ripple tank to show reflection, refraction and diffraction of waves

Supplement (Extended):

  • Describe how wavelength and gap size affects diffraction through a gap

  • Describe how wavelength affects diffraction at an edge

17.2 14.1 What Is Wave Motion?

17.2.1 Energy Transfer Without Matter Transfer

In physics, a wave is a disturbance that travels through a medium or space, transferring energy from one point to another without transferring matter.

Key Concept: When a wave passes through a medium, the particles of the medium oscillate (vibrate) about fixed positions, but they do not travel with the wave. Only the energy and the waveform progress forward.

17.2.2 The Source of Waves

All waves originate from an oscillation or vibration.

  • A vibrating guitar string produces sound waves

  • A vibrating dipper in water produces water waves

  • Oscillating electric and magnetic fields produce electromagnetic waves

17.3 14.2 Types of Waves

Waves are classified into two main types based on the direction of particle vibration relative to the direction of wave propagation.

17.3.1 Transverse Waves

In a transverse wave, the direction of vibration is perpendicular (at right angles) to the direction of wave propagation.

Examples:

  • Water waves (surface ripples)

  • Electromagnetic waves (light, radio, X-rays)

  • Seismic S-waves (secondary waves)

  • Waves on a rope or string

Visual Representation: - Particles move up and down - Wave moves left to right - Features include crests (highest points) and troughs (lowest points)

17.3.2 Longitudinal Waves

In a longitudinal wave, the direction of vibration is parallel to the direction of wave propagation.

Examples:

  • Sound waves in air

  • Seismic P-waves (primary waves)

  • Waves in a compressed spring (Slinky pushed and pulled)

Visual Representation: - Particles move back and forth along the same line as the wave - Creates regions of compression (particles close together) and rarefaction (particles spread apart)

Warning

Important: Do not confuse the motion of the medium with the motion of the wave. In longitudinal waves, particles oscillate parallel to energy transfer; in transverse waves, they oscillate perpendicular to it.

17.4 14.3 Properties of Waves

To describe waves quantitatively, we use specific terminology and measurements.

17.4.1 Key Wave Features

Feature Definition SI Unit
Crest Highest point of a transverse wave —
Trough Lowest point of a transverse wave —
Wavelength (\(\lambda\)) Shortest distance between two consecutive points in phase (e.g., crest to crest, or trough to trough) metre (m)
Amplitude (\(A\)) Maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position metre (m)
Period (\(T\)) Time taken for one complete wave to pass a point second (s)
Frequency (\(f\)) Number of complete waves produced per second hertz (Hz)
Wave speed (\(v\)) Distance travelled by the wave per second metre per second (m/s)
Wavefront Imaginary line joining adjacent points that are in phase —

17.4.2 Relationship Between Frequency and Period

\[f = \frac{1}{T} \quad \text{and} \quad T = \frac{1}{f}\]

Example: If a wave has a period of 0.02 s, its frequency is: \[f = \frac{1}{0.02} = 50 \text{ Hz}\]

17.4.3 Displacement–Distance vs Displacement–Time Graphs

Graph Type What It Shows What Can Be Read
Displacement–distance Shape of the wave at one instant in time Wavelength (\(\lambda\)), amplitude (\(A\))
Displacement–time Motion of a single particle over time Period (\(T\)), amplitude (\(A\)), frequency (\(f\))

17.5 14.4 The Wave Equation

The speed of a wave is determined by how frequently waves are produced and how long each wave is.

\[v = f\lambda\]

Where: - \(v\) = wave speed (m/s) - \(f\) = frequency (Hz) - \(\lambda\) = wavelength (m)

Derivation Insight: - In one period (\(T\)), a wave travels one wavelength (\(\lambda\)) - Speed = distance/time = \(\lambda / T\) - Since \(f = 1/T\), then \(v = f\lambda\)

Note

Key Concept: For a wave travelling through a given medium, the speed is usually constant. If frequency increases, wavelength must decrease proportionally, and vice versa.

17.6 14.5 Wave Behaviour: Reflection, Refraction, Diffraction

Waves exhibit three fundamental behaviours when they encounter boundaries, changes in medium, or obstacles.

17.6.1 1. Reflection

When waves strike a plane barrier, they bounce back.

Law of Reflection: - Angle of incidence (\(i\)) = Angle of reflection (\(r\)) - Wavelength, frequency, and speed remain unchanged - Direction changes according to the surface geometry

Example: Water waves reflecting off a straight barrier in a ripple tank.

17.6.2 2. Refraction

Refraction occurs when waves pass from one medium to another, causing a change in speed and often a change in direction.

For Water Waves: - Deep water → faster speed, longer wavelength - Shallow water → slower speed, shorter wavelength - If waves enter at an angle, they bend towards the normal when slowing down (deep → shallow) - Frequency remains constant during refraction

17.6.3 3. Diffraction

Diffraction is the spreading out of waves when they pass through a gap or around an edge.

Key Observations: - Diffraction is most noticeable when the gap size is similar to the wavelength - If gap size \(\gg\) wavelength: waves pass through with minimal spreading - If gap size \(\approx\) wavelength: waves spread out significantly in a semi-circular pattern - Wavelength and frequency remain unchanged after diffraction

Warning

Important: Diffraction does not change the speed, frequency, or wavelength of the wave. It only changes the direction of propagation at the edges.

17.7 Key Concepts Summary

17.7.1 Core Concepts

  1. Waves transfer energy, not matter; particles oscillate about fixed positions

  2. Transverse waves: vibration ⊥ propagation (e.g., light, water waves, S-waves)

  3. Longitudinal waves: vibration ∥ propagation (e.g., sound, P-waves)

  4. Wave features: wavelength (\(\lambda\)), frequency (\(f\)), period (\(T\)), amplitude (\(A\)), wavefront

  5. Wave equation: \(v = f\lambda\)

  6. Reflection: \(i = r\); speed and wavelength unchanged

  7. Refraction: change in speed causes bending; frequency constant

  8. Diffraction: spreading through gaps/around edges; most pronounced when gap \(\approx \lambda\)

17.7.2 Extended Concepts

  1. Gap size vs wavelength: smaller gaps relative to \(\lambda\) produce greater diffraction

  2. Edge diffraction: longer wavelengths diffract more around barriers than shorter wavelengths

17.8 Common Misconceptions

17.8.1 ⚠️ Misconception 1: “Waves carry matter along with them”

Incorrect: “Water waves push water forward across the ocean”

Correct:

  • Waves transfer energy, not matter

  • Water particles move in circular or elliptical orbits but return to their original positions

  • Only the wave pattern and energy progress forward

17.8.2 ⚠️ Misconception 2: “Longitudinal waves have crests and troughs”

Incorrect: “Sound waves have crests and troughs like water waves”

Correct:

  • Longitudinal waves consist of compressions and rarefactions, not crests and troughs

  • Crests/troughs are exclusive to transverse waves

  • Both types still have wavelength, frequency, and amplitude

17.8.3 ⚠️ Misconception 3: “Changing frequency changes wave speed”

Incorrect: “If I increase the frequency of a wave, it travels faster”

Correct:

  • Wave speed is determined by the medium, not the frequency

  • In a given medium, \(v\) is constant

  • Increasing \(f\) causes \(\lambda\) to decrease proportionally (\(v = f\lambda\))

17.8.4 ⚠️ Misconception 4: “Refraction changes the frequency of a wave”

Incorrect: “When light enters glass, its frequency decreases”

Correct:

  • Frequency remains constant during refraction

  • Speed and wavelength change, but the source vibration rate (\(f\)) does not

  • Energy is conserved per photon/wave cycle

17.8.5 ⚠️ Misconception 5: “Diffraction only happens with light”

Incorrect: “Diffraction is exclusive to electromagnetic waves”

Correct:

  • Diffraction occurs with all types of waves (water, sound, seismic, electromagnetic)

  • The degree of spreading depends on the ratio of wavelength to obstacle/gap size

  • Sound waves diffract around doorways because their wavelength is comparable to the door width

17.8.6 ⚠️ Misconception 6: “A narrower gap always increases wave speed”

Incorrect: “Waves speed up when squeezed through a small opening”

Correct:

  • Gap size does not affect wave speed

  • Speed depends only on medium properties (depth, density, tension, etc.)

  • Narrower gaps increase spreading (diffraction), not speed

17.9 Worked Examples

17.9.1 Example 1: Wave Speed Calculation

Question: A wave has a frequency of 12 Hz and a wavelength of 0.25 m. Calculate its speed.

Solution: \[v = f\lambda = 12 \times 0.25 = \boxed{3.0 \text{ m/s}}\]

17.9.2 Example 2: Frequency from Period

Question: A floating buoy bobs up and down once every 2.5 seconds. Calculate the frequency of the water waves.

Solution: \[f = \frac{1}{T} = \frac{1}{2.5} = \boxed{0.40 \text{ Hz}}\]

17.9.3 Example 3: Wavelength in Different Depths

Question: Water waves travel at 1.8 m/s in deep water with a frequency of 0.60 Hz. They enter shallow water where their speed reduces to 1.2 m/s. Calculate the wavelength in both regions.

Solution: Frequency remains constant: \(f = 0.60 \text{ Hz}\)

Deep water: \[\lambda_{\text{deep}} = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{1.8}{0.60} = \boxed{3.0 \text{ m}}\]

Shallow water: \[\lambda_{\text{shallow}} = \frac{1.2}{0.60} = \boxed{2.0 \text{ m}}\]

Note: Wavelength decreases in shallow water because speed decreases while frequency remains constant.

17.9.4 Example 4: Diffraction Condition

Question: Explain why radio waves can be received behind hills, but visible light cannot.

Solution: - Radio waves have long wavelengths (metres to kilometres) - Visible light has very short wavelengths (400–700 nm) - Hills are comparable in size to radio wavelengths, causing significant diffraction around the obstacle - Hills are vastly larger than light wavelengths, so light travels in straight lines with negligible diffraction - Therefore, radio signals bend around hills, but light does not

17.10 Practice Questions

17.10.1 Core Level

  1. State two differences between transverse and longitudinal waves. Give one example of each.

  2. Define wavelength, amplitude, frequency, and period. State their SI units.

  3. A wave has a period of 0.04 s. Calculate its frequency.

  4. Sound travels through air at 340 m/s. If the frequency of a musical note is 512 Hz, calculate its wavelength.

  5. Describe what happens to water waves when they pass from deep to shallow water at an angle. Include reference to speed, wavelength, frequency, and direction.

  6. Explain why a student speaking outside a classroom can be heard inside even if the door is only slightly open.

  7. A wave travels 12 m in 4.0 s. If its frequency is 2.0 Hz, calculate its wavelength.

  8. State the law of reflection for waves. What happens to the wavelength and speed of water waves when they reflect off a straight barrier?

17.10.2 Extended Level

  1. A dipper in a ripple tank vibrates at 5.0 Hz, producing waves with a wavelength of 3.0 cm. The waves enter a region of shallower water where their speed is 12 cm/s. Calculate:
    1. the frequency of the waves in the shallow region
    1. the wavelength in the shallow region
    1. the speed of the waves in the deep region

  1. Explain, using wave principles, why bass sounds from a music system can be heard through a doorway, but treble sounds are more directional.

  2. A transverse wave travels along a rope at 4.0 m/s. The distance between a crest and the adjacent trough is 0.50 m. Calculate the frequency of the wave.

  3. Describe how a ripple tank can be used to demonstrate refraction. Include a labelled diagram of the apparatus and expected wave pattern.

  4. A wave has an amplitude of 3.0 cm and a frequency of 10 Hz. Sketch a displacement–distance graph for two complete wavelengths. Label the amplitude and wavelength clearly.

  5. Explain why the frequency of a wave does not change when it undergoes refraction, but its speed and wavelength do.

17.11 Laboratory Investigation

17.11.1 Experiment 14.1: Investigating Diffraction in a Ripple Tank

Objective: To investigate how gap size and wavelength affect the diffraction of water waves.

Apparatus: - Ripple tank with transparent base - Light source and projection screen - Straight wave generator (dipper or motor-driven bar) - Adjustable barriers (two flat plates) - Ruler - Stroboscope or video camera (optional) - Power supply for wave generator

Variables: - Independent: Gap width; wavelength (adjusted via frequency) - Dependent: Degree of wave spreading (qualitative observation and measurement of diffraction angle) - Controlled: Water depth, wave generator amplitude, projection distance

Procedure: 1. Set up the ripple tank with uniform water depth (~5 mm).

  1. Adjust the wave generator to produce plane waves at a low frequency (~5 Hz).

  2. Measure and record the wavelength \(\lambda\) from the projected pattern.

  3. Place two barriers to form a gap. Set the gap width to approximately \(2\lambda\).

  4. Observe and sketch the wave pattern after passing through the gap.

  5. Reduce the gap width to approximately \(\lambda\), then to \(0.5\lambda\). Observe and sketch changes.

  6. Increase the frequency to double the wavelength (keeping depth constant). Repeat steps 4–6.

  7. Compare the degree of spreading for different gap-to-wavelength ratios.

Analysis: 1. Describe the relationship between \(d/\lambda\) and diffraction.

  1. State the condition for maximum diffraction.

  2. Explain why wavelength and frequency remain unchanged after diffraction.

Precautions: - ✓ Maintain constant water depth throughout the experiment

  • ✓ Ensure wave generator produces straight, parallel wavefronts

  • ✓ Avoid vibrations or air currents disturbing the tank

  • ✓ Allow water to settle between trials

Expected Results: - Maximum spreading occurs when gap width \(\approx\) wavelength

  • Narrower gaps relative to \(\lambda\) produce semi-circular wavefronts

  • Wider gaps produce minimal edge spreading

Conclusion Template: “The experiment demonstrated that diffraction is most pronounced when the gap size is comparable to the wavelength. When \(d \gg \lambda\), waves pass through with minimal spreading. When \(d \approx \lambda\), waves spread out in a circular pattern. This confirms that the extent of diffraction depends on the ratio of wavelength to obstacle/gap size.”

  • Compare diffraction of longitudinal vs transverse wave simulations

  • Relate findings to real-world examples: radio reception, architectural acoustics, optical instruments

17.13 Chapter Summary

17.13.1 Key Points to Remember

✓ Waves transfer energy, not matter; particles oscillate about fixed positions

✓ Transverse waves: vibration ⊥ propagation (light, water, S-waves)

✓ Longitudinal waves: vibration ∥ propagation (sound, P-waves)

✓ Wave features: \(\lambda\), \(f\), \(T\), \(A\), wavefront; \(f = 1/T\)

✓ Wave equation: \(v = f\lambda\)

✓ Reflection: \(i = r\); speed and \(\lambda\) unchanged

✓ Refraction: change in speed → bending; \(f\) constant

✓ Diffraction: spreading through gaps/around edges; max when gap \(\approx \lambda\)

✓ Ripple tank: essential tool for demonstrating all three wave behaviours

17.13.2 Formulae Sheet

Quantity Formula Variables
Wave speed \(v = f\lambda\) \(v\)=speed (m/s), \(f\)=frequency (Hz), \(\lambda\)=wavelength (m)
Frequency–period \(f = \frac{1}{T}\) or \(T = \frac{1}{f}\) \(T\)=period (s), \(f\)=frequency (Hz)
Law of reflection \(i = r\) \(i\)=angle of incidence, \(r\)=angle of reflection
